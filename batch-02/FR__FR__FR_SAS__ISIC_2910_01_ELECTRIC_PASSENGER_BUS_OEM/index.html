Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

Registre du Commerce et des Sociétés (RCS), kept by the Greffe du Tribunal de Commerce of the registered-office district; filing is processed through the single business formalities portal (Guichet Unique) operated on behalf of the RCS by the French National Institute of Industrial Property (INPI).

Governing statute

French Commercial Code (Code de commerce), Book II, Title II, Chapter VI — Articles L.227-1 to L.227-27 (Société par Actions Simplifiée) and Articles L.210-1 to L.210-10 (general incorporation rules); RCS registration governed by Articles L.123-1 and R.123-1 et seq.

Formation / constitutional filings

Electronic company-creation declaration (Déclaration de création d'entreprise, reference M0) completed on formalites.entreprises.gouv.fr and lodged with the notarised or signed original Articles of Association and all statutory annexes.

Incorporation processing time

⚡ 19 business days (SOP sum)

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

A French SAS has no statutory minimum share capital; the capital is freely fixed by the Articles. However, the capital must be fully subscribed, and at least 50% of cash contributions must be paid up before registration and held in a blocked capital account (compte de dépôt bloqué) at a licensed French bank or before a notary; the balance must be paid within five years. In-kind contributions must be fully delivered, and the President must certify the deposit for release no earlier than the issue of the RCS registration certificate.

Funding & unlock path: Fire-safety clearance: commission-de-sécurité visit by SDIS (Service Départemental d'Incendie et de Secours) and issue of the fire conformity certificate for the industrial building, escape routes and extinguishing systems. Structural clearance: static and dynamic structural-load inspection of roofing, gantry cranes, bus lifts and assembly mezzanines by an accredited technical-control organisation (APAVE, Bureau Veritas or Dekra) under the French building and Labour Code. Occupational clearance: CARSAT and Labour Inspectorate compliance for noise, vibration, electrical and machine-safety risk prevention, including the mandatory occupational-health enrolment, PPE plans and emergency-response drills before production start.

Paid-in capital rule: A French SAS has no statutory minimum share capital; the capital is freely fixed by the Articles. However, the capital must be fully subscribed, and at least 50% of cash contributions must be paid up before registration and held in a blocked capital account (compte de dépôt bloqué) at a licensed French bank or before a notary; the balance must be paid within five years. In-kind contributions must be fully delivered, and the President must certify the deposit for release no earlier than the issue of the RCS registration certificate.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Full current-account and capital-account convertibility as an EU member under Articles 63-65 TFEU; euro flows are entirely unrestricted for share capital, dividends, royalties, debt service, and liquidation proceeds. The only gateways on profit remittance are corporate distributability rules and tax withholding at source (25% French statutory WHT; 0% intra-EEA under the Parent-Subsidiary Directive; 5-10% under the France-China DTA). The sole non-FX screening step is the FDI prior-authorisation regime (CMF Articles L151-1 et seq. and Decree No. 2019-1590) for non-EEA investors in listed sensitive sectors; electric-bus assembly is presumptively non-sensitive unless dual-use or critical-technology components are involved.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory surrender, conversion ratio, or repatriation timeline applies to French export proceeds. Export revenues may be freely retained in EUR or any foreign currency, converted at the company's discretion via standard bank FX products, and remitted to any counterparty. The only obligation is statistical cross-border payment/collection reporting transmitted by the bank to Banque de France for balance-of-payments compilation, which does not restrict liquidity or require prior approval.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (BdF), acting within the Eurosystem of the European Central Bank; capital-movement and FDI policy implemented by the Direction Générale du Trésor (DG Trésor, French Treasury); prudential and AML supervision by the Autorité de Contrôle Prudentiel et de Résolution (ACPR).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Low for core treasury, Medium as an ancillary low-value disbursement rail. An offshore non-EEA digital EMI cannot lawfully provide the documentary-credit, guarantee, or public-sector settlement services required by an electric-bus OEM without full credit-institution licensing and a French/EEA passport; reverse-solicitation use is tolerable only for small operating expenses and must be swept daily to the Tier-1 bank. An EEA-licensed EMI (e.g., Wise Europe or Revolut) may automate expense payments but must not become a repository for municipal LC proceeds or import-settlement balances.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Dividends paid by a French SAS to a non-resident shareholder are generally subject to a 25% French withholding tax under domestic law, subject to exemptions and treaty relief. Dividends paid to a qualifying EU or EEA parent may be exempt under the French parent-subsidiary regime, generally requiring a qualifying ownership percentage, minimum holding period, subject-to-tax status, and anti-abuse compliance. Dividends paid to French-resident corporate shareholders are generally not subject to withholding tax, although the participation-exemption regime may apply at shareholder level.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a financial year ending on 31 December, the annual return and balance are generally due by the second business day following 1 May of the following year. For a financial year ending on another date, the return is generally due within three months after the year-end. The final balance is generally due by the filing deadline, subject to statutory payment rules. Corporate tax instalments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September, and 15 December, subject to exceptions for the first year, low liability, and short financial years.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory full transfer-pricing documentation generally applies where the French entity or French tax group has annual pre-tax turnover or gross assets of at least EUR 400 million, or belongs to a group meeting the statutory EUR 400 million threshold, subject to the detailed French rules. A French entity below that threshold must still be able to demonstrate arm's-length pricing and may be required to provide simplified documentation or information during an audit. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million. The local file and master file thresholds and content must be checked against the law applicable for the relevant financial year.

Economic substance scope: France has no single standalone economic-substance regulation equivalent to a dedicated ESR regime in certain other jurisdictions. Substance is enforced through the French abuse-of-law doctrine, general anti-abuse rules, beneficial-ownership requirements, controlled foreign company rules, anti-hybrid rules, interest limitation, principal-purpose-test treaty provisions, parent-subsidiary anti-abuse rules, and permanent-establishment principles. A French SAS should maintain real decision-making, qualified personnel, premises, operational assets, manufacturing capability, board governance, banking control, risk assumption, and contemporaneous commercial evidence. A conduit entity or intragroup financing or IP structure without genuine functions and risks may lose treaty or statutory benefits and may generate reassessment, withholding tax, penalties, and director exposure.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate tax evasion and fraudulent accounting

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, late-payment interest, and penalties up to 80% of the evaded tax. Criminal tax fraud may result in imprisonment of up to five years and a fine of up to EUR 500,000, with aggravated cases potentially reaching seven years and EUR 3 million, subject to statutory conditions and available aggravations.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto managers may face criminal prosecution, personal tax-fraud liability, management bans, civil restitution, asset seizures, and professional disqualification. Travel restrictions may be imposed by judicial authorities where legally justified; imprisonment is possible following conviction.
Late filing, non-payment, and obstruction of tax audit

Statutory sanction: Generally a 10% surcharge for late filing or payment, higher surcharges after formal notice or in cases of bad faith or fraud, and late-payment interest generally at 0.20% per month. Obstruction may trigger additional assessments, procedural penalties, and criminal consequences in serious cases.

Director personal liability: Managers may incur liability for serious and repeated non-payment or fraudulent conduct, including civil contribution to tax liabilities, management bans, asset-preservation measures, and criminal sanctions where obstruction or fraud is established.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment under the arm's-length principle, interest, penalties up to 40% for deliberate conduct or 80% for fraudulent conduct, and documentation penalties generally based on 0.5% of affected transactions or 5% of adjustments, subject to a EUR 10,000 minimum.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil liability for losses caused by deliberate mispricing or breach of tax duties, and criminal exposure where false records, fraud, corruption, or money laundering is involved.
Shell company, treaty abuse, and beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty or EU exemption, withholding at the domestic rate, reassessment, interest, abuse-of-law penalties potentially up to 80%, and loss of deductions or tax credits.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil restitution and criminal liability where they knowingly implemented fraudulent structures. Courts may impose asset freezes, management prohibitions, and travel restrictions under applicable judicial procedures; imprisonment is possible for related fraud or money laundering.
Unlawful profit distribution and insolvency misconduct

Statutory sanction: Repayment of unlawful distributions, shareholder and director liability, tax reassessment, insolvency clawback, and potential criminal sanctions for misuse of corporate assets or fraudulent bankruptcy.

Director personal liability: Directors may be ordered to contribute to insolvency shortfalls, disqualified from management, held liable for misuse of company assets, and subjected to criminal penalties, including imprisonment where statutory elements are satisfied.
Anti-money laundering, corruption, and sanctions breach

Statutory sanction: Criminal fines, confiscation, procurement exclusion, contract termination, tax reassessment, and imprisonment under the French Penal Code and applicable sanctions, anti-corruption, customs, and AML legislation. Corporate penalties may be substantially higher than individual fines and may include dissolution or prohibition from conducting activities in serious cases.

Director personal liability: Directors and senior officers may be personally prosecuted for bribery, money laundering, sanctions evasion, customs fraud, or failure to prevent prohibited conduct. Possible consequences include imprisonment, fines, confiscation, management bans, civil restitution, and judicial travel restrictions.
Customs, import VAT, and origin fraud

Statutory sanction: Recovery of customs duties and import VAT, customs penalties, seizure or forfeiture of goods, late-payment interest, and criminal customs sanctions for deliberate fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors and customs representatives may face joint or personal liability under applicable customs rules, criminal prosecution, asset seizure, and professional disqualification where intentional false declarations are proven.
Research tax credit and incentive abuse

Statutory sanction: Disallowance and recovery of the credit, interest, and penalties generally ranging from 10% to 80% depending on negligence, bad faith, fraud, or abuse. False documentation may generate separate criminal liability.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil liability to the company and criminal exposure for fraudulent claims, false records, or organized tax fraud; management bans and asset seizures may apply in serious cases.
Failure to maintain accounting and beneficial-owner records

Statutory sanction: Rejection of deductions or tax positions, estimated assessment, accounting penalties, inability to defend treaty or tax-credit claims, and penalties for inaccurate or omitted beneficial-owner information.

Director personal liability: The president and directors may be personally liable for governance failures, civil damages, management bans, and criminal sanctions where concealment, false declarations, or money laundering is involved.
