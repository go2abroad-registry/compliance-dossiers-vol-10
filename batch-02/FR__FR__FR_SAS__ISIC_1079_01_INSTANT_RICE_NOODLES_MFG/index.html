Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

The competent authority is the Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP), under the Ministry of the Economy, Finance and Industrial and Digital Sovereignty. A French SAS is generally registered through the business-formalities single window (Guichet unique des formalités des entreprises), which transmits formation information to the relevant authorities. Corporate tax and VAT filings and payments are handled through the DGFiP professional online account (Espace professionnel) on impots.gouv.fr. A non-established foreign taxable person is generally handled by the Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères (SIEE).

E-government filing portal

On incorporation, file the SAS formation formalities through the Guichet unique. Following registration in the Registre national des entreprises and, where applicable, the commercial and companies register, the company receives a SIREN identifier and establishment-specific SIRET identifier; these are not, by themselves, a VAT number. For VAT-taxable activity, request VAT identification from the competent business tax office, providing corporate registration documents, a description of activity, expected transactions, establishment and representative details, and evidence supporting the need for registration. A French VAT number generally takes the form FR followed by a two-digit key and the SIREN. Non-EU businesses without a French establishment generally apply through the SIEE and may be required to appoint a French fiscal representative; qualifying businesses established in the EU are generally not required to appoint one. Register for the DGFiP professional online account to file and pay applicable taxes. Obtain an EORI number separately through French customs when required for customs operations. Confirm the applicable registration route and document list with the DGFiP or customs authority before commencing transactions.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A French SAS is ordinarily subject to French corporation tax (impôt sur les sociétés, or IS) on its taxable profits; the standard rate is generally 25%, subject to applicable reduced-rate conditions and other statutory provisions. An eligible newly formed SAS may elect temporary taxation under the income-tax regime for a limited period, generally up to five financial years, only if statutory ownership, activity, size, age, and other conditions are satisfied and the required shareholder consent and filing formalities are met. Certain qualifying family-owned companies may have a separate election route, subject to statutory conditions. France does not provide a general US-style check-the-box election for an SAS. Classification and elections should be assessed against current French law and the company’s facts.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For a French-established SAS, the domestic small-business VAT exemption (franchise en base) may apply only if the applicable statutory turnover conditions are met and no disqualifying circumstances or voluntary election apply. The commonly applicable thresholds for domestic supplies are EUR 85,000 for supplies of goods and EUR 37,500 for services, subject to statutory reference-period rules, transitional provisions, and legislative changes. A manufacturer making taxable supplies should verify the applicable thresholds and eligibility for the relevant tax year. A business that is liable to VAT or opts to charge VAT must register as required. A non-established taxable person generally cannot assume that the domestic small-business exemption is available; registration may be required from the first taxable transaction, subject to applicable EU schemes and rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

For goods dispatched or transported from France to a destination outside the European Union, the supply may be exempt from French VAT with the right to deduct input VAT when statutory export conditions are met and the supplier can substantiate that the goods left the EU customs territory. The supplier should retain consistent commercial, transport, accounting, and customs evidence and report the transaction correctly. A sale to an EU customer is generally treated under intra-Community supply rules, not export rules; exemption depends on the applicable conditions, including the customer’s valid VAT identification and evidence of transport to another Member State. Services are not automatically zero-rated because the customer is offshore: place-of-supply rules determine whether French VAT applies, and any exemption or outside-scope treatment must be supported by the service type, customer status, and evidence. Input VAT recovery remains subject to ordinary deduction restrictions and documentation requirements.

  • Export customs declaration and evidence of exit from the EU customs territory, including the customs export result or equivalent exit confirmation
  • Commercial invoice, sales contract or purchase order, and accounting records identifying the goods and the foreign customer
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, CMR consignment note, or other transport document, as applicable
  • Carrier, freight-forwarder, delivery, or other corroborating evidence linking the goods to the export declaration and destination
  • Customer payment and bank records where relevant to substantiate the transaction; an inward remittance certificate is not a general French statutory prerequisite for export VAT exemption
  • For intra-Community supplies rather than exports, the customer’s VAT identification evidence and proof of dispatch or transport to another EU Member State

France is implementing domestic B2B electronic invoicing and transaction/payment data reporting in phases. From 1 September 2026, all businesses in scope must be able to receive electronic invoices; issuance obligations are scheduled to apply from that date to large and intermediate-sized businesses and from 1 September 2027 to small and medium-sized businesses, subject to current implementation rules. In-scope invoices must be transmitted through an approved platform (plateforme agréée) and conform to prescribed formats and data requirements. Relevant cross-border and business-to-consumer transactions, and certain payment data, are subject to electronic reporting rather than domestic B2B invoice transmission. France does not generally require real-time tax-authority clearance or digital stamping of each invoice. Confirm scope, platform requirements, exemptions, and effective dates against current DGFiP guidance.

There is no general French statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline of 180 days applicable to export VAT zero-rating. The supplier bears the burden of substantiating the export exemption and should retain records for the applicable statutory retention and audit periods. If export or exemption conditions are not established, the DGFiP may assess output VAT, late-payment interest, and applicable penalties. French tax penalties can include a 40% surcharge for deliberate failure and an 80% surcharge in specified cases of fraudulent activity or abuse, in addition to interest; the applicable sanction depends on the facts and legal characterization. Incorrect input VAT deductions or VAT refunds may also be reassessed, recovered, and penalized. A fraudulent declaration may entail additional civil or criminal consequences.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France has no general exchange controls restricting conversion or cross-border transfer of euros or other currencies. Current-account payments, including trade payments, are generally freely transferable, subject to sanctions, anti-money-laundering, counter-terrorist-financing, tax, and documentary requirements. Capital movements are also generally free under EU law, subject to narrowly applicable statutory restrictions, sanctions, and reporting obligations. Dividends, interest, and other profits may generally be remitted without exchange-control approval after applicable French corporate approvals, withholding-tax analysis, treaty or EU-relief documentation, and tax filings. Banks may require evidence of the underlying transaction, source of funds, beneficial ownership, and tax status. French-resident companies must assess any applicable Banque de France balance-of-payments statistical reporting and declare foreign accounts where required; these are reporting obligations, not general remittance permissions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France has no general statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio for export receipts. Export proceeds may generally be retained in euros or foreign currency and held domestically or abroad, subject to bank terms, sanctions screening, accounting and tax rules, and applicable statistical reporting. There is no general statutory deadline requiring conversion of export receipts. Cash or monetary instruments physically transported across the EU external border with an aggregate value of EUR 10,000 or more are subject to EU cash-control declaration requirements; separate French and EU reporting rules may apply to particular transactions or financial intermediaries.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France is France’s central bank and participates in the Eurosystem. The European Central Bank sets euro-area monetary policy. The French Treasury (Direction générale du Trésor) administers applicable financial sanctions and certain capital-control measures.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for routine payments, subject to the provider’s country, customer, currency, and transaction coverage. An EU-authorised electronic money institution may provide payment and e-money services under its authorisation and passporting rights, but e-money is not a bank deposit and generally is not covered by the deposit-guarantee scheme. An EMI account is not automatically suitable for statutory share-capital deposits, escrow, credit, documentary trade finance, or all SWIFT/LC needs. Verify the provider’s authorisation in the relevant regulator register, safeguarding arrangements, transfer limits, sanctions controls, and ability to receive the intended capital or trade payments. Use an authorised credit institution for capital-deposit certification or escrow where legally or contractually required.

Verify the provider’s authorisation in the relevant regulator register, safeguarding arrangements, transfer limits, sanctions controls, and ability to receive the intended capital or trade payments. Use an authorised credit institution for capital-deposit certification or escrow where legally or contractually required.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés). A 3.3% social contribution may apply to companies meeting the applicable turnover and tax thresholds, producing an effective rate of approximately 25.83%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The standard French withholding tax on dividends distributed by a French company to a non-resident is generally 25%, subject to applicable exemptions, tax treaties, and anti-abuse rules. A 0% domestic exemption may apply to qualifying distributions to an EU or EEA parent company holding at least 10% of the French payer for the required period, or providing an appropriate commitment, if the statutory conditions and anti-abuse requirements are met. A 75% rate may apply to payments to certain non-cooperative jurisdictions or territories.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

The annual return is generally due within three months after the end of the accounting period. Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent tax return

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, late-payment interest, a penalty of up to 80% of the avoided tax, and criminal tax-fraud sanctions generally including up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 500,000 fine. Aggravated cases may carry up to seven years' imprisonment and a EUR 3 million fine, subject to statutory conditions.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto managers may face criminal prosecution, personal tax liability in cases of fraudulent or repeated obstruction under French tax procedure law, civil restitution, management disqualification, and confiscation. Travel restrictions may be imposed only under applicable judicial or enforcement measures; they are not an automatic tax penalty.
Fraudulent insolvency and abuse of corporate form

Statutory sanction: Civil liability for all or part of the company's insufficient assets, clawback of transactions, bankruptcy-related sanctions, management bans, and potential criminal penalties for specified insolvency offenses.

Director personal liability: Directors may be ordered to contribute to the shortfall, prohibited from managing companies, and prosecuted personally. The limited-liability shield does not protect fraudulent conduct, personal guarantees, commingling, or deliberate mismanagement.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment under the arm's-length principle, denial of deductions, withholding tax exposure, interest limitation adjustments, penalties for inadequate documentation, and potential 40% or 80% tax penalties where intent or fraud is established.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur personal exposure where they knowingly approve false records, fraudulent declarations, asset diversion, or conduct causing tax loss. Ordinary transfer-pricing adjustments do not automatically create personal criminal liability.
Beneficial-ownership and treaty abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty or EU exemption, recovery of withholding tax, interest, penalties up to 80% in aggravated cases, and potential anti-abuse or abuse-of-law proceedings.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly certify false treaty eligibility may face tax-fraud, document-fraud, or aiding-and-abetting exposure, in addition to civil restitution and management sanctions.
Value added tax and customs fraud

Statutory sanction: Recovery of VAT, customs duties, excise and import charges, interest, penalties generally ranging from 10% to 80% depending on conduct, customs penalties, seizure, and criminal prosecution for serious fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal criminal liability, customs-related sanctions, civil recovery, management bans, and confiscation where they participated in or knowingly facilitated the scheme.
Anti-money laundering and terrorist-financing breach

Statutory sanction: Criminal money-laundering liability may include up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 375,000 fine, with aggravated or organized cases carrying materially higher penalties. Assets may be frozen or confiscated, and regulatory or professional sanctions may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted for participation, concealment, facilitation, or failure to prevent unlawful transactions where legally applicable. Confiscation, civil restitution, management disqualification, and judicial travel restrictions may follow.
False accounting and corporate records

Statutory sanction: Accounting and corporate-law sanctions, tax reassessment, criminal penalties for specified false-accounting conduct, auditor reporting obligations, and potential insolvency or tax-fraud proceedings.

Director personal liability: The president, directors, de facto managers, and participating officers may incur criminal, civil, and management-ban liability. A corporate form does not shield intentional falsification or personal participation.
Unlawful profit extraction and dividend distribution

Statutory sanction: Repayment of unlawful distributions, corporate tax and withholding tax recovery, interest, penalties, director liability, and possible criminal sanctions for misuse of corporate assets or fraudulent distribution.

Director personal liability: The president and approving officers may be personally liable for restitution, creditor losses, tax amounts, and misuse-of-corporate-assets offenses. Judicial management bans and confiscation may apply in serious cases.
