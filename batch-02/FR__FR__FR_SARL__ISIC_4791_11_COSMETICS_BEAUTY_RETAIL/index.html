UBO Ultimate Beneficial Owner Screening Rules Official Registry Only

>=25% ownership / control

Applicable AML statute: AML/CFT obligations are codified in the Code monétaire et financier (CMF), Articles L.561-1 to L.561-57 and R.561-1 et seq., transposing Directive (EU) 2015/849 and Directive (EU) 2018/843 (4th and 5th AML Directives), supplemented by Regulation (EU) 2023/1113 on funds transfers. The French UBO register is governed by CMF Articles L.561-45 to L.561-46-3 and R.561-53 to R.561-56, implemented by Ordonnance n° 2016-1635 of 1 December 2016 and Décret n° 2017-1094 of 29 June 2017, as amended by Décret n° 2020-118 of 12 February 2020. A UBO is any natural person directly or indirectly holding more than 25% of the capital or voting rights, or exercising control within the meaning of Code de commerce Article L.233-3 (CMF Article L.561-2-2). UBO declarations are filed with the Registre du Commerce et des Sociétés (RCS) through the Guichet Unique operated by INPI (Code de commerce Articles L.123-1 and R.123-1 et seq., R.123-9). Financial intelligence and national freezing regimes are administered by TRACFIN (CMF Article L.561-36) and the DG Trésor under CMF Articles L.562-1 to L.562-12, EU Regulation (CE) n° 881/2001, EU Regulation (CE) n° 2580/2001, as amended, and national VIGIES emergency economic-control lists.
Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

Permissible with conditions: under Code de commerce Article L.123-11-1, as amended by Loi n° 2019-486 (Pacte law) and Ordonnance n° 2020-114, an SARL may domicile its siège social with a licensed commercial domiciliataire (business-address provider) holding the required prefectoral agrément, for a maximum of five years. The provider must operate a physical, permanently staffed reception point capable of receiving mail and service of process. Pure online 'virtual' addresses without any staffed administrative physical presence do not satisfy RCS verification or DGFiP substance expectations. Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France is part of the Eurozone; the euro (EUR) is a freely convertible currency. Current account transactions (trade, services, profit remittance) are fully liberalized under EU Regulation 2018/1672 and the French Monetary and Financial Code (Code monétaire et financier). Capital account transactions are generally free but subject to reporting obligations for statistical purposes and anti-money laundering (AML) scrutiny. Profit remittance to non-resident shareholders (dividends) is unrestricted, provided corporate taxes are paid and intercompany transactions are at arm's length. No prior approval is required for repatriation of capital or profits, but banks must verify the economic justification of transfers exceeding certain thresholds (e.g., €50,000 for non-recurring transfers).

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender or conversion requirement for export proceeds. French resident entities may hold foreign currency accounts domestically or abroad. However, export receipts must be repatriated to France within one month of receipt if the beneficiary is a French resident, per Article L.152-2 of the Monetary and Financial Code. Proceeds may be retained in foreign currency. For e-commerce and digital services, proceeds from payment processors (e.g., Shopify Payments, PayPal) are typically settled in EUR or USD; if settled in USD, funds must be repatriated to a French bank account within the statutory timeline. Reporting to the Banque de France is required for statistical purposes via the 'Déclaration des opérations avec l'étranger' for annual aggregate flows exceeding €100 million.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (BdF) and Autorité de Contrôle Prudentiel et de Résolution (ACPR) for banking supervision; Direction Générale du Trésor for foreign exchange policy; European Central Bank (ECB) for monetary policy.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

High feasibility. French and EU-licensed Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) and Payment Institutions (PIs) such as Wise, Revolut Business, Qonto, Lydia, and Stripe can provide multi-currency accounts and cross-border payment services. These entities operate under the ACPR and ECB passporting regime. Legal constraints: EMIs cannot offer interest-bearing accounts or lending services. They are subject to stringent AML/CFT directives (EU AMLD5/6, French LCB-FT). For cross-border e-commerce, EMIs are viable for collecting and disbursing funds, but a traditional bank account is often required for tax registration and social security payments. Non-resident directors may open EMI accounts remotely, but enhanced due diligence applies.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital injections into a French SARL must be wired to the corporate bank account using SWIFT MT103 messages. The purpose code should be 'INVESTMENT' or 'EQUITY CAPITAL' (e.g., using ISO 20022 codes). For non-resident shareholders, the transfer must be documented with a shareholder resolution, share subscription agreement, and proof of source of funds. The bank will verify that the transfer aligns with the declared capital increase or initial capital. For amounts exceeding €50,000, the bank may request a notarized certificate of the capital deposit. Inbound transfers are reported to the Banque de France if they exceed €12,500 for statistical purposes. No prior authorization is required, but the bank must comply with AML/CFT screening.Outbound trade settlements for cosmetics retail (e.g., payments to suppliers in China, EU, or US) can be executed via SWIFT MT103 for clean payments or SWIFT MT700 for commercial letters of credit (LC). For e-commerce, disbursements to suppliers are often made via SEPA transfers within the EU (instant or standard) or SWIFT for non-EU. LC is advisable for high-value orders or new suppliers to mitigate counterparty risk. The bank will require commercial invoices, packing lists, and bills of lading for LC transactions. For recurring low-value payments, SWIFT MT101 or SEPA credit transfers are used. All transactions must include a purpose code (e.g., 'TRADE PAYMENT' or 'GOODS PURCHASE').Foreign Exchange Forwards (FX Forwards) - Customizable maturity and notional amount to hedge EUR/USD or EUR/CNY exposure from supplier payments or receivables.Non-Deliverable Forwards (NDFs) - Permissible for currencies with restrictions (e.g., CNY), settled in EUR or USD, commonly used for hedging exposure to Chinese suppliers.Currency Swaps - Interest rate and currency swaps to manage long-term exposure, often used for financing arrangements or large capital expenditures.FX Options - Purchased call/put options to hedge against adverse currency movements, with premium upfront; suitable for uncertain cash flows.Natural Hedging - Matching revenue and expense currencies (e.g., receiving USD from sales and paying USD to suppliers) to reduce net exposure, permissible and encouraged.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% is the general French withholding tax rate on dividends paid by a French company to a non-resident corporate shareholder, subject to domestic exemptions and applicable tax treaties. Dividends paid to a qualifying EU or EEA parent may be exempt under the French implementation of the Parent-Subsidiary Directive, generally requiring a qualifying minimum participation, minimum holding period or commitment, subject-to-tax status, and anti-abuse compliance. Treaty rates commonly range from 0% to 15%, but the applicable rate is country-specific.

For a financial year ending on 31 December, the return and final payment are generally due by the second business day following 1 May of the following year. Electronic filing and payment obligations apply to French companies.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Instalments are generally due by the 15th day of the relevant month. The final balance is generally due by the annual corporate income tax return deadline.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: A French entity is generally subject to the full transfer pricing documentation requirement under Article L13 AA of the French Tax Procedures Code where its annual pre-tax turnover or gross assets is at least EUR 150 million, or where it belongs to or has transactions with an enterprise meeting the statutory relationship and threshold conditions. The annual transfer pricing summary declaration Form 2257-SD generally applies at the EUR 150 million threshold or where the entity is connected to an enterprise meeting the relevant statutory criteria. French master-file obligations generally apply to entities within the Article L13 AA scope; additional country-by-country reporting applies to qualifying multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million. Transactions below the documentation threshold remain subject to the arm's-length principle and may require simplified supporting documentation.

Economic substance scope: France has no single generally applicable standalone economic substance regulation equivalent to certain offshore ESR regimes. Economic substance is nevertheless relevant through the general anti-abuse doctrine, beneficial-owner requirements, controlled foreign company rules, hybrid mismatch rules, interest limitation rules, principal-purpose and treaty anti-abuse provisions, and the substance requirements for EU parent-subsidiary and interest-royalty exemptions. The SARL should maintain French operational substance proportionate to its retail activity, including genuine management, commercial decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational control, premises or fulfillment arrangements, inventory and customer-service functions, books and records, and commercially justified related-party agreements.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Deliberate Tax Fraud

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, recovery of unpaid tax, late-payment interest, tax increases generally ranging from 40% for deliberate conduct to 80% for fraudulent maneuvers, and criminal prosecution. Tax fraud may carry imprisonment of up to five years and a fine of up to EUR 500,000, with higher penalties in aggravated cases, including up to seven years and EUR 3 million where statutory aggravating circumstances apply.

Director personal liability: Managers may incur criminal liability, civil liability for personal misconduct, tax-solidarity liability in cases of fraudulent or repeated non-compliance, management-liability proceedings, disqualification from management, asset seizure, and imprisonment. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may result from judicial control, enforcement, or criminal proceedings.
Value Added Tax Fraud and Incorrect Invoicing

Statutory sanction: VAT reassessment, late-payment interest, penalties generally ranging from 10% to 80%, repayment of improperly claimed credits, and possible criminal tax-fraud sanctions where the conduct is intentional or organized.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed for fraudulent or deliberately repeated VAT failures, including tax-solidarity orders, criminal fines, imprisonment in aggravated cases, asset restraints, and management disqualification.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Adjustment of French taxable profit, interest, and penalties. Failure to produce required transfer pricing documentation may attract a penalty generally calculated by reference to the amount of undocumented or adjusted transactions, subject to statutory minimum and maximum limits, including a minimum commonly set at EUR 10,000 per audited year under applicable rules.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure for intentional misrepresentation, fraudulent accounting, or organized profit diversion, but ordinary transfer pricing adjustment normally remains a liability of the SARL.
Shell Company and Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty or directive benefits, withholding tax reassessment, interest, anti-abuse penalties, possible abuse-of-law assessment, and reassessment of the SARL's taxable profit. Sham arrangements may also trigger criminal fraud proceedings.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil and criminal liability where they knowingly implement sham arrangements or false residence and beneficial-owner representations. Management disqualification and imprisonment may apply in aggravated fraud cases.
Corporate Distribution and Insolvency Misconduct

Statutory sanction: Repayment of unlawful dividends, damages, insolvency clawback, director-liability proceedings, bankruptcy-related sanctions, management prohibition, and potential criminal penalties for misuse of corporate assets or fraudulent insolvency.

Director personal liability: Managers may be ordered to contribute to all or part of an insufficient insolvency estate, prohibited from managing companies, fined, or imprisoned. Asset seizure is possible; travel bans depend on judicial orders and proceedings.
Anti-Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing

Statutory sanction: Regulatory warnings, administrative fines, business restrictions, account or transaction controls, confiscation, and criminal money-laundering penalties. Aggravated money laundering may carry imprisonment of up to ten years and substantial fines, subject to the applicable facts.

Director personal liability: Directors and managers may be personally prosecuted for knowing participation, complicity, concealment, or obstruction. Civil damages, asset seizure, management bans, and imprisonment may apply.
Accounting and Corporate Record Falsification

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, accounting penalties, rejection of accounts, civil damages, insolvency sanctions, and criminal penalties for falsification, misuse of company assets, or distribution based on fictitious accounts.

Director personal liability: The gérant may be personally liable for intentional accounting misconduct, misuse of corporate assets, insolvency losses, criminal fines, imprisonment, management disqualification, and asset seizure.
Failure to File, Pay, or Cooperate with the Tax Administration

Statutory sanction: Late-filing increases, late-payment interest, formal notices, estimated assessment, audit extensions, higher penalties for opposition to audit, and enforcement against bank accounts or assets. Deliberate obstruction may generate additional criminal or administrative consequences.

Director personal liability: The gérant may face personal tax-solidarity liability where non-compliance is fraudulent or repeatedly prevents collection, together with civil damages, management disqualification, asset enforcement, and possible criminal liability.
Customs and Import Misdeclaration

Statutory sanction: Customs duty and VAT recovery, administrative fines, seizure or forfeiture of goods, suspension of customs facilities, product withdrawal, and possible criminal customs proceedings.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for intentional customs fraud or instructions causing misdeclaration. Asset seizure, management sanctions, fines, and imprisonment may apply in serious cases.
