Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP), administered through the competent Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE); online tax administration portal: impots.gouv.fr. Company formation and business-registration filings are submitted through the Guichet unique operated by the Institut national de la propriété industrielle (INPI).

E-government filing portal

A French SAS obtains its statutory identifiers through the INPI Guichet unique: (1) file incorporation documents, registered-office evidence, beneficial-owner information, management details, corporate purpose, and capital information; (2) receive a SIREN for the legal entity and a SIRET for each establishment from the national business register; (3) obtain the applicable tax account with the competent SIE; (4) request a French intra-Community VAT identification number through the SIE or the DGFiP professional tax portal; and (5) activate the professional account for VAT returns, payment, correspondence, and electronic filing. A foreign entity without a French establishment must apply to the French non-established taxable-person VAT service, generally the Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères, before or when French VAT obligations arise. The application must identify the legal entity, foreign tax number, activities, French customers or transactions, bank details, representative or correspondence arrangements where required, and evidence of taxable transactions. France does not generally impose a turnover threshold on non-established businesses for VAT registration where France is the place of taxation, subject to reverse-charge and special-regime rules. A foreign entity operating through a French permanent establishment must obtain French business-registration identifiers and register with the SIE assigned to that establishment.

France does not generally impose a turnover threshold on non-established businesses for VAT registration where France is the place of taxation, subject to reverse-charge and special-regime rules. A foreign entity operating through a French permanent establishment must obtain French business-registration identifiers and register with the SIE assigned to that establishment.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A SAS is subject to French corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés, IS) by default. A qualifying SAS may elect temporarily for partnership-style income taxation (impôt sur le revenu, IR) for a maximum statutory period of five financial years, subject to conditions including qualifying ownership, size, age, activity, and shareholder approval; the election is notified to the competent tax authority within the applicable deadline and is not a general check-the-box election. A family-owned qualifying company may use a separate family-company election where statutory conditions are met. France has no United States-style entity classification check-the-box regime. Hybrid mismatch rules can deny deductions, include income, or restrict treaty and directive benefits where arrangements produce a deduction/non-inclusion, double deduction, or imported mismatch outcome. These rules do not create a general elective tax classification.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For a non-established taxable person making transactions taxable in France, there is generally no de minimis VAT registration threshold: registration is required when a French VAT obligation arises, unless the customer accounts for VAT under a reverse-charge rule or a special regime applies. For French-established businesses eligible for the domestic franchise en base regime, the principal 2025 turnover thresholds are EUR 37,500 for services and EUR 85,000 for supplies of goods, with enhanced thresholds of EUR 41,250 and EUR 93,500 respectively; the regime is subject to statutory conditions, activity-specific rules, and legislative amendments. A business may voluntarily opt for VAT taxation where permitted. The standard French VAT rate is 20%; reduced rates apply only to specifically listed transactions.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods dispatched from France to a destination outside the European Union may be invoiced at 0% French VAT where the supplier is the exporter or can prove that the goods left the EU customs territory and the statutory export conditions are satisfied. The supplier must retain a customs export declaration and reliable electronic evidence of exit, normally including the export MRN and the customs exit result or equivalent validated exit evidence. Supplies to an EU member state are generally intra-Community supplies, not third-country exports, and require separate conditions including customer VAT identification and transport evidence. B2B services supplied to a business customer established outside France are generally taxed at the customer’s establishment under the place-of-supply rule and are normally invoiced without French VAT, subject to use-and-enjoyment, immovable-property, event, transport, intermediary, and other special rules. Automated data, scraping, proxy, and API services require transaction-by-transaction classification; B2B supplies to a foreign business generally follow the B2B rule, while B2C supplies may be subject to French VAT or an EU special regime depending on customer location and the electronically supplied-service rules. Export zero-rating or exemption must be supported by contemporaneous commercial, contractual, transport, customs, and payment records. VAT refunds require valid input VAT invoices, business-purpose evidence, non-exempt taxable activity, and compliance with the applicable French or EU refund procedure.

  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, customer, goods, value, delivery terms, and zero-rating basis
  • French export customs declaration containing the export MRN and accurate commodity, value, origin, exporter, and consignee data
  • Electronic customs exit evidence, such as the Export Accompanying Document and validated exit result or equivalent customs exit message
  • Transport evidence, such as bill of lading, air waybill, consignment note, carrier proof, or equivalent logistics record
  • Packing list and proof linking the shipped goods to the commercial invoice and customs declaration
  • Purchase order, sales contract, order confirmation, and delivery records
  • Evidence of payment or other commercially reliable settlement records where relevant
  • For intra-Community supplies, customer VAT number validation and transport evidence establishing dispatch to another EU member state
  • For offshore services, contract, customer business-status evidence, customer establishment and location evidence, service-delivery records, invoice, and reverse-charge wording where applicable
  • Foreign-exchange or inward-remittance evidence only where required by the applicable customer, banking, accounting, or refund substantiation process; France does not impose a general export-VAT rule requiring an inward-remittance certificate

France is implementing mandatory electronic invoicing and transaction e-reporting for businesses established in France. From 1 September 2026, all businesses must be able to receive compliant electronic invoices, and large and medium-sized businesses must issue them electronically; small and micro-businesses are scheduled to issue them from 1 September 2027, subject to the applicable implementation timetable. Invoices must generally pass through an approved private electronic invoicing platform (plateforme agréée), with public-sector invoices continuing through Chorus Pro where applicable. France does not operate a universal real-time invoice-by-invoice fiscal-clearance model comparable to Italy. Required data, invoice status information, and applicable domestic and cross-border transaction data are transmitted to the tax administration through the approved platform and e-reporting channels. Electronic records must preserve authenticity, integrity, readability, auditability, and the statutory retention period. Non-established suppliers may be outside the French e-invoicing mandate unless they have a French establishment or otherwise fall within a covered domestic reporting obligation; their French VAT invoicing and reporting duties remain transaction-specific.

France has no general statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export VAT zero-rating. The supplier must be able to prove the legal conditions for the exemption or zero rate and must correct or repay VAT where the evidence is incomplete, contradictory, fabricated, or obtained after the fact. The DGFiP may reassess unpaid VAT, deny input-tax deductions or refunds, recover improperly exempted output VAT, and charge late-payment interest generally at 0.20% per month, subject to statutory rules. Tax penalties can include a 10% surcharge for late payment or filing, 40% for deliberate failure or serious non-compliance, and 80% for fraudulent manoeuvres or abuse of rights; the applicable percentage depends on the conduct and statutory findings. False customs declarations may trigger customs debt, administrative penalties, seizure, and prosecution under the Union Customs Code and French customs law. Intentional tax fraud may result in criminal penalties, including fines and imprisonment under the French Tax Code, in addition to civil tax reassessment. The limitation period and recovery period depend on the relevant VAT, customs, and criminal provisions; records should generally be retained for at least the applicable French VAT and accounting retention periods, commonly ten years for VAT invoices and supporting records.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The euro is fully convertible for both current-account and capital-account transactions within the EU legal framework. France imposes no exchange controls, no prior-authorization requirements, and no quantitative limits on capital inflows or outflows for a French SAS. Profit and dividend remittance to non-resident shareholders is unrestricted, subject only to standard corporate documentation (audited or approved financial statements, statutory auditor certification if applicable) and to FATCA/CRS beneficial-ownership declarations. There are no remittance hurdles at central-bank level; however, bank-level AML/CFT screening of beneficial owners and source-of-funds evidence remains the de facto compliance gate.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. France and the euro area do not maintain statutory export-proceeds surrender obligations, mandatory conversion ratios, repatriation timelines, or foreign-currency holding limits for export and cross-border service receipts. An SAS operating a data-pipeline API business may retain receivables in EUR, USD, or any freely convertible currency. Treasury practice should nevertheless implement a disciplined conversion and cash-pooling policy to minimize EUR/USD working-capital exposure and to align with VAT cash-flow timing.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (BdF) in coordination with the Autorité de Contrôle Prudentiel et de Résolution (ACPR), the European Central Bank (ECB), and the Direction Générale du Trésor (DG Trésor) under the French Monetary and Financial Code (Code Monétaire et Financier) and EU Treaty provisions on free movement of capital.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Rating: High feasibility for payment collection, Medium for full treasury concentration, with legal constraints. A French SAS may onboard EU-licensed Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) and Payment Institutions (PIs) such as Qonto, Shine, or Railsbank-type platforms either under ACPR authorization or under the EU payment-services passport. Offshore (non-EEA) digital EMIs are not permitted to hold client funds or provide payment accounts in France without ACPR or cross-border passporting; they may only be used as supplementary collection rails, never as the primary treasury account, and must not accept regulated deposits. Fully regulated ACPR-licensed EMIs with French IBANs are viable for Stripe-payout reconciliation and for converting USDC into EUR via licensed crypto-fiat exchangers; however, the primary operating account should remain with a tier-1 credit institution to satisfy shareholder audit and lender expectations.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant routing for an SAS capital increase or shareholder loan: SWIFT MT103 from the foreign shareholder bank to the French bank with unstructured or structured remittance information (RFB) designating the beneficiary IBAN, the corporate identifier, and the purpose-of-payment code. For FDI-linked equity injections, banks accept EU Balance of Payments purpose code 'M100 - Equity Capital' or the French BDR (Banque de France reporting) code category for direct investment; for intercompany loans, use code 'M11 - Loans and deposits' or 'R.2 - Financial credit'. The Banque de France maintains statistical reporting (cotation) requirements for direct investment flows exceeding EUR 15 million per calendar year or 10% of capital subscribed by non-residents; banks will request the shareholder resolution, subscription agreement, and bank-account-attestation from the notary if a notarized deed is used. All inbound wires must reference the exact SAS legal name and SIREN number to avoid quarantine.Primary clearing rails for trade and operational settlements: (1) SWIFT MT103 for cross-border supplier payments and intercompany disbursements, with required purpose codes such as 'PUR - Purchase of software and cloud infrastructure' or 'SPI - Service payment intangibles'; (2) SEPA Credit Transfer (SCT) in EUR within the EU with mandatory ISO 20022 structured remittance; (3) Commercial Letter of Credit (L/C) issued via the French relationship bank only for high-value physical-goods procurement, with Incoterms 2020 and SWIFT MT700 flow; (4) Stripe payout settlement to the operational account via SCT for merchant-acquiring revenues; and (5) segregated USDC conversion flow from the ACPR-compliant VASP partner to the EUR operational account, executed on a same-day T+0/T+1 basis with a full reconciliation file.Deliverable EUR/USD forward contracts (plain vanilla, up to 12-24 months) with a licensed French credit institution or investment firm, requiring ISDA documentation or a bank master agreement and evidence of underlying commercial exposureEUR/USD and EUR/GBP FX swaps (swap points rollover for working-capital liquidity management in multi-currency accounts)Euro/USD cross-currency swaps (CCS) for longer-dated shareholder loan or intercompany funding hedgesZero-cost EUR/USD option collars (range forwards) for protecting export-API revenue margins without upfront premiumNon-deliverable forwards (NDFs) permissible only for non-convertible or restricted exotic currency pairs (e.g., emerging-market client settlement currencies) where deliverable forward markets are absent; NDFs are not necessary for EUR/USD given full euro convertibility, and would require individual bank credit approval

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% domestic withholding tax generally applies to dividends and similar distributions paid by a French company to a non-resident beneficial owner, subject to exemptions, treaty relief and anti-abuse rules. A qualifying EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive exemption may reduce the rate to 0%, commonly requiring at least 10% ownership, a qualifying corporate recipient, minimum holding-period conditions or a holding commitment, subject-to-tax status and anti-abuse compliance.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The annual return is generally due within three months after the end of the fiscal year. For a fiscal year ending on 31 December, the normal deadline is the second business day after 1 May of the following year. The final corporate income tax balance is generally payable by the 15th day of the fourth month following year-end; for a 31 December year-end, the usual date is 15 May. Tax instalments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September and 15 December.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Instalments are generally due on the 15th of March, June, September and December. The final balance is generally due by the 15th day of the fourth month after fiscal year-end.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: EUR 400,000,000: French master-file and local-file documentation requirements generally apply to entities or French tax groups meeting the EUR 400 million turnover or gross-assets threshold, or linked to an entity or group meeting the threshold. A separate annual simplified transfer-pricing declaration, Form 2257-SD, generally applies where the French entity or group has turnover or gross assets of at least EUR 50 million, or is linked to an entity meeting that threshold, subject to statutory conditions. Documentation may be requested below the thresholds where the tax administration challenges pricing.

Economic substance scope: France does not operate a single standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Economic substance is nevertheless required in practice through the arm's-length principle, beneficial-ownership requirements, general anti-abuse doctrine, abuse-of-law rules, controlled-foreign-company rules, interest-limitation rules, permanent-establishment principles, anti-hybrid rules, EU ATAD measures and principal-purpose-test treaty provisions. A French SAS providing scraping, data-pipeline or proxy API services should maintain French decision-making, personnel or demonstrable outsourced operational control, technical capability, contracts, risk management, accounting records, customer-service functions and commercially credible remuneration. An entity with nominal directors, no operational capacity and pass-through invoicing is exposed to recharacterization, denial of deductions, withholding-tax disputes and permanent-establishment or beneficial-ownership challenges.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Fraud and Deliberate Evasion

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, late interest, penalties generally ranging from 40% for intentional misconduct to 80% for fraudulent maneuvers or abusive arrangements, and criminal tax-fraud sanctions that can include imprisonment of up to 5 years and a fine of up to EUR 500,000. Aggravated cases, including organized fraud or use of accounts or entities in certain jurisdictions, may carry up to 7 years' imprisonment and a fine of up to EUR 3 million, subject to statutory conditions.

Director personal liability: Directors may face criminal liability where they personally participate in fraud. In addition, a director may be declared jointly liable for tax debts under Article L267 of the French Tax Procedures Book where fraudulent maneuvers or serious and repeated non-compliance make recovery impossible. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax sanction but may arise from judicial proceedings or enforcement measures. Civil restitution, asset seizure and professional disqualification may also apply.
Shell Company, Beneficial Ownership and Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, recharacterization of payments, withholding at domestic or aggravated rates, denial of deductions, abuse-of-law penalties, reassessment and interest. Anti-abuse penalties may reach 80% where fraudulent or abusive conduct is established.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil and criminal exposure for knowingly implementing sham arrangements or false records. Joint tax-debt liability is possible under Article L267 where the statutory conditions are met; insolvency-related management liability may also arise if misconduct contributes to insufficient assets.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under French transfer-pricing rules, denial of deductions, withholding-tax exposure, late interest, 40% or 80% penalties depending on conduct, and a penalty for missing or materially defective transfer-pricing documentation that can be substantial and is linked to reassessed profits or statutory amounts.

Director personal liability: Directors are not automatically liable for an ordinary pricing adjustment, but personal liability may arise for intentional concealment, false documentation, obstruction, fraudulent accounting or conduct causing unrecoverable tax debt.
Cryptocurrency, Payment Services and AML

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment and penalties for unreported income, accounting penalties, freezing or seizure of assets, and AML sanctions. Money laundering can carry up to 5 years' imprisonment and a EUR 375,000 fine, with aggravated penalties potentially reaching 10 years and EUR 750,000 where statutory conditions apply. Regulated-activity breaches may result in administrative or criminal sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors and beneficial owners may be personally prosecuted for knowingly facilitating laundering, sanctions evasion or unlicensed financial activity. Civil confiscation, asset seizure, management bans and judicial restrictions may apply; travel bans are case-specific and not automatic.
False Invoicing and Undocumented Intercompany Services

Statutory sanction: Denial of deductions, corporate tax reassessment, VAT reassessment where applicable, penalties for deliberate or fraudulent conduct, and possible criminal prosecution for accounting or tax fraud.

Director personal liability: A director who authorizes or knowingly approves fictitious invoices may face criminal liability, personal civil claims, tax-debt solidarity in exceptional statutory circumstances and insolvency-related liability.
Unlawful Web Scraping, Data Protection and Cybersecurity

Statutory sanction: Administrative orders, processing restrictions, data deletion, contractual damages, intellectual-property or database-right claims and GDPR fines of up to EUR 20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, for the relevant infringements. Unauthorized access to an automated data-processing system may also trigger criminal sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal criminal exposure for intentional unauthorized access, unlawful data processing or obstruction, as well as civil claims where personal fault is established. Corporate tax deductions may be challenged if expenditure relates to unlawful conduct.
Failure to Maintain Accounting and Corporate Records

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment based on estimated income, reversal of deductions, accounting and tax penalties, rejection of claims, enforced collection and potential criminal consequences for obstruction or fraudulent accounting.

Director personal liability: The president and other de facto managers may be liable for serious management failures, fraudulent accounting, insolvency misconduct and unrecoverable tax debts. A corporate veil is not ordinarily pierced solely because of ownership, but personal liability increases materially where the company is used as an instrument of fraud.
Late Filing, Late Payment and Repeated Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Generally a 10% late-filing surcharge, a 5% late-payment surcharge and late interest of 0.20% per month, with higher penalties after formal notice or where deliberate or fraudulent conduct is established. Repeated failures may trigger audit escalation and enforced recovery.

Director personal liability: Ordinary late filing does not automatically create director liability. Serious and repeated non-compliance combined with fraudulent maneuvers can support Article L267 tax-debt solidarity and management-liability proceedings.
