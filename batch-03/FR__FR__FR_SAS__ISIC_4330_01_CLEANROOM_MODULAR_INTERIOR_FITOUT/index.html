Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction générale des finances publiques (DGFiP), principally through the relevant Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE) or the Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères (SIEE) for non-established businesses. Official tax portal: impots.gouv.fr; business formalities are filed through the Guichet unique operated by INPI at formalites.entreprises.gouv.fr.

E-government filing portal

A French SAS is registered through the Guichet unique. Registration results in assignment of a SIREN and establishment-specific SIRET; the SIREN is generally used as the French business tax identifier. The company must separately request VAT identification from the competent SIE if it makes transactions requiring French VAT identification. A foreign company with a French VAT obligation applies to the SIEE, submitting evidence of incorporation, constitutional documents, business activity, identity and authority of representatives, and details of French taxable transactions. A non-EU-established taxable person generally appoints a French fiscal representative unless an applicable statutory exception or mutual-assistance arrangement applies; an EU-established business generally does not require one. VAT identification does not itself establish a French permanent establishment.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A SAS is ordinarily subject to French corporation tax (impôt sur les sociétés, IS). A qualifying young company may elect temporarily for income-tax treatment under the conditions and time limits in the French Tax Code, including ownership, activity, age, and turnover or balance-sheet tests. Certain qualifying family-owned companies may elect for partnership-style income-tax treatment subject to statutory conditions. France has no US-style check-the-box election; elections do not generally convert the SAS into a fiscally disregarded entity. Hybrid mismatch rules under French law implement applicable EU anti-hybrid provisions.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For a France-established business using the domestic small-business exemption (franchise en base), the general thresholds are EUR 85,000 for supplies of goods and EUR 37,500 for services, subject to statutory higher thresholds, prior-year/current-year tests, exclusions, and amendments. These thresholds are not a general registration safe harbor for foreign businesses: a non-established business making French taxable supplies generally must register from the first taxable transaction unless a specific exemption or reverse-charge rule applies. EU cross-border B2C distance sales are subject to the EUR 10,000 EU-wide threshold where applicable; the EU One Stop Shop may be used for qualifying supplies.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods dispatched or transported from France to a destination outside the EU may be exempt with credit (effectively 0% VAT) when statutory export conditions are met and the supplier retains evidence that the goods left the EU. Intra-EU supplies of goods may be exempt only if the customer is VAT-identified in another Member State, the goods are transported there, and reporting and evidentiary requirements are satisfied. Services are not zero-rated merely because the customer is outside France or payment is received from abroad: the general B2B place-of-supply rule, applicable exceptions, and any specific export-related exemption must be assessed. Cleanroom fit-out and services connected with identified immovable property are generally located where that property is situated; French-located work is not converted into an export by a non-French customer. Input VAT recovery remains subject to ordinary deduction rules and valid evidence.

  • Customs export declaration and export movement reference, including proof of exit from the EU customs territory
  • Commercial invoice, contract or purchase order, and records identifying the goods and the foreign customer
  • Transport evidence appropriate to the route, such as bill of lading, airway bill, CMR, or carrier delivery documentation
  • Evidence of delivery or receipt outside the EU where available
  • Customer VAT identification and transport evidence for qualifying intra-EU exempt supplies
  • Bank payment and foreign-exchange records where relevant to transaction substantiation; France imposes no general inward-remittance certificate requirement for VAT zero-rating

France is implementing mandatory domestic B2B electronic invoicing and transaction/payment e-reporting. Under the enacted timetable, all businesses must be able to receive e-invoices from 1 September 2026; issuance obligations begin on that date for large and intermediate-sized businesses and on 1 September 2027 for SMEs and micro-enterprises, subject to any later legal amendments. Invoices must pass through an approved private platform (plateforme agréée), directly or through a compatible solution, with required data transmitted to the tax administration. This is not a general real-time pre-clearance or digital-stamping regime. Cross-border transactions and specified transactions outside e-invoicing are generally addressed through e-reporting rules.

France imposes no general statutory deadline requiring export proceeds to be repatriated to France. Export exemption may be denied and output VAT assessed if the taxpayer cannot establish the export or other exemption conditions; input VAT deductions or refunds may be adjusted, with interest for late payment and applicable tax penalties. Deliberate or fraudulent conduct can attract aggravated penalties and, where statutory elements are met, criminal sanctions. The applicable assessment limitation period is generally three years for VAT, subject to statutory extensions, interruption, or longer periods for specified circumstances.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France maintains full currency convertibility for both current-account and capital-account transactions pursuant to EU Treaty freedom of capital movement (Articles 63-66 TFEU) and the complete post-1989 deregulation of exchange controls. EUR is fully convertible into all deliverable currencies without license, approval, or quota restrictions. Profit remittance from an SAS is freely permitted upon certification of distributable reserves, compliance with legal capital-maintenance rules, and absence of an EU sanctions nexus. Administrative constraints are limited to: (i) balance-of-payments statistical declarations to the Banque de France for cross-border transactions at or above EUR 750,000 per operation; (ii) mandatory sanctions screening; and (iii) enhanced due diligence on flows involving the EU list of non-cooperative tax jurisdictions. No prior government approval applies to dividend distribution, trade settlement, or intercompany funding.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No statutory export-proceeds surrender mechanism or mandatory conversion ratio applies in France. Export receivables may be held indefinitely in EUR or foreign currency, credited to French or EU accounts, or converted at market rate without any timeline constraint. Residual obligations are limited to: (i) statistical reporting to the Banque de France for export transactions exceeding EUR 750,000 per operation; (ii) documentary support for VAT through customs export declarations; and (iii) sanctions and anti-money-laundering screening at the receiving credit institution.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (Bank of France); foreign-exchange and financial-sanctions administration delegated to the Direction Générale du Trésor (DG Trésor) of the Ministère de l'Économie, des Finances et de la Souveraineté industrielle et numérique under the Code monétaire et financier.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: LOW-to-MEDIUM. ACPR-licensed electronic money institutions (EU-authorised EMIs passporting into France) can provide EUR IBAN accounts, SEPA instant and standard credit transfers, and multi-currency wallet settlement. They cannot, however, issue, confirm, or negotiate documentary letters of credit under UCP 600, provide uncommitted trade finance, or hold capital-escrow funds requiring segregated trust treatment. Non-EU or offshore EMIs are not viable primary banking partners for an SAS due to (i) absence of EU passporting rights, (ii) inability to evidence clearing accounts with the Banque de France, and (iii) rejection of such providers by downstream cleanroom clients and project guarantors. Offshore EMIs should be used only as ancillary disbursement rails for immaterial operational payments, never as the treasury settlement backbone.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital injections from non-resident shareholders must be routed via SWIFT MT103 or MT202 to the SAS's EUR account at its French bank, crediting the recipient's full IBAN and BIC. The wire must carry an ISO 20022 purpose code for equity participation (e.g., EQUI), the name and country of the ultimate remitter, and a reference to the share-subscription agreement. The French receiving bank must be pre-advised of the intended inflow to complete (i) sanctions screening of the outside shareholder and any intermediary banks, (ii) the Banque de France balance-of-payments statistical declaration for amounts at or above EUR 750,000, and (iii) internal investment-capital reporting. Upon clearing through TARGET2 (for EUR) or correspondent banking chains, the bank will match the payment to the deposited board resolutions and proof-of-funds dossier; funds may then be segregated into a dedicated Capital Escrow Account and released to the operational account only against documented project commencement milestones and approved drawdown instructions.France-based ISIC 4330 cleanroom fit-out contractors may disburse via the following compliant rails: (1) SEPA Credit Transfer (ISO 20022 pain.001/pain.002) for EUR-denominated domestic and EU supplier payments cleared through TARGET2; (2) SWIFT MT103 for USD, GBP, CHF, and other non-EUR payments through correspondent banking; (3) irrevocable Commercial Documentary Letters of Credit (UCP 600) issued via SWIFT MT700 for major cleanroom MEP equipment procurement, payable against checking certificates; (4) Standby Letters of Credit (SWIFT MT760) issued as performance bonds or advance-payment guarantees; (5) documentary collections (DP/DA) for low-risk, established sub-supplier relationships; and (6) milestone-based progress wire transfers matching signed interim payment applications, inspection certificates, and architect/engineer certifications under the construction contract. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% corporate income tax on taxable profits under Article 219 of the French Tax Code (Code général des impôts, CGI). A reduced 15% rate applies to the first EUR 42,500 of taxable profit for qualifying small companies, generally subject to annual turnover not exceeding EUR 10 million, fully paid-up share capital, and at least 75% ownership by qualifying individuals or qualifying entities.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The standard French withholding tax on dividends paid by a French company to a non-resident shareholder is generally 25%, subject to treaty relief, EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive implementation, and domestic exemptions. Dividends paid to a qualifying EU or EEA parent company may be exempt where minimum ownership, holding-period, subject-to-tax, legal-form, and anti-abuse conditions are satisfied. A 75% rate may apply to certain payments to entities located in non-cooperative states or territories, subject to statutory exceptions and proof requirements.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a financial year ending on 31 December, the electronic corporate income tax return is generally due by the second business day after 1 May of the following year, subject to the annual administrative calendar. For a financial year closing on a date other than 31 December, the return is generally due within three months after the financial year-end. The balance of corporate income tax is generally payable by the fifteenth day of the fourth month following year-end, with special rules for 31 December year-ends. Quarterly installments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September, and 15 December, subject to statutory exceptions and annual calendar adjustments.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: EUR 400,000,000. Full French transfer pricing documentation obligations generally apply where the French entity or French tax-consolidated group exceeds EUR 400 million of annual pre-tax turnover or gross assets, or where the entity is controlled by or controls an entity exceeding that threshold, subject to the detailed conditions of Articles L.13 AA and L.13 AB of the French Tax Procedures Code. Below the threshold, arm's-length documentation and simplified documentation may still be required where requested by the French tax authority, and the arm's-length principle applies to all controlled transactions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to groups meeting the EUR 750 million consolidated revenue threshold. Related-party transactions should be identified by category, counterparty, jurisdiction, amount, contractual basis, pricing method, and business rationale.

Economic substance scope: France has no standalone UAE-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. French tax authorities apply domestic substance, abuse-of-law, anti-fraud, transfer pricing, beneficial ownership, permanent establishment, and general anti-abuse doctrines. A French SAS must have genuine French functions, decision-making, personnel or appropriately controlled resources, premises or operational capacity where commercially required, accounting records, bank activity, contractual authority, and risk assumption consistent with its claimed business. A cleanroom fit-out contractor should evidence project management, technical supervision, procurement, subcontractor oversight, site activity, health and safety compliance, insurance, customer negotiations, and asset or personnel allocation. Artificial profit booking, circular payments, conduit financing, undocumented management fees, or a company lacking operational capacity may result in denial of deductions, withholding-tax relief, permanent establishment findings, transfer pricing adjustments, penalties, and director or officer exposure.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate income tax evasion and fraudulent accounting

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, recovery of unpaid tax, late-payment interest, penalties generally ranging from 40% to 80% depending on conduct, and criminal tax-fraud sanctions that may include imprisonment of up to seven years and a fine of up to EUR 3 million, subject to the applicable aggravated-fraud provisions and court findings.

Director personal liability: Directors or de facto managers may incur criminal liability, civil liability for personal fault, and tax-payment liability where they have made fraudulent maneuvers or seriously and repeatedly breached tax obligations, including action under the French director tax-liability procedure. Travel restrictions are not automatic; judicial control, passport restrictions, or travel bans may be ordered in criminal proceedings. Imprisonment requires a criminal conviction.
Shell company, artificial substance, or abuse of law

Statutory sanction: Denial of deductions, recharacterization of transactions, withholding-tax recovery, permanent establishment or beneficial-ownership findings, 40% or 80% tax penalties where applicable, and possible abuse-of-law penalty of up to 80% of the tax avoided, subject to statutory conditions and committee procedures.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for intentional or grossly negligent misconduct causing tax loss, fraudulent management, or criminal participation. Corporate limited liability does not protect a director from personal criminal liability or liability for a distinct personal tort.
Transfer pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Primary adjustment, possible secondary adjustment or deemed distribution treatment, withholding tax, late-payment interest, and penalties for deliberate or fraudulent understatement. Missing or materially inadequate documentation may trigger the specific transfer pricing documentation penalty and permit assessment based on information available to the tax authority.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil or criminal exposure where pricing manipulation is intentional, fraudulent, or causes insolvency or tax loss. Ordinary pricing disagreement generally creates corporate tax exposure rather than automatic personal liability.
Withholding tax and profit repatriation failure

Statutory sanction: Assessment of unpaid withholding tax against the French payer, late-payment interest, applicable 10%, 40%, or 80% penalties, and potential 75% withholding for relevant payments connected with non-cooperative states or territories. The payer may remain liable even where the foreign recipient has already received the payment.

Director personal liability: A director may be personally exposed where failure to withhold or remit results from deliberate misconduct, fraudulent maneuvers, or serious and repeated non-compliance. No automatic personal liability or travel ban arises solely from an ordinary withholding error.
Value added tax fraud and false invoicing

Statutory sanction: VAT reassessment, interest, penalties generally up to 40% for deliberate conduct and 80% for fraudulent conduct or certain abusive arrangements, potential joint and several recovery in fraud cases, and criminal prosecution for aggravated fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for fraudulent invoicing, tax evasion, or knowingly participating in VAT fraud. Courts may impose fines, imprisonment, confiscation, and professional restrictions. Travel bans or passport restrictions require a competent judicial order.
Anti-money laundering and terrorist financing

Statutory sanction: Potential criminal money-laundering sanctions include imprisonment and substantial fines, with aggravated penalties for organized or repeated conduct. Assets and proceeds may be frozen or confiscated. Separate administrative sanctions may apply to regulated professionals and reporting entities.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally prosecuted for knowingly laundering proceeds, facilitating prohibited transactions, or obstructing required controls. Confiscation, imprisonment, professional prohibitions, judicial control, and court-ordered travel restrictions may apply; none is automatic merely because a company has an AML deficiency.
Sanctions, export-control, and customs violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative or criminal sanctions may include seizure, confiscation, fines, suspension or denial of authorizations, customs recovery, and imprisonment under applicable French, EU, and international sanctions regimes. The exact penalty depends on the instrument, destination, goods, intent, and applicable authorization.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may be prosecuted for knowing or negligent participation where the applicable regime creates such liability. Corporate authorization or delegation does not protect a person who personally ordered, approved, or concealed the conduct.
Labor, construction, and undeclared-work violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative fines, back-payment of social contributions and taxes, exclusion from public contracts, suspension of activity, civil claims, and criminal sanctions including fines and imprisonment for serious undeclared-work or safety violations.

Director personal liability: The SAS and responsible managers may be liable for labor and safety violations. Directors can face criminal prosecution for personal participation or failure to discharge legally assigned safety duties; civil claims may include employee compensation and restitution.
Corporate records, beneficial ownership, and filing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative fines, injunctions, rejection or correction of filings, tax reassessment, director-management bans, and criminal sanctions for false accounts, fraud, or concealment where the statutory elements are satisfied.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur personal criminal liability for false accounts, concealment, or intentional filing misconduct. Insolvency proceedings may impose personal contribution to liabilities or management sanctions where mismanagement caused or aggravated the insufficiency of assets.
Insolvency and fraudulent preference

Statutory sanction: Avoidance of transactions, recovery of assets, personal contribution to insufficient assets, management bans, bankruptcy-related criminal sanctions, fines, and imprisonment for fraudulent bankruptcy or related misconduct.

Director personal liability: A director may be ordered to contribute to company liabilities, prohibited from managing businesses, or prosecuted where personal misconduct caused or worsened insolvency. Travel restrictions and imprisonment depend on judicial proceedings and conviction; they are not automatic consequences of insolvency.
