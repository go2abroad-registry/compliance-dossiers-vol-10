Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France maintains full current-account and capital-account convertibility under Article 63 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (free movement of capital) and the French Monetary and Financial Code (Code Monétaire et Financier, CMF). Non-resident shareholders may freely subscribe to capital, receive dividends, profit distributions, loan repayments, and liquidation proceeds. No pre-approval from the Banque de France is required for inbound equity or outbound profit remittances. Statutory reporting (Bof/FEC declarations) applies solely for statistical and balance-of-payments monitoring when cross-border transactions exceed €30,000 per calendar month; such reporting does not constitute a control or a restrictiveness hurdle. Profit remittance can be executed through normal banking channels without conversion quota, provided the transfer is accompanied by proper tax clearance (e.g., corporate income tax returns filed) and a valid corporate resolution of the distributing French SAS.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France no longer imposes statutory export-proceeds surrender or mandatory foreign-exchange conversion ratios. Export receipts of the French SAS may be retained in foreign currency in a transferable foreign-currency account (compte de change) or credit-converted into EUR at the prevailing market rate. Obligations are purely declarative: (i) intra-EU dispatches must be reported to customs via the DEB (Déclaration d'Échanges de Biens); (ii) extra-EU exports require a customs export declaration (DAE) with the product classified under HS 2103/2104; and (iii) cross-border financial flows exceeding €30,000 per month composed of trade payments, advances on invoices, or final settlements must be declared to Banque de France using the FEC reporting framework. There is no mandatory conversion ratio; invoicing in EUR or foreign currency is free under French commercial law, although the bank may ask for a signed contract to evidence the underlying trade.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (Central Bank of France) acting in coordination with the ACPR (Autorité de Contrôle Prudentiel et de Résolution, the French Prudential Supervision and Resolution Authority) and DG Trésor (Direction Générale du Trésor, Ministry of Finance) for exchange control and balance-of-payments matters.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. French-regulated fintechs and electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as Qonto, Shine, and Lydia, as well as EEA-passported EMIs (Revolut, Wise, N26 through EU licenses), can open a multi-currency operational account for a French SAS using digitized e-KYC. Legal constraints: (i) an EMI does not hold a banking license and cannot provide domiciliation, documentary credit issuance, or a full treasury/swaption platform; (ii) UK-licensed fintechs without EEA passport are no longer permitted to provide payment accounts into France post-Brexit and require a French or EU authorization; (iii) strict segregation of client funds applies under Article L. 526-10 CMF, and interest-bearing or escrow features are prohibited; (iv) large trade settlements via letter of credit require an ACPR-licensed credit institution; and (v) the French 'right to an account' (droit au compte, Article L. 312-1 CMF) guarantees access only at Banque de France assigned counterparties, not at unregulated EMIs. The recommended blueprint is a primary EUR current account at a Tier-1 bank plus a digital multi-currency account at Qonto or Wise for low-value disbursements and currency sweep automation.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

The capital injection from a non-resident shareholder is routed by authenticated SWIFT MT103 (or MT202 for interbank credit) to the French SAS's EUR current account at a Tier-1 French bank. The wire must carry the French 'NCO' (Nomenclature des Codes Objets) purpose code for direct investment — typically 'S10 – Fonds propres – Investissements directs' — and the credit reference should cite the share subscription agreement date and registration stage. Where the injection exceeds €30,000, the bank files the BOF/FEC statistical declaration with Banque de France within 20 business days of the month-end. The receiving bank performs origin-of-funds verification and credit-scoring; funds above €50,000 may trigger a KYCC request for the funding entity's annual report and board resolution. If the parent is a Chinese corporate, the sending bank requires the SAFE FDI registration number (or equivalent ODI filing) and the transaction reference must be included in the wire; an escrow account in the name of the '… en formation' or post-registration 'SAS capital' account is acceptable only if segregated at the same credit institution, releasing upon issuance of the Kbis certificate.Settlement of import purchases from suppliers in China/East Asia is executed through (1) the SWIFT MT103 channel for open-account trade and advance payments, with NCO purpose code 'S30/G00 – Règlement des importations de marchandises' or the specific goods trade declaration referenced on the wire; (2) a documentary Commercial Letter of Credit (L/C) issued by the French bank on the importer's behalf governed by UCP 600, negotiated against presentation of commercial invoice, full set of clean-on-board bills of lading, packing list, certificate of insurance, EUR.1 or certificate of origin, and a halal/composition certificate for the tallow base; and (3) for EU-destined trade, normal SEPA CT or intra-EU payment channels. All outbound wires require the underlying KYC profile of the beneficiary to be pre-validated for anti-corruption and OFAC/EU sanction screening. E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium. French-regulated fintechs and electronic money institutions (EMIs) such as Qonto, Shine, and Lydia, as well as EEA-passported EMIs (Revolut, Wise, N26 through EU licenses), can open a multi-currency operational account for a French SAS using digitized e-KYC. Legal constraints: (i) an EMI does not hold a banking license and cannot provide domiciliation, documentary credit issuance, or a full treasury/swaption platform; (ii) UK-licensed fintechs without EEA passport are no longer permitted to provide payment accounts into France post-Brexit and require a French or EU authorization; (iii) strict segregation of client funds applies under Article L. 526-10 CMF, and interest-bearing or escrow features are prohibited; (iv) large trade settlements via letter of credit require an ACPR-licensed credit institution; and (v) the French 'right to an account' (droit au compte, Article L. 312-1 CMF) guarantees access only at Banque de France assigned counterparties, not at unregulated EMIs. The recommended blueprint is a primary EUR current account at a Tier-1 bank plus a digital multi-currency account at Qonto or Wise for low-value disbursements and currency sweep automation.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% French withholding tax generally applies to dividends and similar distributions paid by a French company to a non-resident recipient, subject to treaty relief and domestic exemptions. A qualifying EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive exemption may reduce withholding to 0% where statutory ownership, holding-period, subject-to-tax, anti-abuse and beneficial-ownership conditions are satisfied. The recipient must generally provide the required residence, ownership and eligibility documentation before payment.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The annual return and schedules are generally due within three months after the end of the financial year. For a financial year ending on 31 December, the filing deadline is generally the second business day after 1 May of the following year. Corporate income tax instalments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September and 15 December. The final balance is generally due by the 15th day of the fourth month following the financial year-end; for a 31 December year-end, the balance is generally due by 15 May.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: EUR 150 million: large French entities generally subject to full transfer-pricing documentation must prepare a Master File and Local File where annual pre-tax turnover or gross assets reaches at least EUR 150 million, or where a qualifying related French or foreign entity meets the statutory conditions. Entities meeting the lower EUR 50 million threshold may also have an annual simplified transfer-pricing declaration obligation, generally using Form 2257-SD. Documentation must be available in French or with an acceptable French translation when requested.

Economic substance scope: France has no single general-purpose Economic Substance Regulation equivalent to the United Kingdom or certain offshore regimes. Nevertheless, French substance is required under transfer-pricing, beneficial-ownership, principal-purpose, abuse-of-law, permanent-establishment, controlled-foreign-company, anti-hybrid, interest-deduction and EU anti-abuse rules. A French SAS should maintain genuine French management and control, appropriate personnel or outsourced operational capacity, premises or functional infrastructure, French books and records, decision-making evidence, inventory and logistics controls, bank-account governance, commercial risk assumption and demonstrable functions. A conduit company with no commercial rationale, no operational capacity or back-to-back arrangements may lose treaty benefits and deductions and may create tax, VAT, customs and permanent-establishment exposure.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Tax Returns

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, late-payment interest, penalties generally ranging from 40% to 80%, and criminal prosecution. Ordinary tax fraud may carry imprisonment of up to five years and a fine of up to EUR 500,000; aggravated cases may carry up to seven years and EUR 3 million, subject to statutory aggravating conditions.

Director personal liability: The president and directors may incur criminal liability for intentional participation, personal civil liability for wrongful conduct and tax liability under the French Tax Procedure Code where fraudulent or repeated conduct makes recovery from the company impossible. Travel restrictions or asset restraints may be ordered by judicial authorities; imprisonment is not automatic and requires criminal conviction.
VAT Fraud and Carousel Transactions

Statutory sanction: VAT assessment, denial of input credit, interest, penalties generally up to 40% or 80%, possible joint and several liability in fraudulent chains, and criminal tax-fraud sanctions where intent is established.

Director personal liability: Directors knowingly participating in VAT fraud may face criminal prosecution, asset seizure, civil recovery and management disqualification. Personal liability is fact-dependent and is not created solely by the existence of an unpaid VAT balance.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, denial of deductions, withholding-tax adjustments, interest, penalties, possible 2257-SD and documentation penalties, and potential treaty relief denial.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable where they intentionally approve fraudulent arrangements or commit separable management misconduct. A mere transfer-pricing adjustment ordinarily remains a company liability.
Shell Company and Abuse of Treaty Benefits

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty or EU directive relief, domestic withholding-tax assessment, reassessment under abuse-of-law or principal-purpose rules, interest and penalties, and possible permanent-establishment or beneficial-ownership findings.

Director personal liability: Directors may face criminal or civil exposure if they knowingly implement sham arrangements, submit false certificates or conceal the real recipient. Courts may order asset restraints and management bans; imprisonment requires proof of a criminal offense.
AML, Terrorist-Financing and Sanctions Breaches

Statutory sanction: Suspicious-transaction reporting failures may lead to regulatory sanctions, while money laundering and sanctions offenses may result in substantial fines, confiscation, business restrictions and criminal imprisonment. Sanctions vary by offense and competent authority.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be personally prosecuted for knowing participation, facilitation or intentional reporting failures. Corporate veil protection does not protect deliberate criminal conduct. Travel bans, asset freezes and confiscation may be ordered under applicable judicial or sanctions procedures.
Customs and Import Misdeclaration

Statutory sanction: Recovery of customs duties and import VAT, customs fines, seizure or forfeiture of goods, suspension of customs simplifications and possible criminal proceedings for aggravated fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for intentional customs fraud or serious management misconduct. The SAS generally remains liable for ordinary customs debt, but criminal sanctions and asset measures may reach responsible individuals.
Food-Product Regulatory and Product-Liability Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Product withdrawal or recall, administrative closure, destruction or seizure, administrative fines, consumer compensation and criminal penalties under food-safety and consumer-protection legislation.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally prosecuted where they knowingly market unsafe or misleading products or fail to take required corrective action. Civil product liability may apply to the company and responsible individuals depending on the facts.
Accounting, Corporate-Record and Beneficial-Ownership Failures

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, accounting penalties, refusal of tax deductions, civil claims, director disqualification and, for false accounts or insolvency-related misconduct, criminal sanctions.

Director personal liability: The president may incur liability for mismanagement, unlawful distributions, fraudulent accounts or contribution to an insufficient insolvency estate. Courts may impose asset contribution orders, management bans and, in serious cases, imprisonment.
Illegal Profit Distribution and Thin-Capitalization Abuse

Statutory sanction: Repayment of unlawful distributions, withholding-tax and corporate-tax reassessments, denial of interest deductions, penalties and possible insolvency or abuse-of-assets proceedings.

Director personal liability: The president and directors approving unlawful distributions may owe restitution and damages, face management bans and, where criminal elements exist, fines or imprisonment. Shareholders may also be required to repay distributions received in bad faith.
Late Tax Filing and Payment

Statutory sanction: Generally a 10% late-filing or late-payment surcharge, increased in specified cases, plus 0.20% monthly late-payment interest and any specific transfer-pricing, withholding or tax-credit penalties.

Director personal liability: Ordinary late payment normally remains a corporate debt. Personal liability may arise where a director's fraudulent conduct or repeated deliberate obstruction makes tax recovery impossible, subject to judicial findings.
