Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction Générale des Finances Publiques (DGFiP) of the French Ministry of Economy and Finance; official tax portal: https://www.impots.gouv.fr (professional 'Espace Professionnel'); business-formation one-stop shop: https://formalites.entreprises.gouv.fr; VAT OSS/IOSS portal and Chorus Pro e-invoicing platform operated under DGFiP supervision.

E-government filing portal

Step 1 – Incorporate the SAS through the centralised 'Guichet Unique Électronique des Formalités des Entreprises' (formalites.entreprises.gouv.fr), which transmits the file to the Greffe du Tribunal de Commerce, INSEE and DGFiP; the company receives its SIREN/SIRET identifiers and its RCS registration extract (Kbis). Step 2 – Where the company has its registered office or a fixed establishment in France, the competent Service des Impôts des Entreprises (SIE) issues the French VAT identification number (n° de TVA intracommunautaire) automatically or upon filing of the standard registration form (CERFA n° 15954 / 'TVA 3530') pursuant to CGI Articles 286-I-2° and 287. Step 3 – A non-resident entity without a French fixed establishment must obtain a French VAT ID from the competent DGFiP non-resident unit before supplying taxable services in France and, unless the OSS/IOSS or an applicable exclusion applies, must appoint an accredited VAT fiscal representative (représentant fiscal accrédité under CGI Article 164 quinquies A) who assumes joint and several liability. Step 4 – The supporting dossier comprises: power of attorney, Kbis/extract, articles of association, evidence of the French establishment or business verification, certificate of the effective manager's residence and, for vessel leasing, the vessel registration/flag certificate and the applicable maritime charter licence (EU Regulation 852/2004 manning/flag evidence or French flag certificate).

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation — A French SAS is an opaque commercial company which is mandatorily subject to French corporate income tax ('impôt sur les sociétés' – IS) at entity level on all profits within its territorial scope under CGI Articles 206-1 and 209. France does not offer a pass-through/check-the-box classification: the SAS cannot be treated as a partnership or disregarded entity. The territorial scope follows the place of effective management ('siège de direction effective'); an SAS chartering offshore supply vessels worldwide remains fully within the French IS net, with the separate leveraging of tax treaties and CGI Article 209 to prevent double taxation of foreign charter income.

Available election forms

France does not provide a US-style 'check-the-box' election; the SAS is per se entity-level taxed. The applicable statutory elections relevant to the indirect and hybrid-planning posture are: (i) formation of a single VAT taxable person ('assujetti unique') under CGI Article 267 bis, consolidating group members for VAT without merging corporate income tax positions; (ii) the election under CGI Article 269 to pay VAT on 'débits' (invoice date) instead of 'encaissements' (cash receipts), useful for long-term offshore charter hire; (iii) the limited option for small enterprises to waive VAT exemption under CGI Article 293 B IV, which is practically irrelevant for a capital-intensive SAS; (iv) the French anti-hybrid election and limitation rules under CGI Articles 212 bis and 212 quater, governing the deductibility of hybrid-financing payments and reverse-hybrid structures; and (v) the option for non-resident shareholders concerning gross-up on distributions under CGI Article 167 bis. No tax-exempt-zone election exists for mainland France: the jurisdiction is unitary and the offshore vessel charter, when managed from a French port, is automatically within the French supply-of-services rules of CGI Articles 259 A and 259 B.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

French-established taxable persons must register for VAT upon the first taxable transaction with no turnover de minimis, beyond the small-business franchise ('franchise en base de TVA', CGI Article 293 B) capped at EUR 85,000 worldwide turnover for supplies of goods and EUR 37,500 for supplies of services; the franchise is not accessible to a foreign-owned corporation exercising a fixed French establishment. Non-resident suppliers of distance sales of goods and electronically supplied services to French consumers become registrable once their EU-wide annual turnover exceeds EUR 10,000 (CGI Article 293 A), unless the OSS is used. A non-resident lessor supplying vessel-chartering services B2B to a French VAT-identified operator is registrable without any turnover threshold by operation of Articles 286-I and 259 A CGI.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Zero rating (0% TVA) is available on: (A) supplies of goods physically exported to destinations outside the customs territory of the EU, under CGI Article 262-I-1°, upon conclusive proof of dispatch; and (B) the supply, leasing, hire or charter (bareboat, time or voyage) of seagoing commercial vessels used in high-seas or international maritime navigation – including offshore platform supply vessels, their crew and equipment – under CGI Article 262-II-1°/2° ('navires de commerce destinés à la navigation en haute mer' and services rendered for the needs of such vessels). To sustain the zero rating the French SAS must demonstrate: (i) the vessel is a seagoing commercial ship (French/EU-flag certificate, safe-manning licence); (ii) the charter contract and AIS logs evidence missions performed predominantly beyond French territorial waters or across international boundaries; (iii) any French-flagged cabotage element or crew-service component that remains fundamentally territorial is carved out and billed at 20%; and (iv) where the customer is a non-French taxable person, the normal B2B place-of-supply rule of Article 259 A may locate the service outside France entirely, which also achieves 0% effective taxation subject to OECD/TP documentation.

  • Customs Export Declaration (DAU) filed via the DELTA-X electronic customs system together with the Exit Summary Declaration (ENS) evidencing the physical exit of the vessel/goods from the EU customs territory
  • Bill of Lading, Sea Waybill or a lawfully executed Charter Party containing the French port of loading, foreign port of discharge and vessel particulars
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or Bank SWIFT credit confirmation proving settlement of the export consideration in the agreed foreign currency
  • Master's declaration and continuous AIS position log for the voyage period, corroborating the high-seas or international-route operation
  • The commercial invoice in structured Factur-X/UBL format cross-referencing the zero-rate exemption basis (CGI Article 262-II-1°)
  • Vessel registration/flag certificate, EU/EEA manning licence, and the offshore operations order from the charterer for the offshore supply missions

France mandates structured e-invoicing and e-reporting under CGI Article 289-2, with compulsory deployment as follows: large and mid-size taxpayers from 1 September 2026; small and micro enterprises from 1 September 2027. All B2B invoices must be emitted in a certified structured format (Factur-X, UBL 2.x or UN/CEFACT CII) and exchanged through either a registered commercial 'Plateforme de Dématérialisation Partenaire' (PDP) or the State portal Chorus Pro; B2C transactions are subject to simultaneous e-reporting to DGFiP. France does not operate invoice-level fiscal stamping or pre-clearance, but the transmission occurs in near-real-time at issuance with a full audit trail (irréversibilité des données). In parallel, the offshore marine charter business is subject to the electronic customs export manifest system DELTA-X and the Entry/Exit Summary Declaration (ENS), and French-flag vessels operating from French ports must maintain continuous AIS position reporting as part of the e-regulatory evidence.

DGFiP audit practice (Articles L. 10 and L. 13 of the Livre des Procédures Fiscales) applies documentary strict-proof standards to export zero-rating. Where the export evidence is missing, inconsistent or circular, DGFiP retroactively reclassifies the supply as taxable in France, imposing: (i) interest de retard at 0.2% per month (Article 1727 CGI); (ii) a 40% penalty for bad-faith insufficiency of declared VAT (Article 1729-a CGI); (iii) an 80% penalty for documented fraud involving abusive arrangements, fictitious vessel ownership or forged transit documents (Article 1729-b CGI); and (iv) a 15% accuracy penalty under Article 1788 A CGI for material declaration errors. Criminal tax fraud under Article 1741 CGI carries up to seven years' imprisonment and a EUR 500,000 fine. In addition, the supplier is jointly and severally liable under Article 283-4 CGI for VAT not collected from the client, and the standard correction window (Article L. 169 LPF) of three years extends to ten years in fraud or dissimulation cases, allowing retrospective clawback of already-refunded export VAT with interest from the date of each payment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France is a Eurozone member with full capital and current account convertibility under EU Treaty provisions (TFEU Article 63). There are no binding exchange controls on foreign direct investment, trade in services, or profit remittances. However, all cross-border capital flows (including equity injections, shareholder loans, charter payments, and loan repayments) are subject to periodic statistical reporting to the Banque de France for balance-of-payments compilation. Profit remittance hurdles are generally limited to corporate tax clearance and compliance with anti-money-laundering documentation; there is no prior exchange-control authorization requirement for dividends from an SAS to a non-resident parent.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no statutory obligation in France to surrender export proceeds to the central bank or to convert foreign currency into euros within a specified timeline. Export receipts for offshore vessel chartering may be held in foreign currency in French banks, subject to the general balance-of-payments reporting requirement (Titre V of the French Monetary and Financial Code). Reporting thresholds and timelines apply for transactions exceeding €750,000 (intra-group) or €50,000 for certain statistical declarations under Banque de France’s Regulation 2006-01, as amended. Mandatory conversion ratios are not applicable.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (Bank of France) and the ACPR (Autorité de Contrôle Prudentiel et de Résolution) acting as the national prudential supervisor; the French Treasury (Direction Générale du Trésor) exercises certain foreign exchange and balance-of-payments oversight.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium for domestic French EMI-type fintechs (e.g., Qonto, Shine) that hold an ACPR-registered licence or an electronic money institution passport. Low for offshore digital EMIs domiciled in the UK, US, or non-EU jurisdictions due to lack of European banking licence passporting and potential breach of the French ban on unlicensed credit institution activity (Article L511-5 of the French Monetary and Financial Code). Offshore EMIs cannot open a French statutory escrow account or receive Banque de France balance-of-payments coded wire transfers without a domestic correspondent banking relationship.

Root cause: Ultimate beneficial owner (UBO) opacity in the offshore vessel ownership chain

Defense evidence: Certified UBO registry extract (RCS / Registre des Bénéficiaires Effectifs), corporate chain chart with full ownership percentages, passport copies of all natural persons with ≥25% interest, and apostilled articles of association of each intermediate holding entity.

Root cause: Charter counterparty located in a high-risk jurisdiction or sanctioned-flagged vessel

Defense evidence: Charter party contract with ISPS/MTSA compliance clauses, international Chamber of Shipping beneficial ownership declaration, sanctions screening reports (OFAC/EU/UN lists), valid vessel certificate of registry, commercial registry extracts of charterers, and proof of legitimate vessel operations under ISIC 5222.

Root cause: Unusually high cross-border payment volume through the operational account relative to EBITDA

Defense evidence: Audited financial statements for the prior two years, forecasted cash flow plan with line-item details on charter hire, period maintenance fee schedules, dry-dock cost reserves, and tax residence certificates of all counterparties to demonstrate economic substance.
Source of initial capital injection from offshore tax haven entity

Root cause: Source of initial capital injection from offshore tax haven entity

Defense evidence: Bank statements and wire confirmations showing the original deposit chain, tax opinion from a recognised tax advisor confirming the parent group’s substance, transfer pricing documentation under OECD Chapter II for the intercompany financing, and a declaration to the French customs and indirect taxes authority on the nature of the capital inflow.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

All inbound equity or shareholder-loan proceeds for the French SAS must be routed via a SWIFT MT103 message to a Euro-denominated or foreign-currency account held at a Banque de France regulated bank. The wire must include the correct balance-of-payments purpose code — for equity investment use '3100' (Foreign direct investment in France) or '3300' (Investment in French companies) where pre-2008 nomenclature applies; for shareholder loans use '3301' or '3311' depending on maturity. The bank will request a copy of the SAS’s constitutional documentation and a signed declaration from the treasurer stating the nature of the capital injection. For amounts exceeding €15,000, the bank must also submit a French customs (DGDDI/DGT) cross-border monetary declaration to the Banque de France within 20 business days. Verification includes matching the UBO and legal person identity against the ACPR’s Single Digital Register and ensuring the escrow account is segregated in the bank's books.The primary clearing rail for outbound trade disbursements is the SWIFT MT103 for unattested charter hire and operational payments, with MT700 series for documentary commercial letters of credit issued when vessel procurement or major repair contracts are settled against parallel D/A or L/C terms. For servicing a vessel master’s disbursement account (D/A) in foreign ports, a separate MT101/Multi-Cash service should be established with a correspondent network. All outbound payments must be tagged with the ECB/Eurosystem BOP code appropriate to the underlying transaction (e.g., '2001' for chartering services rendered, '2081' for vessel leasing). E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium for domestic French EMI-type fintechs (e.g., Qonto, Shine) that hold an ACPR-registered licence or an electronic money institution passport. Low for offshore digital EMIs domiciled in the UK, US, or non-EU jurisdictions due to lack of European banking licence passporting and potential breach of the French ban on unlicensed credit institution activity (Article L511-5 of the French Monetary and Financial Code). Offshore EMIs cannot open a French statutory escrow account or receive Banque de France balance-of-payments coded wire transfers without a domestic correspondent banking relationship.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% standard corporate income tax rate. A qualifying small company may obtain a 15% rate on the first EUR 42,500 of taxable profit, subject to statutory ownership and turnover conditions; the excess is taxed at 25%. A 3.3% social contribution may apply to companies with annual turnover of at least EUR 7.63 million and corporate income tax exceeding EUR 763,000, producing a marginal effective rate of approximately 25.825%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

For dividends paid by a French SAS to a non-resident corporate shareholder, the domestic withholding tax rate is generally 25%, subject to treaty relief and statutory exemptions. A qualifying EU or EEA parent may obtain exemption under the French implementation of the Parent-Subsidiary Directive, generally requiring minimum ownership, holding-period and subject-to-tax conditions. Dividends paid to non-resident individuals generally have a different domestic rate, commonly 12.8%, subject to applicable rules.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a financial year ending on a date other than 31 December, the electronic return is generally due within three months after the financial year-end. For a 31 December year-end, the return is generally due by the second business day after 1 May of the following year. The corporate income tax balance is generally due by the 15th day of the fourth month after year-end, or by 15 May for a 31 December year-end. Installments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September and 15 December.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Full transfer-pricing documentation is generally mandatory for a French entity or French permanent establishment where annual pre-tax turnover or gross assets are at least EUR 400 million, or where the entity is controlled by or controls an entity meeting the EUR 400 million threshold, subject to statutory scope rules. A simplified annual transfer-pricing declaration, Form 2257-SD, generally applies from EUR 50 million of annual turnover or gross assets, or where the relevant group relationship meets statutory conditions. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million.

Economic substance scope: France has no single generally applicable economic-substance regulation equivalent to a standalone ESR regime for all companies. Substance is enforced through transfer-pricing rules, permanent-establishment principles, beneficial-ownership requirements, the abuse-of-law doctrine, general anti-abuse rules, anti-hybrid rules, controlled-foreign-company rules, interest limitation rules, withholding-tax anti-conduit principles and principal-purpose testing under treaties. The SAS should maintain French management authority, decision-making evidence, qualified personnel, premises, operational systems, vessel-management functions, risk control, insurance, banking records and commercially supportable contracts. Mere registered-office, nominee-director, back-to-back financing or conduit arrangements are high-risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Returns

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, interest and penalties up to 80% in aggravated cases. Tax fraud may be punished by up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 500,000 fine, with aggravated cases potentially carrying up to seven years' imprisonment and a EUR 3 million fine, subject to the facts and statutory conditions.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto managers who personally participate, authorize or facilitate the fraud may incur criminal liability, tax-manager liability, civil restitution, director disqualification and asset seizure. Travel restrictions or detention may arise through criminal procedure; piercing of the corporate veil is fact-dependent and not automatic.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Taxable income adjustment, withholding tax, late-payment interest, penalties for intentional or fraudulent conduct, documentation penalties and possible denial of deductions or treaty benefits.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil liability for breach of management duties and criminal exposure where fabricated documentation, deliberate tax fraud or money laundering is established. Ordinary transfer-pricing errors do not automatically create personal criminal liability.
Shell Company, Conduit and Beneficial-Ownership Abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty relief, withholding-tax reassessment, abuse-of-law penalties, corporate tax reassessment, interest, possible beneficial-ownership and anti-money-laundering sanctions, and potential criminal liability if concealment is fraudulent.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed where they knowingly implement nominee structures, false residence claims or sham transactions. Corporate separateness normally remains effective absent fraud, abuse of rights or commingling.
Money Laundering and Terrorist-Financing Controls

Statutory sanction: Money laundering may carry up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 375,000 fine, with aggravated forms potentially carrying up to ten years' imprisonment and a EUR 750,000 fine. Legal-entity fines may be multiplied under French criminal law, with confiscation and regulatory consequences also possible.

Director personal liability: Directors, beneficial owners and compliance officers may face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, concealment or serious facilitation. Asset freezing, confiscation, professional bans and travel restrictions may accompany prosecution.
Sanctions, Export-Control and Maritime Counterparty Violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative asset freezes, payment blocking, contract invalidation, substantial criminal fines, imprisonment, confiscation and loss of access to banking and maritime services. Exact sanctions depend on the applicable EU and French restrictive measure.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally prosecuted for intentional violations or knowing facilitation. Corporate authorization does not shield a director who approved prohibited transactions or ignored obvious sanctions indicators.
Undeclared Permanent Establishment, Place of Effective Management or Payroll

Statutory sanction: French corporate tax, VAT, payroll tax and social-security reassessments, interest, late-filing penalties, permanent-establishment adjustments and possible withholding obligations.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil and, in deliberate concealment cases, criminal exposure. A foreign parent's separate legal personality is not a defense to an unreported French permanent establishment.
VAT, Customs and Maritime Documentation Breaches

Statutory sanction: VAT and customs reassessments, repayment of improperly claimed exemptions, interest, administrative fines, seizure, suspension of customs privileges and possible criminal customs penalties.

Director personal liability: Personal liability may arise for intentional customs fraud, false declarations or obstruction. Directors remain responsible for implementing reliable compliance controls and document retention.
Illegal Distribution and Insolvency Misconduct

Statutory sanction: Recovery of unlawful distributions, shareholder repayment, civil damages, management-liability claims, insolvency proceedings and potential criminal penalties for misuse of corporate assets or bankruptcy-related offenses.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil restitution, contribution to insolvency liabilities, management bans and imprisonment for misuse of corporate assets, fraudulent bankruptcy or deliberate continuation of loss-making operations.
Accounting, Record-Keeping and False Documentation

Statutory sanction: Tax assessment based on estimation, rejection of deductions, accounting penalties, criminal sanctions for false documents or obstruction, and adverse evidentiary presumptions.

Director personal liability: Directors and accountants may be personally liable where they ordered, approved or knowingly used false records. Criminal exposure is separate from the SAS's limited liability.
