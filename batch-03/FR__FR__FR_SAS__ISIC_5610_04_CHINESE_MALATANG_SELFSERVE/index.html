Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

French Directorate General of Public Finances (Direction générale des finances publiques, DGFiP), acting through the competent Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE) and the official impots.gouv.fr portal. Business formation and corporate registration information is filed through the mandatory one-stop shop operated by the Institut national de la propriété industrielle (INPI) at formalites.entreprises.gouv.fr.

E-government filing portal

A French SAS must submit its incorporation and beneficial-owner information through the INPI one-stop shop. The filing is transmitted to the competent registries and tax administration. The entity receives a SIREN number for the legal entity, a SIRET number for each establishment, and an APE activity code. The DGFiP then assigns the French intra-Community VAT identification number, generally composed of the country prefix FR, a two-character control key, and the nine-digit SIREN number. The entity must provide constitutional documents, registered-office evidence, identification of legal representatives, beneficial-owner information, business activity details, bank information where requested, and evidence supporting the effective commencement of taxable activities. A foreign entity with no French establishment that carries out transactions requiring French VAT registration must apply to the competent non-established-business tax service of the DGFiP. A non-EU business may generally be required to appoint an accredited French tax representative unless an applicable mutual-assistance arrangement removes that requirement. VAT registration is not itself a substitute for customs registration; an EORI number is required for customs operations involving imports or exports.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A SAS is subject to French corporate income tax by default. It may elect temporary taxation under the personal income tax regime if statutory conditions are satisfied, including qualifying size, ownership, management, activity, and age requirements; the election is generally limited to five financial years and must be notified within the applicable statutory period. The election is not a United States-style check-the-box election. France has no general elective hybrid classification mechanism for a SAS. French anti-hybrid-mismatch rules implement the EU Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive provisions and may deny deductions, impose inclusion, or restrict relief for arrangements involving hybrid instruments or entities.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For a French business, VAT registration is generally required from the commencement of taxable transactions unless the business qualifies for the domestic VAT exemption for small businesses (franchise en base de TVA). The principal franchise threshold for services and mixed activities is generally EUR 37,500 of annual turnover, with a tolerance threshold generally around EUR 41,250, subject to the applicable financial year and legislative amendments. A restaurant business exceeding the applicable threshold, opting voluntarily for VAT, or undertaking transactions requiring a VAT number must register. The EUR 10,000 EU-wide threshold may apply to specified cross-border B2C digital, telecommunications, broadcasting, and electronically supplied services; it is not the ordinary threshold for an establishment-based French restaurant.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods dispatched from France to a destination outside the European Union may qualify for VAT exemption with the right to deduct input VAT if the seller is the exporter or can substantiate the export through customs records and transport evidence. Goods supplied to a customer in another EU Member State may qualify as an exempt intra-Community supply only where the statutory conditions are met, including customer VAT identification, dispatch to another Member State, accurate recapitulative reporting, and proof of transport. Services are not generally treated as exported goods. B2B services supplied to a customer established outside France are ordinarily taxed according to the applicable place-of-supply rule, often with no French VAT where the customer is established outside France, provided the customer status and contractual performance evidence are substantiated. Restaurant and catering services are generally taxed where physically performed, so services physically provided in France ordinarily remain subject to French VAT. Export exemption or non-taxation must be supported contemporaneously; unsupported claims may result in VAT assessment, denial of input-tax recovery, interest, and penalties.

  • French or EU customs export declaration containing the Movement Reference Number (MRN) and electronic customs exit confirmation
  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, customer, goods, value, delivery terms, and applicable VAT treatment
  • Transport evidence, such as bill of lading, airway bill, CMR consignment note, courier record, or equivalent evidence of dispatch outside the European Union
  • Proof of delivery or destination outside the European Union where the customs exit record does not independently establish the complete export chain
  • Contract, purchase order, packing list, and inventory records linking the exported goods to the customs declaration
  • Evidence of the customer's identity and place of establishment where an intra-Community supply or cross-border service rule is relied upon
  • French VAT account records, VAT return support, and recapitulative statements or customs data required for the relevant transaction
  • Inward remittance certificate: not a standard French statutory export-VAT document; bank payment evidence may be retained as corroborative commercial evidence but does not replace customs exit proof

France does not currently operate a universal real-time invoice-clearance system for all domestic transactions. French businesses must issue invoices containing mandatory legal particulars and retain them in an auditable form. Domestic B2B e-invoicing and transaction reporting are being phased in under the French reform: all businesses must be able to receive electronic invoices from 1 September 2026; large and medium-sized businesses must issue domestic B2B electronic invoices from 1 September 2026, while small and micro-enterprises generally become subject to issuance from 1 September 2027. B2C and certain cross-border transaction data will be reported electronically under the e-reporting rules. Invoices will generally be transmitted through an approved private platform or the public invoicing portal under the final implementation framework. Digital signatures, qualified electronic seals, electronic data interchange, or documented audit-trail controls may be used to establish authenticity, integrity, and legibility. Restaurant point-of-sale systems must support compliant receipts, accounting records, and VAT evidence; there is no general French requirement for a separate real-time fiscal stamp on every receipt.

France does not impose a general statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds. Export-VAT exemption requires reliable customs, transport, contractual, and accounting evidence. The DGFiP and customs authorities may deny or claw back the exemption and assess the underlying VAT, applicable late-payment interest, and penalties. A deliberate tax breach may attract a penalty of up to 40 percent of the reassessed tax; fraudulent manoeuvres or abuse of rights may attract up to 80 percent. Failure to provide documents, inaccurate invoices, omitted reporting, or unsupported input-VAT recovery may trigger additional penalties under the applicable Tax Code provisions. Fraudulent VAT refund claims or organized VAT fraud may also create criminal exposure, including fines and imprisonment, in addition to repayment of the improperly obtained refund and accessories.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France is a fully liberalised jurisdiction for both current and capital account transactions under the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (Articles 63-66 TFEU) and the French Monetary and Financial Code (Code Monétaire et Financier). The euro is fully convertible; there are no exchange-control approvals for current-account payments, dividends, royalty remittances, or capital movements. Profit remittance from the French SAS is unrestricted in law, subject only to standard corporate formalities (audited accounts where applicable, shareholder approval of dividends, or contractual royalty documentation) and to mandatory statistical reporting to the Banque de France for the balance of payments. Non-resident shareholders may freely receive dividends; no special profit-transfer authorisation is required. The only constraints are general EU/UN/OFAC sanctions compliance and the obligation to declare cross-border transactions exceeding regulatory thresholds for balance-of-payments statistics.

Export proceeds surrender rules

None applicable. France imposes no surrender, repatriation, or mandatory conversion obligation on export or trade receivables. Export proceeds may be retained in foreign-currency accounts with authorised banks without any statutory conversion ratio. However, the Banque de France requires a monthly 'external trade declaration' (declaration des échanges de biens) for goods exports and a 'declaration de transfert de fonds' for all cross-border monetary flows (transfers between residents and non-residents) exceeding €25,000 per transaction or aggregation, for statistical purposes only; this does not constitute a surrender or conversion mandate.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (French central bank, executing ESCB monetary policy) and the Direction Générale du Trésor (French Treasury, acting as the Foreign Exchange Administration under the Ministry of Economy and Finance) for external financial regulations, together with the ACPR (Autorité de Contrôle Prudentiel et de Résolution) for banking supervision

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium-to-High for licensed EU/UK EMIs; Low for offshore or unregulated digital payment providers. Under the French transposition of PSD2, a licensed Electronic Money Institution (EMI) with a full EEA passport can lawfully provide payment accounts, currency conversion, and cross-border transfers to the French SAS without a banking licence. French-licensed neobanks (e.g., Qonto, Shine, Lydia Solutions) and major EEA EMIs (e.g., Ebury, WorldFirst) are viable for operational treasury, royalty collections via virtual IBANs, and supplier disbursements. However, a capital escrow account for substantiating share capital and receiving inbound FDI should be held at a full credit institution, not an EMI, given the stricter repayment-guarantee and segregation constraints under French banking law. Offshore EMIs without an ACPR licence or EEA passport cannot provide regulated payment services from within France and may not be used as the primary treasury rail.

Economic substance scope: France applies the OECD/G20 BEPS measures, including the Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive (ATAD), requiring genuine economic substance for treaty benefits and to avoid 'abus de droit'. An SAS must demonstrate actual management activity, payroll, and operational decision-making in France; absence may trigger tax residency reclassification or denial of treaty relief.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Fraude Fiscale)

Statutory sanction: Civil penalty of 40% to 80% of the evaded tax, plus late payment interest; criminal fines up to €500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years for corporate officers.

Director personal liability: Directors face personal liability for the full tax debt if they committed 'faute de gestion' or acted in bad faith; travel bans and criminal imprisonment may be imposed. The corporate veil can be pierced for fictitious or fraudulent inter-company transactions.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Reassessment of taxable income at arm’s length, penalty of 5% of the reassessed amount (or 10% if no documentation), plus interest at 0.2% monthly.

Director personal liability: The president and legal representative may be ordered to pay the additional tax if they controlled the pricing decisions and knowingly departed from correct transfer pricing rules, leading to a judicial conviction for 'abus de biens sociaux'.
Shell Company / Economic Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, immediate application of the 25% dividend withholding tax without reduction; fines up to €50,000 for failure to meet substance guidelines; reinstatement of tax under anti-abuse rules.

Director personal liability: Directors personally liable for the difference between the treaty rate and statutory rate if they certified false statements; criminal penalties for misuse of corporate assets may apply.
AML / Know-Your-Customer Breach

Statutory sanction: Administrative fines up to €1 million per violation for SARL/SAS entities; non-compliance with beneficial ownership reporting can result in €5,000 to €25,000 penalties for the legal entity and directors separately.

Director personal liability: Criminal liability for 'blanchiment' (money laundering) if directors deliberately circumvent AML controls to facilitate franchisee fraud; imprisonment up to 5 years and personal asset forfeiture.
