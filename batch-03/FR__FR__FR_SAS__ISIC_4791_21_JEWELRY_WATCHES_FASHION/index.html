Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

French Directorate-General of Public Finance (Direction générale des finances publiques, DGFiP), acting through the competent Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE) and the Foreign Businesses Tax Service (Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères, SIEE) where applicable. The principal online portal is impots.gouv.fr. Corporate formation and initial enterprise identification filings are generally submitted through the Guichet unique operated by the Institut National de la Propriété Industrielle (INPI), with enterprise identifiers maintained through the national SIRENE system.

E-government filing portal

A French SAS must be incorporated through the Guichet unique, after which the entity receives a SIREN number for the legal entity and a SIRET number for each establishment. The entity must register with the competent SIE and provide its incorporation documents, articles of association, registered-office evidence, beneficial-owner information, business activity description, bank details, and expected VAT transactions. The SIE assigns or confirms the corporate income tax and VAT accounts. The French VAT number is generally formatted as FR followed by a two-digit control key and the nine-digit SIREN. A foreign entity without a French establishment must register directly with the SIEE where it makes taxable French supplies, unless the applicable reverse-charge, OSS, import-VAT, or marketplace rules eliminate the registration obligation. Non-EU businesses generally require a French fiscal representative for VAT compliance unless an applicable mutual-assistance arrangement or statutory exception applies. An EORI number must be obtained from French Customs for customs declarations and import or export activity. VAT registration does not replace customs, OSS, Intrastat, consumer-law, or payment-service registrations where separately applicable.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A SAS is ordinarily subject to French corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés, IS) at entity level. It may, subject to statutory eligibility conditions, elect temporarily for the partnership-style regime under Article 239 bis AB of the French Tax Code, generally for a maximum period of five financial years; the election is not a general United States-style check-the-box election. Certain qualifying family companies may elect a partnership regime under the applicable French tax rules. France has no general entity-classification check-the-box system for SAS entities and no election that generally converts a SAS into a permanent pass-through entity. Hybrid-mismatch and anti-hybrid rules may deny deductions or impose inclusion consequences, but they are not elective classification mechanisms.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For a French-established business, there is no general VAT registration threshold that permits taxable supplies without VAT once the business is outside the French franchise en base regime. The principal domestic franchise threshold for commercial sales, including ordinary jewelry, watches, and fashion goods, is EUR 85,000 of annual turnover, subject to the applicable tolerance threshold and statutory year-specific adjustments. A business exceeding the relevant franchise limits becomes liable for VAT under the applicable effective-date rules. A non-established business generally has no equivalent French turnover threshold and may be required to register and account for VAT from its first taxable French transaction, subject to reverse charge, import arrangements, marketplace provisions, and the One Stop Shop (OSS). For intra-EU distance sales to consumers, the EU-wide EUR 10,000 threshold may apply, with VAT reportable through OSS or under local registrations after the threshold is exceeded.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods physically dispatched or transported from France to a destination outside the European Union may qualify for VAT exemption with the right to deduct, commonly described commercially as export zero-rating, provided the supplier is the exporter or acts through an authorized intermediary and can prove exit from the EU. Required evidence normally includes the export declaration, MRN or equivalent customs exit confirmation, transport documentation, commercial invoice, and records linking the goods, buyer, shipment, and payment. Goods supplied for export by a qualifying intermediary may also qualify where the statutory conditions are met. Services are not automatically zero-rated merely because the customer is foreign. For B2B services, the place-of-supply rule generally places taxation at the customer’s establishment and may result in an out-of-scope supply or customer reverse charge. Specific exemptions or place-of-supply rules apply to services directly connected with exports, international transport, and certain financial or intermediary services. Jewelry, watches, and fashion goods remain subject to the ordinary VAT rules unless a specific exemption applies.

  • French or EU export customs declaration containing the applicable customs procedure and shipment data
  • MRN and customs office exit confirmation or equivalent electronic proof that the goods left the European Union
  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, customer, goods, values, delivery terms, and VAT treatment
  • Packing list and transport documentation, such as bill of lading, air waybill, CMR, or courier manifest
  • Proof linking the export declaration to the specific goods and invoice, including product descriptions, quantities, serial numbers, or shipment references where relevant
  • Evidence of delivery or destination outside the European Union where required by the facts or requested during audit
  • Accounting records and VAT returns supporting the exempt export with right to deduct
  • Payment and order records supporting the commercial transaction; an inward remittance certificate is not generally a French statutory export-VAT document, although bank evidence may support transaction substantiation

France does not currently operate a universal real-time VAT invoice-clearance system for all private-sector transactions. Domestic B2B electronic invoicing and transaction-data e-reporting are being introduced under the French reform timetable: all businesses must be able to receive compliant electronic invoices from 1 September 2026; large and medium-sized businesses must issue domestic B2B electronic invoices from 1 September 2026, and smaller businesses from 1 September 2027, subject to legally adopted transition rules. Invoices will generally pass through an approved private platform or the public invoicing portal, with transaction and payment data reported to the tax administration. B2C transactions, exports, intra-EU supplies, and transactions outside the French domestic B2B scope may trigger separate e-reporting obligations rather than clearance. Public-sector invoices are submitted through Chorus Pro. French invoices must satisfy statutory content, numbering, authenticity, integrity, storage, and VAT recordkeeping requirements; there is currently no general requirement for a digital tax stamp on every invoice.

France does not impose a general statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline of 180 days for export proceeds. Export VAT exemption may be reassessed if customs exit cannot be proven, the goods did not leave the EU, the documentation is inconsistent, the customer or intermediary conditions are not met, or the transaction is fictitious. The reassessment may include unpaid VAT, late-payment interest, and applicable penalties. A 40% penalty may apply for deliberate failure or deliberate inaccuracies, and an 80% penalty may apply in cases involving fraudulent maneuvers or abuse of rights, subject to statutory conditions and procedural safeguards. Late-payment interest is generally calculated under the applicable French tax rate, commonly 0.20% per month, subject to legislative changes. Incorrect customs declarations may generate customs duties, import VAT, customs interest, administrative fines, and seizure or forfeiture consequences under the Union Customs Code and French customs law. Serious tax fraud may also result in criminal prosecution, including imprisonment and substantial fines. Refund claims may be delayed, reduced, or denied where the taxable person cannot demonstrate the underlying transactions, valid invoices, payment records, export exit, or entitlement to input-VAT deduction.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The euro is fully convertible for ordinary current-account and capital-account transactions. A French SAS may generally receive, hold, convert, and remit euros and foreign currencies, subject to bank anti-money-laundering, counter-terrorist-financing, sanctions, beneficial-ownership, tax, and payment-transparency controls. Dividends, interest, service fees, royalties, loan repayments, and liquidation proceeds may generally be remitted to non-residents without a general French withholding authorization or foreign-exchange approval, provided that corporate-law approvals, distributable-profit tests, transfer-pricing requirements, withholding-tax analysis, treaty documentation, accounting records, and beneficial-ownership evidence are satisfied. Cross-border transactions with related parties must be commercially justified and supported by written agreements, arm's-length pricing, transfer-pricing documentation where applicable, and evidence of actual performance. Banks may delay or reject transactions involving incomplete source-of-funds evidence, high-risk jurisdictions, sanctioned parties, unusual payment chains, cash-intensive activity, inconsistent invoices, third-party settlement, or unexplained beneficial-ownership structures. Cash payments and cash-equivalent transactions are subject to French limits and reporting obligations, including restrictions applicable to payments by or to French tax residents and professional traders. Import and export payments must also remain consistent with customs declarations, VAT records, commercial invoices, transport documents, and applicable sanctions controls.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France has no general statutory requirement for an exporter to surrender export proceeds to the Banque de France, no general mandatory conversion ratio, and no general statutory deadline requiring conversion of foreign-currency export receipts into euros. Export proceeds may generally be retained in foreign-currency accounts or converted at the holder's discretion, subject to the bank's account terms, accounting requirements, tax reporting, customs and VAT substantiation, sanctions screening, and anti-money-laundering controls. The SAS should maintain a transaction-level reconciliation between sales invoices, customs declarations, payment confirmations, refunds, chargebacks, marketplace settlements, and VAT returns. Any exceptional reporting, blocking, licensing, or asset-freezing obligation arising from EU or French sanctions, capital controls imposed during an emergency, or a specific regulated transaction must be followed separately.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France, operating within the Eurosystem under the European Central Bank monetary framework. France has no general exchange-control authority requiring routine approval for ordinary commercial foreign-exchange transactions; applicable exchange-control powers and exceptional measures derive principally from the French Monetary and Financial Code and European Union law, with the French Treasury and Ministry for the Economy responsible for certain restrictive-measure and sanctions matters.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium for payment collection and operational disbursements, but not a complete substitute for a French bank. EU-authorized electronic-money institutions and payment institutions may provide IBAN accounts, payment initiation, card acquiring, foreign-exchange conversion, and merchant settlement under their authorization and passporting status. An electronic-money account is not a bank deposit and generally does not provide deposit-guarantee protection; customer funds are safeguarded under the applicable authorization regime. Offshore digital EMIs without an EU authorization, French passporting basis, or acceptable correspondent arrangement should not be used for core corporate treasury. Stripe and PayPal may be used for customer collections only after merchant underwriting, with settlement to an account accepted by the provider. The SAS should retain a regulated bank account for capitalization, tax payments, payroll, supplier settlements, reserve liquidity, refunds, chargebacks, and continuity. Fintech onboarding may be restricted for jewelry, watches, luxury goods, high-value goods, cross-border sales, elevated refund rates, marketplace activity, crypto exposure, or delivery to high-risk jurisdictions.

For paid-in SAS capital, route funds to the designated capital-deposit account before incorporation or capital increase, using the exact legal name of the SAS or subscriber and the bank's prescribed capital-deposit reference. Obtain the deposit certificate and transfer the funds to the operational account only after registration and release procedures are completed. For shareholder loans or additional funding, use a written loan, contribution, or subscription agreement and transfer from an account held in the funder's own name. Payment instructions should state the precise purpose, such as 'paid-in share capital', 'shareholder loan pursuant to agreement dated [date]', or 'equity contribution', and should not describe an equity injection as trade revenue. Maintain SWIFT or SEPA payment confirmation, remitter identity, ownership evidence, board or shareholder approval, source-of-funds records, general-ledger entries, and intercompany documentation. Use SEPA Credit Transfer for eligible euro payments and SWIFT cross-border wires for non-SEPA currencies or jurisdictions. No general Banque de France pre-approval or French foreign-exchange registration is required for ordinary inbound capital, but banks may apply enhanced due diligence and sanctions screening.Use SEPA Credit Transfer for eligible euro supplier payments and SWIFT MT103 or equivalent ISO 20022 customer-credit-transfer messaging for non-SEPA payments. Payment files should contain accurate originator, beneficiary, bank, invoice, purchase-order, customs, and purpose information. For higher-value, new-counterparty, or jurisdictionally sensitive transactions, use a commercial documentary letter of credit, standby letter of credit, documentary collection, or bank-guaranteed payment where commercially appropriate. A commercial LC should specify issuing and advising banks, applicant, beneficiary, amount, currency, Incoterms, shipment dates, documents required, inspection requirements, discrepancy rules, sanctions clauses, and governing rules such as UCP 600 where selected. Reconcile every payment to the supplier invoice, purchase order, goods-receipt or transport evidence, customs declaration, and inventory record. Avoid third-party settlement unless contractually justified, approved under the bank's policy, and supported by a complete payment-chain explanation. For card and wallet collections, use Stripe or PayPal only under approved merchant terms, maintain settlement and reserve reconciliations, and do not treat processor balances as unrestricted bank liquidity. Monitor SEPA Instant, card-network, acquiring, chargeback, refund, and reserve risks separately.Deliverable forward exchange contracts for forecast or committed receivables and payables, subject to bank credit limits, documented exposure, suitability or appropriateness controls, confirmation procedures, and a board-approved hedging policy.Non-deliverable forwards may be used for currencies or jurisdictions where delivery is restricted or impractical, provided the counterparty is authorized, the transaction is legally permitted in the relevant jurisdiction, settlement currency and fixing source are documented, and sanctions and local-control rules are observed.Currency swaps and cross-currency swaps may be used for recurring or longer-term currency funding exposures, subject to negotiated master documentation, collateral or margin terms, valuation, counterparty-credit limits, and applicable European Market Infrastructure Regulation reporting, clearing, risk-mitigation, and trade-repository requirements.FX options, collars, and option-based structures may be used only where the SAS understands premium, strike, barrier, settlement, early-termination, and contingent-liability risks; complex or leveraged products should require enhanced board approval.Spot foreign-exchange transactions may be used for ordinary settlement and liquidity conversion. Hedging should be linked to identifiable commercial exposure, documented at inception, reconciled to underlying invoices or forecasts, and reviewed for accounting, tax, transfer-pricing, and financial-reporting treatment. The SAS should not engage in unauthorized financial-services activity, speculative dealing unrelated to its business, or transactions with unregulated or sanctioned counterparties.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium for payment collection and operational disbursements, but not a complete substitute for a French bank. EU-authorized electronic-money institutions and payment institutions may provide IBAN accounts, payment initiation, card acquiring, foreign-exchange conversion, and merchant settlement under their authorization and passporting status. An electronic-money account is not a bank deposit and generally does not provide deposit-guarantee protection; customer funds are safeguarded under the applicable authorization regime. Offshore digital EMIs without an EU authorization, French passporting basis, or acceptable correspondent arrangement should not be used for core corporate treasury. Stripe and PayPal may be used for customer collections only after merchant underwriting, with settlement to an account accepted by the provider. The SAS should retain a regulated bank account for capitalization, tax payments, payroll, supplier settlements, reserve liquidity, refunds, chargebacks, and continuity. Fintech onboarding may be restricted for jewelry, watches, luxury goods, high-value goods, cross-border sales, elevated refund rates, marketplace activity, crypto exposure, or delivery to high-risk jurisdictions.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% standard corporate income tax under Article 219 of the French General Tax Code, plus a reduced 15% rate on the first EUR 42,000 of taxable profit for qualifying SMEs

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Article 119 bis 2 of the French General Tax Code; 75% for distributions to non-cooperative States; 0% if the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive applies to a qualifying EU/EEA parent holding at least 5% for two years

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: EUR 400,000,000 consolidated gross turnover or gross total assets of a qualifying group with at least 50% direct or indirect French/foreign cross-border ownership links and with the French entity in the scope; below the threshold the French entity must still retain sufficient transfer pricing evidence to demonstrate arm's-length pricing

Economic substance scope: France does not apply offshore Economic Substance Regulations as in OFC regimes; however, the substance-over-form principle, abuse of law under Article L.64 of the French Tax Procedures Book, and ATAD anti-abuse rules require a French SAS to carry on genuine economic activity in France, including French employees, real inventory, actual management decisions, local bank accounts, and substantive commercial risk control

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Return

Statutory sanction: Tax penalties of 40% to 80% of evaded tax, 0.20% monthly interest, criminal fine up to EUR 500,000 or double the tax evaded, imprisonment up to five years, confiscation of assets and proceeds

Director personal liability: SAS President and directors may be held personally liable under Article L.267 of the French Tax Procedures Book for corporate tax debts where serious misconduct and bad faith are shown; courts may impose management bans, travel restrictions, and imprisonment
Artificial Arrangement / Shell Company

Statutory sanction: Reassessment under the abuse-of-law procedure with an 80% penalty in case of abuse; denial of treaty benefits; recharacterization of the French structure as a permanent establishment of the foreign parent

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly maintain a fictitious entity may be jointly and severally liable for reassessed tax, interest and penalties; judicial management disqualification and sequestration of personal assets are possible
Transfer Pricing and Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Transfer pricing adjustment by the French tax administration; penalty of 5% of reassessed profits under Article 1735 ter of the French General Tax Code, increased to 10% in abuse cases; additional fixed penalties for documents not produced after formal notice; risk of economic double taxation

Director personal liability: Directors who sign false certificates, approve circular transactions, or fail to prevent gross negligence may be ordered to pay the resulting corporate liabilities, with personal bankruptcy measures if the company is insolvent
AML / Cash and Payment Compliance

Statutory sanction: Criminal fines up to EUR 500,000, confiscation of goods, administrative sanctions by Tracfin/AMF/customs authorities, VAT and customs penalties up to 80%, and possible termination of payment processing services by Stripe or PayPal

Director personal liability: Directors or officers who personally direct or tolerate AML/cash-payment violations incur individual criminal liability, imprisonment up to five years, civil restitution, and, where corporate assets are dissipated, attachment of personal assets
