Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction générale des finances publiques (DGFiP), including the relevant Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE) and, for non-established businesses, the Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères (SIEE). Official portal: impots.gouv.fr. Business registration data and SIREN/SIRET identifiers are administered through the national business formalities portal (Guichet unique des formalités des entreprises), operated by the Institut national de la propriété industrielle (INPI), with INSEE assigning the SIREN and SIRET identifiers.

E-government filing portal

1. Incorporate the SAS and file the formation formalities through the Guichet unique; the filing generates the registration record and enables INSEE allocation of the SIREN and establishment-level SIRET. 2. Obtain the French tax registration notice and identify the competent SIE based on the registered office or establishment. 3. Register for corporate income tax and applicable VAT obligations with the SIE through the professional tax account on impots.gouv.fr. 4. Request or validate the French intra-Community VAT number, generally formatted as FR plus a two-character control key and the nine-digit SIREN. 5. A foreign entity without a French establishment must register with the SIEE where it carries out taxable transactions requiring French VAT compliance; it must provide constitutional documents, foreign tax-registration evidence, proof of activity, identification of representatives, bank details where requested, and transaction or contract evidence. 6. For intra-Community transactions, validate counterpart VAT numbers through the VIES system and file the applicable European sales lists and VAT returns. 7. Maintain separate records for corporate income tax, VAT, customs, payroll, and regulated pharmaceutical-logistics obligations. A French SAS generally does not use a separate federal-style TIN; the SIREN identifies the legal entity and the French VAT number identifies it for VAT purposes.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A SAS is generally subject to French corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés) by default. Subject to statutory eligibility conditions, certain SAS entities may elect temporary taxation under the personal income tax regime (impôt sur le revenu) for up to five financial years, including requirements relating to size, ownership, activity, age, and shareholder participation. This is not a general United States-style check-the-box election. France does not provide a broad entity-classification election allowing an ordinary SAS to elect unrestricted pass-through treatment. Hybrid mismatch, anti-hybrid, controlled foreign company, interest limitation, and treaty anti-abuse rules may restrict the tax effect of contractual or cross-border classification differences.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No single general VAT-registration threshold applies to a French SAS carrying out taxable transactions outside the small-business franchise. The principal French domestic franchise thresholds are generally EUR 85,000 for supplies of goods and accommodation and EUR 37,500 for services, with increased thresholds generally of EUR 93,500 and EUR 41,250 respectively, subject to statutory conditions and legislative changes. A business exceeding the applicable threshold loses the franchise and becomes liable under the relevant rules. A non-established business may be required to register and account for French VAT from its first taxable French transaction, subject to place-of-supply rules, reverse charge, special schemes, and the applicable EU small-business scheme.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods from France to destinations outside the European Union may qualify for VAT exemption with the right to deduct input VAT if the exporter can substantiate that the goods physically left the EU customs territory and that the exporter or an authorized intermediary completed the export formalities. Required evidence normally includes the electronic customs export record and exit result, export MRN, commercial invoice, transport documentation, and proof linking the goods to the transaction. Intra-Community supplies of goods may qualify for the French VAT exemption where the customer is VAT-identified in another EU Member State, the goods are transported from France to that Member State, and the supplier holds the required customer VAT number and transport evidence. Freight and logistics services require separate place-of-supply analysis. B2B services are generally supplied where the business customer is established, with customer-side reverse charge where applicable; they are not automatically treated as exports merely because the customer is foreign. Special rules apply to transport, intermediary, warehousing, import-related, and services connected with goods. A French VAT refund requires valid purchase invoices, a deductible business purpose, correct VAT invoicing, and compliance with reporting and limitation rules.

  • Electronic export customs declaration and export Movement Reference Number (MRN)
  • Customs exit confirmation or equivalent electronic proof that the goods exited the European Union customs territory
  • Commercial invoice identifying the exporter, customer, goods, value, delivery terms, and VAT treatment
  • Packing list and transport documentation, including bill of lading, airway bill, consignment note, or equivalent freight evidence
  • Proof linking the exported goods to the invoice and shipment, including carrier or freight-forwarder records
  • Export accompanying document or other customs movement evidence where applicable
  • Evidence of the customer's location and, for intra-Community supplies, the customer's valid EU VAT identification number
  • VAT return, accounting ledger, and reconciliation supporting the exemption or deduction claim
  • Inward remittance evidence where relevant to commercial substantiation; France generally does not impose a universal requirement for an inward remittance certificate as a condition of export VAT exemption

France is implementing mandatory electronic invoicing and electronic transaction reporting for businesses established in France. All businesses must be able to receive electronic invoices from 1 September 2026; issuance obligations apply progressively, with large and medium-sized businesses from 1 September 2026 and small and micro-businesses generally from 1 September 2027, subject to legislative or regulatory amendments. Domestic B2B invoices must generally pass through an approved private platform, known as a Plateforme de dématérialisation partenaire (PDP), or another legally permitted public infrastructure arrangement. France does not generally operate a universal real-time pre-clearance or digital-stamp system for ordinary domestic invoices. Transaction data and payment-status data may be reported electronically to the tax administration under e-reporting rules, particularly for certain international and B2C transactions. Public-sector invoices are generally processed through Chorus Pro. VAT invoices must contain mandatory legal particulars, support the applicable VAT treatment, and be retained for the statutory period.

France has no general statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline applicable to ordinary commercial export proceeds. Export VAT exemption or deduction may be denied or clawed back where the taxpayer cannot prove the physical export, customer status, transport, customs exit, or correct place of supply. The DGFiP may assess unpaid VAT, statutory late-payment interest generally calculated at 0.20% per month, and penalties generally ranging from 10% for certain filing or payment failures to 40% for deliberate breaches and up to 80% for fraudulent maneuvers or abusive concealment, subject to the applicable procedure and facts. Incorrect VAT refunds may be recovered with interest and penalties. Serious intentional tax fraud can generate criminal exposure, including imprisonment and substantial fines under the French Tax Code. False customs declarations or misuse of export evidence may also trigger customs recovery, administrative penalties, seizure, loss of customs simplifications, and criminal sanctions. The taxpayer must preserve accounting, customs, transport, invoice, and electronic evidence for the applicable statutory audit period, commonly at least six years for VAT accounting records.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France is in the euro area and the euro is freely convertible for current-account and capital-account transactions, subject to EU and French anti-money-laundering, counter-terrorist-financing, sanctions, tax, customs, prudential, and statistical-reporting requirements. A French SAS may generally receive foreign investment, hold foreign-currency accounts, purchase foreign currency, pay non-residents, repatriate profits, make intercompany payments, and remit dividends without a general exchange-control approval. Dividends require distributable profits, valid corporate approval, withholding-tax analysis, beneficial-owner documentation, and applicable treaty or EU relief. Interest, royalties, management fees, and other related-party payments require contractual support, transfer-pricing evidence, commercial substance, withholding-tax analysis, and arm's-length pricing. Banks may require transaction-purpose evidence, ultimate-beneficial-owner information, sanctions screening, tax-residence forms, and supporting invoices before executing or releasing funds. Certain foreign investments in protected French sectors may be subject to prior French foreign-investment authorization; ordinary GDP pharmaceutical freight and logistics activities are not automatically within a protected sector, but regulated healthcare, critical infrastructure, data, or strategic-supply-chain activities require a specific review. Cross-border cash movements of EUR 10,000 or more carried by individuals entering or leaving the European Union require customs declaration, and cash transported within France may be subject to declaration and evidentiary requirements.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France has no general statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no universal deadline requiring exporters to convert foreign-currency receipts into euros. Export proceeds may generally remain in foreign currency or be converted at the company's discretion, subject to the bank's account terms, sanctions controls, AML controls, accounting rules, VAT and customs documentation, and applicable statistical or balance-of-payments reporting. Export declarations, transport records, customer invoices, proof of delivery, and customs evidence should be retained to substantiate the underlying trade. Banks may request prompt receipt allocation and supporting documents under risk-based transaction monitoring, but such bank procedures are not a statutory surrender rule.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France, acting within the Eurosystem under the European Central Bank; foreign-exchange policy is principally governed by European Union law, French financial legislation, the French Treasury, the Autorité de contrôle prudentiel et de résolution, and the Autorité des marchés financiers.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium to High for payment accounts, collection, foreign-exchange conversion, cards, and SEPA transfers, provided the provider is an EU-authorized electronic-money institution or payment institution and is properly passported or otherwise authorized to provide services in France. An offshore digital EMI without EU authorization, French passporting, or an appropriately regulated correspondent structure cannot lawfully provide unrestricted French payment-account services. An EMI account is not a substitute for a French bank account where a capital deposit certificate, statutory escrow, lender covenant, regulated client-money structure, or bank-specific verification is required. EMI safeguarding, payment limits, settlement cut-offs, de-risking, account closure, and limited credit or trade-finance capabilities should be assessed before primary reliance. Client funds must not be commingled with company operating funds where the provider's terms or applicable safeguarding rules prohibit it.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route paid-in capital to the designated French bank capital account or capital escrow account from an account held in the name of the subscribing shareholder or investor at a regulated financial institution. Use a payment reference identifying the French SAS, subscription or shareholder-loan instrument, investor name, and capital or funding purpose. Provide the bank with the executed subscription agreement, draft or final articles, shareholder resolution, beneficial-owner information, certificate of incorporation or registration evidence, origin-of-funds documents, and sender-account proof. After registration and release of funds, reconcile the bank certificate, share issuance, accounting entry, and corporate records. For shareholder loans or intercompany funding, use a documented agreement, arm's-length terms, transfer-pricing support, withholding-tax analysis, and a clear principal-versus-interest allocation. Do not route capital through personal accounts, unrelated third parties, anonymous payment intermediaries, or unapproved fintech wallets. Incoming international transfers may use SWIFT MT103; EUR transfers within SEPA should use the relevant SEPA credit-transfer format and include accurate IBAN, BIC where required, originator, beneficiary, and purpose data. Maintain evidence for bank AML review, French accounting, tax, audit, and any applicable statistical reporting.Use SEPA Credit Transfer or SEPA Instant Credit Transfer for eligible euro suppliers and SWIFT MT103 for non-SEPA or foreign-currency settlements. Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% standard corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés, IS).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Generally 25% on dividends paid by a French company to a non-resident corporate shareholder, subject to statutory exemptions, EU law, and an applicable tax treaty. A qualifying EU parent company may obtain an exemption under the French implementation of the Parent-Subsidiary Directive, subject to ownership, holding-period, tax-status, anti-abuse, and documentation conditions. Domestic law may also provide relief for qualifying non-EU corporate parents meeting statutory conditions.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: French full transfer-pricing documentation generally applies where the French entity or relevant group entity has annual turnover excluding tax or gross assets of at least EUR 150 million, or meets specified ownership or control links with an entity meeting the threshold. The simplified annual declaration obligation under Form 2257-SD has a comparable EUR 150 million threshold and qualifying group-link triggers. Master-file requirements under Article 223 quinquies C apply to qualifying groups meeting statutory conditions, generally including a EUR 750 million consolidated revenue threshold for the relevant country-by-country reporting framework. Thresholds and entity scope must be tested under the applicable provisions for the fiscal year.

Economic substance scope: France does not have a general standalone regime formally called Economic Substance Regulations equivalent to certain offshore regimes. French tax authorities may nevertheless challenge arrangements lacking commercial rationale or economic reality under domestic anti-abuse doctrines, including abuse of law, the principal-purpose test where applicable, transfer-pricing rules, permanent-establishment principles, and beneficial-ownership requirements. A French GDP clinical-logistics business should maintain evidence of real French personnel, premises, decision-making, regulated operational capacity, customer and supplier functions, and risk control consistent with its reported profits.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate tax evasion and fraudulent filing

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessments, late-payment interest, and surcharges may apply. Individual tax fraud can carry up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 500,000 fine; aggravated cases can carry up to seven years and a EUR 3 million fine, subject to statutory conditions. Corporate criminal fines and additional penalties may apply, with amounts depending on the offence and the applicable legal provisions.

Director personal liability: Directors may face individual criminal liability where they personally participate in, direct, or knowingly facilitate the offence. Tax debt is not automatically transferred to directors, but statutory director-liability mechanisms may apply for fraudulent conduct or serious and repeated tax non-compliance. Imprisonment or travel restrictions require a legal basis and competent authority action; they are not automatic consequences of an assessment.
Transfer-pricing misstatement and inadequate documentation

Statutory sanction: Tax adjustments, interest, and applicable surcharges may result. Failure to provide required transfer-pricing documentation may trigger a specific fine, generally the greater of EUR 10,000 or 0.5% of the relevant transactions, subject to statutory limits and conditions; additional consequences may apply for understated tax or incomplete responses.

Director personal liability: Directors may be exposed for personal participation in fraud or other separately established misconduct. Routine transfer-pricing adjustments do not by themselves pierce the corporate veil or automatically create personal liability.
Concealed permanent establishment or artificial profit allocation

Statutory sanction: Unreported French taxable profits may be assessed with interest and applicable surcharges. Criminal sanctions require proof of a separately defined offence, such as fraud; an incorrect treaty or permanent-establishment position alone does not automatically constitute a criminal offence.

Director personal liability: Personal liability may arise only under applicable statutory or criminal rules based on the director's conduct. It is not automatic merely because a permanent establishment is found.
Improper dividend, royalty, or interest withholding

Statutory sanction: The French payer may be assessed for unpaid withholding tax, interest, and applicable surcharges. The precise consequences depend on the payment type, the recipient's status, and the failure involved.

Director personal liability: The company is ordinarily the withholding agent. A director may face personal or criminal consequences only where statutory conditions for personal liability or participation in an offence are met.
Fraudulent company dissolution or asset diversion

Statutory sanction: Civil recovery, insolvency proceedings, tax reassessment, and criminal penalties may apply depending on the conduct. French law does not impose an automatic general travel ban or imprisonment solely for unpaid corporate tax.

Director personal liability: A director may face civil liability under applicable tax, company, or insolvency law where statutory conditions are met, including fraudulent conduct or serious and repeated breaches. Veil-piercing, restitution, restrictions on management, or criminal punishment require a specific legal basis and competent decision.
GDP, customs, and pharmaceutical logistics non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Regulatory measures may include inspections, corrective orders, suspension or withdrawal of authorization, product or shipment holds, customs reassessments, administrative fines, and criminal sanctions where the relevant offence is established. Tax penalties may additionally apply to false customs or accounting records.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for offences they commit or knowingly facilitate, and may face separate regulatory or criminal consequences under applicable pharmaceutical, customs, and company law. A license breach does not automatically make directors liable for all corporate debts.
Anti-money-laundering and sanctions violations

Statutory sanction: Penalties depend on the specific AML, sanctions, or criminal-law breach and may include substantial fines, confiscation, regulatory restrictions, and imprisonment for criminal offences. A general corporate AML reporting duty should not be inferred without confirming that the SAS is a regulated obliged entity or otherwise subject to the relevant requirement.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal criminal or regulatory liability for their own participation, authorization, or legally relevant failure to act. Travel restrictions and imprisonment are not automatic and require an applicable legal basis and procedure.
