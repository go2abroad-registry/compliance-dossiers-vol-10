Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

The SAS has no legal minimum capital; the articles freely fix the amount. Where cash contributions are made, at least 50% (or 100% if so provided in the articles) must be deposited irrevocably in a blocked escrow account (compte bloqué) with a French bank or the Caisse des Dépôts et Consignations; the funds are released to the company only upon issuance of the Extrait Kbis. In-kind contributions are due in full at incorporation and must be valued in the articles.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety certification issued by the local fire and rescue service (SDIS) or the commission de sécurité for premises accommodating more than 50 staff members or classified as ERP; structural load inspection certificate for storage racks and mezzanines; occupational workplace clearance delivered by the DREETS labor inspectorate following the submission of the DUERP (workplace risk assessment), the medical-service attestation (médecine du travail) of staff fitness, and the electrical-installation conformity check.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction Générale des Finances Publiques (DGFiP); online portal: www.impots.gouv.fr; corporate formalities and VAT registration via the Guichet Unique (formalites.entreprises.gouv.fr).

E-government filing portal

For a French SAS, incorporate through the Guichet Unique by filing a standard notice (form M0) with the Centre de Formalités des Entreprises; obtain SIREN/SIRET numbers from the French business register (INSEE). After incorporation, the DGFiP assigns an intra-Community VAT number. A non-established foreign entity making taxable supplies in France must apply directly to the Service des Impôts des Entreprises (SIE) using form 1432-SD (or the non-EU VAT registration form) and appoint an individual tax representative if not EU-established. Identification is subject to verification of legal existence, business activity, and place of effective nexus.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

France does not operate a check-the-box regime. A French SAS is default subject to corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés). No pass-through election is available. Available elections include: (1) group tax consolidation (intégration fiscale) under Article 223 A of the French General Tax Code, subject to a 95% shareholding and intra-group election; (2) the parent-subsidiary regime (régime mère-fille) election for dividend exemption; and (3) for certain SMEs, temporary and limited cash-basis VAT and reduced-rate elections. Hybrid mismatch rules under Articles 212 bis A and following of the General Tax Code constrain cross-border elections; no elective hybrid classification is recognised.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For established businesses: EUR 85,000 annual turnover for supplies of goods; EUR 37,500 for supplies of services (with lower thresholds for certain liberal activities). For foreign suppliers: no registration threshold applies where taxable transactions are carried out in France; non-established EU suppliers making cross-border distance sales are subject to the EUR 10,000 pan-EU distance-sale threshold with the option of the VAT One-Stop-Shop (OSS).

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A 0% VAT rate (export exemption with right to deduct input VAT) applies to supplies of goods physically exported from the European Union to a destination outside the EU, provided the exporter is the taxable person and the goods leave EU territory under the conditions of Article 262-I-1° and Article 72 or 74 of the French Customs Code. Also zero-rated are dispatches of goods to another EU Member State when the customer is identified for VAT and a recapitulative statement is filed. Services, including software or robotic-AI toy maintenance services, supplied to a recipient established outside the EU are zero-rated where the service is directly linked to exported goods or to goods placed under a suspension regime (Article 259 A of the General Tax Code). Exemption needs adequate commercial and transport evidence.

  • Customs Export Declaration (via DELTA-D or one-line declaration; SAD/DAU number)
  • Bill of Lading (B/L), Air Waybill, CMR or other transport document evidencing physical export
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (bank credit mandate evidencing receipt of the export proceeds from the foreign customer)
  • Commercial invoice and sales contract (including Incoterms and payment terms)
  • Proof of exit from EU customs territory (e.g., customs acknowledgement, EX1 electronic export confirmation)

Mandatory B2B electronic invoicing and e-reporting reform will be phased in from 1 September 2026 (large entities) and 1 September 2027 (all taxable persons). E-invoices must be transmitted through a Partner Dematerialization Platform (PDP) or the Public Billing Portal; transaction data must be e-reported to the DGFiP data hub. No real-time stamping applies to domestic e-invoices, but certified cash-register systems must comply with NF 525. Export invoices must contain the SIREN/VAT references and export exemption references; digital clearance for export follows the EU customs delivery-automation (DELTA) environment.

The French tax administration will claw back improperly claimed export zero-rating, together with input VAT recovered, upon failure to produce the full substantiation bundle within the statutory retention period. Penalties: (1) 40% fine, increased to 80% for bad faith or fraudulent behaviour; (2) late-payment interest at 0.2% per month; (3) a 5% late-payment penalty after 30 days; (4) criminal tax fraud sanctions of up to EUR 500,000 fine and 5 years imprisonment for the legal representative; and (5) seizure and payment blocking for confirmed fraudulent export schemes.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France uses the euro and has no general foreign-exchange control, capital-control, repatriation, or mandatory profit-conversion regime. Current-account payments, including imports, exports, royalties, dividends, interest, and service fees, are generally freely transferable subject to sanctions, AML, tax, customs, transfer-pricing, withholding-tax, corporate-law, and beneficial-ownership controls. Capital-account transactions, including foreign direct investment, intercompany loans, acquisitions, and capital reductions, are generally unrestricted but may require statistical, prudential, investment-screening, or sector-specific filings. Dividends may be remitted after approval of annual accounts, determination of distributable profits, satisfaction of creditor and corporate-law requirements, and application of any required withholding-tax documentation. A French SAS should retain board or shareholder resolutions, approved financial statements, dividend vouchers, tax-clearance evidence where relevant, and related-party pricing support. Transactions involving sanctioned parties, prohibited jurisdictions, unusual payment chains, cryptoasset providers, or high-risk counterparties may be delayed, rejected, frozen, or reported.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France has no statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to the Banque de France, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no general statutory deadline for converting or repatriating foreign-currency export receipts. Export proceeds may generally be retained in foreign-currency accounts or converted into euros at the company's discretion, subject to contractual payment terms, accounting requirements, tax compliance, customs and Intrastat or statistical reporting where applicable, sanctions screening, and bank AML review. Cash or bearer negotiable instruments physically entering or leaving the European Union at amounts of EUR 10,000 or more must be declared to customs. Banks may impose contractual evidence, invoice, shipping, customs, or source-of-funds requirements before crediting or converting export receipts.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France, acting within the Eurosystem under the authority of the European Central Bank; foreign-exchange, payment-services, AML, and cross-border reporting rules are also administered or enforced by the French Treasury, the Autorité de contrôle prudentiel et de résolution, the Autorité des marchés financiers, and the French customs authority, Direction générale des douanes et droits indirects.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Stripe Fit: High

Medium for payment collection and operating payments; Low to Medium for incorporation capital and primary treasury banking. A regulated European Economic Area payment institution or electronic-money institution may provide payment accounts, euro transfers, cards, and foreign-currency services but is not a deposit-taking bank unless separately licensed as a credit institution. An EMI account should not be treated as a substitute for a French bank capital-deposit certificate unless the notary, registry, and incorporation bank expressly accept it. Availability depends on passporting, French regulatory status, safeguarding arrangements, sanctions policy, source-of-funds evidence, permitted countries, and the provider's risk appetite. Offshore digital EMIs without an EU passport, French establishment, or clear cross-border service rights create elevated account-freeze, onboarding, consumer-protection, and payment-finality risks. Stripe may support merchant acquiring or payment processing but does not replace a corporate current account or statutory capital escrow account.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: EUR 50 million (annual turnover) for full Master File and Local File; simplified documentation for companies with turnover below EUR 50 million but above EUR 400,000. The threshold for the country-by-country report (CbCR) is EUR 750 million consolidated group revenue.

Economic substance scope: France applies the 'substance over form' doctrine and anti-abuse rules (e.g., Article 155-A of the French General Tax Code) to counter artificial arrangements. While there is no formal Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) as in offshore jurisdictions, French tax authorities require that a company have real economic activity (employees, premises, decision-making) to benefit from tax treaties and to avoid being deemed a 'shell company' under the EU Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive (ATAD).

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Fraude fiscale)

Statutory sanction: Penalty of 80% of the evaded tax (majoration pour manœuvres frauduleuses) plus interest; criminal fine of up to EUR 500,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years (Article 1741 of the French General Tax Code).

Director personal liability: Directors may be held personally liable for the tax debt if they have committed fraud or mismanagement (action en comblement du passif). Travel bans and asset freezes may be imposed. In serious cases, directors face imprisonment.
Shell Company / Artificial Arrangement

Statutory sanction: Denial of tax treaty benefits, reclassification of income, and imposition of 40% penalty for lack of substance. The company may be deemed a 'conduit' and its income reattributed to the ultimate parent.

Director personal liability: Directors may be subject to 'abus de droit' (abuse of law) penalties and personal liability for the resulting tax deficiencies. Travel bans and restrictions on leaving France may be imposed during tax investigations.
Transfer Pricing Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Adjustment of taxable income, penalty of 5% of the adjusted amount (up to 40% in case of gross negligence), and interest. In cases of unjustified transfer pricing, a 100% penalty may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held personally liable for the additional tax and penalties if they knowingly approved non-compliant transfer pricing policies. In extreme cases, criminal charges for tax evasion may be brought.
AML (Anti-Money Laundering) Violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative fines up to EUR 1 million (or 10% of turnover) and criminal penalties for directors. The company may be blacklisted and lose its ability to operate.

Director personal liability: Directors face personal fines and imprisonment up to 5 years for money laundering offenses. Travel bans and asset seizures are possible.
