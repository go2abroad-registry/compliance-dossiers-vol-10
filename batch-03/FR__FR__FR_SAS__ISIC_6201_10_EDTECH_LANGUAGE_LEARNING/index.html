Stage 02

Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent registry, governing statute, capital lockup rules, and first board resolution template.

Competent corporate registry

Registre du Commerce et des Sociétés (RCS), kept by the Greffe du Tribunal de commerce (Paris: Tribunal de Commerce de Paris); filings are received through the national single window (formalites.entreprises.gouv.fr) operated by the INPI. The certified identity extract issued is the Extrait Kbis.

Governing statute

France – Code de commerce (Commercial Code), Articles L.227-1 to L.227-30 (Société par Actions Simplifiée); Articles L.210-1 to L.210-10 (general company formation); Articles L.225-113 to L.225-135 mutatis mutandis on subscription and payment of share capital; Décret n°2021-1621 and implementing texts on the single digital window (Guichet unique); Article R.123-31 on the contents of the incorporation filing.

Formation / constitutional filings

Déclaration de création d'entreprise (electronic single incorporation declaration) submitted on the Guichet unique, accompanied by the executed Statuts (Articles of Association), the bank attestation of capital deposit (attestation de dépôt des fonds), the certificate of publication of the legal formation notice, and the President's declaration of no criminal conviction.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

A French SAS has no statutory minimum capital. The present entity is formed with EUR 10,000 share capital; every cash subscriber must pay at least 50% of the subscribed value at incorporation. The funds must be deposited into a temporarily blocked escrow account held with a French bank or the Caisse des Dépôts et Consignations and are released only after registration (obtention of the Kbis). Contributions in kind must be fully delivered at incorporation; the unpaid balance may be called within five years.

Stage 03

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction Générale des Finances Publiques (DGFiP), Ministère de l'Économie, des Finances et de la Souveraineté industrielle et numérique (France); official portal: impots.gouv.fr; company law and statutory filings via formalites.entreprises.gouv.fr and the RCS (Infogreffe).

E-government filing portal

(1) French SAS: incorporate through the Guichet Unique des Formalités des Entreprises; the RCS Greffe assigns SIREN/SIRET, INSEE updates the national register, and DGFiP automatically issues the French tax identification number and intra-Community VAT number (FR + key + SIREN). (2) Foreign non-established entity with French VAT obligations: file the DGFiP non-resident VAT/TIN registration (commonly Form 1554-SD) with the territorial Service des Impôts des Entreprises, attaching certificate of incorporation, apostilled/legalized status evidence, foreign tax number, constitutional documents, and description of French taxable activities; DGFiP issues the French TIN/VAT identification number. (3) A non-EU or EU supplier of B2C electronic services may instead use the OSS/IOSS and must obtain an OSS registration number in an EU member state; full French VAT registration is required if the OSS cannot be used or the supply is outside OSS scope.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation — (1) French SAS: incorporate through the Guichet Unique des Formalités des Entreprises; the RCS Greffe assigns SIREN/SIRET, INSEE updates the national register, and DGFiP automatically issues the French tax identification number and intra-Community VAT number (FR + key + SIREN). (2) Foreign non-established entity with French VAT obligations: file the DGFiP non-resident VAT/TIN registration (commonly Form 1554-SD) with the territorial Service des Impôts des Entreprises, attaching certificate of incorporation, apostilled/legalized status evidence, foreign tax number, constitutional documents, and description of French taxable activities; DGFiP issues the French TIN/VAT identification number. (3) A non-EU or EU supplier of B2C electronic services may instead use the OSS/IOSS and must obtain an OSS registration number in an EU member state; full French VAT registration is required if the OSS cannot be used or the supply is outside OSS scope.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

France has no check-the-box or elective pass-through regime for an SAS. An SAS is statutorily subject to corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés), with a territoriality rule exempting profits attributable to foreign permanent establishments. Optional elections include: (i) French fiscal integration (intégration fiscale, CGI Art. 223 A) for a 95%+ French subgroup; (ii) VAT grouping (assujetti unique, CGI Art. 256 C III) for qualifying closely-linked French taxable persons; (iii) election to apply or not apply the parent-subsidiary dividend exemption; and (iv) in cross-border structures, procedural options to elect transparent treatment of a foreign entity are generally denied; hybrid mismatch rules are mandatory and cannot be elected around.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For established small businesses, annual taxable turnover of EUR 85,000 (goods) or EUR 37,500 (services) for the franchise en base de TVA exemption; above those thresholds, subject to tolerance bands, French VAT registration is mandatory. For non-established suppliers there is no turnover threshold; any taxable supply of digital services to French consumers triggers French VAT via OSS/IOSS or direct registration. The EU-wide distance-sale/e-service threshold of EUR 10,000 applies to EU-established suppliers using the Union OSS scheme.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Tangible goods physically exported from France to a destination outside the European Union are eligible for 0% French VAT under CGI Art. 262 I 1° and EU VAT Directive Art. 146 where the seller demonstrates dispatch and the goods leave the EU under a valid customs export declaration. Supporting documentation must evidence the commercial transaction, export of the goods, and the physical exit from EU territory. Electronic/EdTech services are not 'goods exports': cross-border B2B services are generally taxable at the customer's place of establishment and B2C e-services to French consumers are taxed in France via OSS, not zero-rated; where the place of supply is outside France, the supply is merely outside the scope of French VAT.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Déclaration d'Exportation / EXS) accepted by French Customs, including the MRN
  • Bill of Lading or equivalent transport document (Air Waybill/CMR) proving physical removal from France/EU
  • Inward Remittance Certificate and bank/FX records matching the export invoice, payment and MRN

France is implementing mandatory structured e-invoicing for domestic B2B transactions through certified Plateformes de Dématérialisation Partenaire (PDPs) linked to DGFiP's Public Portal (PPF). From 1 September 2026, large and medium issuers must issue e-invoices and all taxpayers must receive them; small issuers are required from 1 September 2027. The system uses API e-reporting and central collection, not real-time tax-authority clearance or digital stamping/QR-code validation; B2C transaction data is reported electronically to DGFiP.

DGFiP and French Customs may audit export zero-rating retrospectively within the statutory limitation period. If the export file lacks mandatory documentation or is fraudulent, the administration recharacterizes the supply as French taxable and assesses 20% VAT on the invoice value, adds late-payment interest at 0.20% per month, and applies penalties of 40% for bad faith or 80% for fraud or tax maneuvers. False customs declarations may incur customs penalties, confiscation and criminal exposure; refunded VAT can be clawed back with interest, and the reassessment period is extended in cases of fraud or undisclosed activity.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France is a full member of the Eurozone; the Euro (EUR) is fully convertible for both current and capital account transactions. No exchange control restrictions apply to non-resident investors or to the repatriation of profits, dividends, or capital. Profit remittance is subject to standard corporate income tax (25% standard rate) and, where applicable, withholding tax on dividends (25% under domestic law, reduced to 0% under the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive for qualifying EU parent companies). No prior administrative authorization is required for capital movements, except for certain transactions involving non-EU countries that may require statistical reporting to the Banque de France for balance-of-payments purposes.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France imposes no mandatory surrender or conversion of export proceeds. Exporters are free to retain foreign currency in designated accounts (comptes en devises) or convert to EUR at their discretion. There is no statutory timeline for repatriation of export receipts, nor any mandatory conversion ratio. However, all cross-border transactions exceeding €50,000 must be reported to the Banque de France for statistical balance-of-payments purposes, typically via the intermediary bank. For EdTech subscription revenues (digital services), the applicable VAT rules (reverse charge for B2B, place of supply rules for B2C) are separate from FX controls.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (Bank of France) in coordination with the French Treasury (Direction Générale du Trésor) and the Autorité de Contrôle Prudentiel et de Résolution (ACPR) for banking supervision; exchange control policy is administered by the Ministry of Economy, Finance and Industrial and Digital Sovereignty.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. France recognizes Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) and Payment Institutions (PIs) under the ACPR licensing regime (transposing the EU PSD2 and EMD2 directives). Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., UK-based or non-EU) may operate in France only under a passporting regime (for EU/EEA entities) or via a French branch/agent. For a French SAS, using a fully licensed French EMI (e.g., Qonto, Shine, or Treezor) is viable for day-to-day treasury operations, but for large cross-border capital injections and trade settlements, a tier-1 commercial bank is recommended due to higher counterparty limits and SWIFT capabilities. Legal constraints: EMIs cannot provide credit or hold client funds beyond the e-money float; they are not suitable for escrow or complex FX hedging.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer protocols: Inbound capital injections (shareholder loans or equity contributions) must be routed via SWIFT MT103 with a clear purpose code. For France, the Banque de France requires a 'Motif de l'opération' (purpose code) for balance-of-payments reporting. Standard codes: 'S010' for equity capital contributions, 'S020' for shareholder loans (if >€50,000). The remitter must include the company's SIREN number and the nature of the transaction. Verification: The receiving bank will request a copy of the share subscription agreement, board resolution, and proof of identity of the investor. For non-EU investors, a French bank may require a 'Déclaration de Transfert de Fonds' (DTF) form to be filed with the Banque de France within 20 days of the transaction. The funds must be credited to a designated EUR account; no prior FX approval is needed.Clearing rails for trade settlements: For EdTech subscription payments to foreign content providers, software licensors, or marketing agencies, the primary rail is SWIFT MT103 (single customer credit transfer) for direct payments. For recurring high-volume disbursements, a SEPA Direct Debit (SDD) or SEPA Credit Transfer (SCT) is used for EU counterparties. For non-EU trade settlements, commercial Letters of Credit (LC) are rarely used for service-based EdTech; instead, open account with SWIFT MT103 is standard. For larger contracts, a standby LC (SBLC) may be issued by the French bank to guarantee payment. All outbound transfers must include a purpose code (e.g., 'S030' for service payments, 'S040' for royalties). The bank will verify the underlying contract and may require a proforma invoice. For payments to sanctioned jurisdictions, the bank will block the transaction; a compliance review is mandatory.FX Forwards (plain vanilla) with a maturity up to 12 months, available from tier-1 banks for EUR/USD, EUR/GBP, EUR/CNY, and other major pairs. No underlying trade documentation required for amounts below €1 million; above that, a commercial contract is needed.Non-Deliverable Forwards (NDFs) are permissible for currencies with limited convertibility (e.g., INR, KRW, TWD) but are rarely used for EUR-based EdTech companies; they are available via major banks with a special approval from the French Treasury for hedging purposes only.Cross-Currency Swaps (CCS) are permissible for long-term hedging (over 1 year) of foreign currency debt or investment flows. Requires a detailed risk management policy and board approval. The bank will require an ISDA Master Agreement and a Credit Support Annex (CSA).FX Options (plain vanilla calls/puts) are permissible for hedging subscription revenue volatility, but are subject to the bank's internal risk appetite and may require a premium payment upfront.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% corporate income tax. A reduced 15% rate may apply to the first EUR 42,500 of taxable profit for qualifying small companies, generally subject to turnover not exceeding EUR 10 million, fully paid-up capital, and at least 75% individual ownership or qualifying equivalent ownership.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% French withholding tax generally applies to dividends and similar distributions paid by a French company to a nonresident. Exemption or reduction may apply under the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive as implemented in France, subject generally to a qualifying holding of at least 10%, minimum holding-period and anti-abuse conditions, or under an applicable tax treaty. The domestic rate for certain French-resident individual recipients is different and does not determine the nonresident corporate rate.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a fiscal year ending on 31 December, the annual return is generally due on the second business day following 1 May, subject to the applicable electronic-filing extension. For other year-ends, the return is generally due within three months after the fiscal year-end. Corporate income tax instalments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September, and 15 December. The final balance is generally due by the 15th day of the fourth month following year-end; for a 31 December year-end, the balance is generally due by 15 May.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: EUR 400 million. Comprehensive Local File and Master File obligations generally apply where the French entity or French tax group has annual pre-tax turnover or gross assets of at least EUR 400 million, where it owns or is owned directly or indirectly by an entity meeting that threshold, or where statutory related-party conditions otherwise apply. Entities below the threshold may still be required to provide simplified transfer-pricing documentation and evidence of arm's-length pricing upon request.

Economic substance scope: France does not operate a separate general economic-substance regulation regime equivalent to a standalone ESR statute. Substance is assessed through transfer-pricing rules, the French general anti-abuse rule, abuse-of-law doctrine, beneficial-ownership requirements, principal-purpose testing under treaties, controlled foreign company rules, interest limitation rules, anti-hybrid provisions, permanent-establishment principles, and EU anti-abuse law. The SAS should maintain French decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, premises or functional infrastructure, commercial risk control, accounting records, and demonstrable functions consistent with its EdTech activities. A conduit entity with no meaningful functions, risks, or control may suffer denial of treaty benefits, recharacterization, withholding, transfer-pricing adjustments, penalties, and joint or secondary liability.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and false corporate income tax filing

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, 10% to 80% tax penalties depending on conduct, 0.20% monthly late-payment interest, and possible criminal tax-fraud prosecution. Aggravated tax fraud may lead to imprisonment of up to seven years and substantial criminal fines, subject to statutory conditions.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur criminal liability for personal participation, civil liability for intentional misconduct or mismanagement, tax liability as a de facto manager in appropriate proceedings, and potential disqualification from management. A travel ban or imprisonment is possible only under applicable criminal or judicial measures and is not automatic.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Reintegration of understated French profits, withholding-tax assessment, interest, penalties, documentation penalties, and possible denial of treaty benefits or tax-credit claims.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil liability for deliberate mismanagement or participation in fraudulent arrangements. Criminal exposure requires proof of an underlying offense such as tax fraud, forgery, corruption, or money laundering.
Shell company and treaty-shopping abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty or EU exemption, withholding at domestic rates, abuse-of-law adjustment, interest, and penalties up to the statutory maximum for abusive conduct.

Director personal liability: Personal liability may arise for knowing participation in abusive or fraudulent conduct. Courts may impose management bans or criminal measures where separate criminal offenses are established.
Withholding-tax failure

Statutory sanction: Recovery of unpaid withholding from the French payer, late-payment interest, applicable penalties, and possible liability for incorrect or omitted withholding declarations.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed in cases of fraudulent concealment, gross misconduct, or criminal participation; ordinary company-level withholding errors do not automatically pierce the corporate veil.
Accounting and recordkeeping failure

Statutory sanction: Tax-base estimation, rejection of deductions, penalties, audit extension, accounting sanctions, and possible criminal liability for falsified accounts.

Director personal liability: Directors have statutory duties regarding proper accounts and may face civil claims, management liability, or criminal prosecution for intentional falsification or obstruction.
VAT and digital-platform misclassification

Statutory sanction: VAT assessment, interest, penalties, denied deductions, joint-and-several collection in specified cases, and possible registration or reporting sanctions.

Director personal liability: Personal liability may arise for deliberate fraud, organized concealment, or serious management misconduct, but ordinary VAT errors remain primarily corporate liabilities.
Money laundering and prohibited payments

Statutory sanction: Suspicious-transaction reporting, account restrictions, confiscation, AML fines, tax reassessment, and criminal money-laundering penalties. Money laundering may carry imprisonment and substantial fines depending on the offense and aggravating factors.

Director personal liability: Directors and beneficial owners may face criminal prosecution, asset confiscation, management bans, travel restrictions, and imprisonment where personal knowledge or participation is proven.
Corruption, bribery, and procurement misconduct

Statutory sanction: Criminal fines, imprisonment, confiscation, debarment, damages, and corporate compliance obligations under French anti-corruption law.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face criminal imprisonment, fines, professional disqualification, travel restrictions, and civil restitution. The company may incur separate criminal fines and compliance-monitoring measures.
Abuse of corporate assets and unlawful distributions

Statutory sanction: Repayment, cancellation of unlawful distributions, civil damages, shareholder claims, insolvency consequences, and criminal sanctions for abuse of corporate assets where applicable.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for restitution and damages and may face imprisonment, fines, and management bans under the applicable French corporate and criminal provisions.
Insolvency and wrongful trading

Statutory sanction: Clawback of transactions, insolvency proceedings, director contribution to insufficient assets, bankruptcy-related penalties, and possible criminal sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors may be ordered to contribute personally to the company's shortfall, prohibited from managing companies, and imprisoned or fined for specified bankruptcy offenses.
Obstruction of tax audit or destruction of evidence

Statutory sanction: Separate procedural penalties, estimated assessment, increased tax penalties, evidentiary presumptions, and possible criminal prosecution for obstruction, forgery, or tax fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers who direct or authorize obstruction may face personal fines, criminal prosecution, management bans, travel restrictions, and imprisonment where legally established.
