Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum share capital for an SAS; the capital is freely fixed in the Articles and may be from €1. In-kind contributions require a statutory auditor's report (commissaire aux apports) unless lawfully dispensed.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction générale des finances publiques (DGFiP), administered through the relevant Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE) and the official impots.gouv.fr tax portal. Non-established businesses generally deal with the Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères (SIEE). Company incorporation and initial enterprise identification are filed through the Guichet unique operated by the Institut national de la propriété industrielle (INPI).

E-government filing portal

A French SAS obtains its enterprise identifiers through the INPI Guichet unique registration filing. The registration process generates a SIREN number for the legal entity, one or more SIRET numbers for establishments, and an APE activity code. The company must activate its professional tax account on impots.gouv.fr and register for VAT with the competent SIE before, or immediately upon commencement of, taxable activities. The French VAT identification number generally has the format FR plus a two-digit control key and the nine-digit SIREN. A foreign entity without a French establishment applies to the SIEE for French VAT registration, normally providing constitutional documents, proof of business activity, identification of directors and beneficial owners, evidence of taxable French transactions, and a French fiscal representative where legally required. The VAT registration process may require submission of the applicable VAT registration information and subsequent periodic CA3 VAT returns. Non-established EU businesses generally do not require a fiscal representative; non-EU businesses may require one unless an applicable legal exception applies.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation. A French Société par Actions Simplifiée is, by default, subject to French corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés, or IS) on its taxable profits. A French-resident SAS is generally taxed on worldwide business profits, subject to treaty allocation rules, foreign tax credits, permanent-establishment principles, and specific territorial provisions. The SAS form is not fiscally transparent by default. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation. A French Société par Actions Simplifiée is, by default, subject to French corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés, or IS) on its taxable profits. A French-resident SAS is generally taxed on worldwide business profits, subject to treaty allocation rules, foreign tax credits, permanent-establishment principles, and specific territorial provisions. The SAS form is not fiscally transparent by default. A medical-clinic EMR SaaS business is ordinarily subject to VAT on its software and SaaS supplies; the VAT exemption for qualifying medical care does not generally exempt software supplied to medical professionals or clinics.

Available election forms

A SAS may elect temporarily for taxation under the personal income tax regime (impôt sur le revenu, or IR) if the statutory conditions are met, including qualifying size, age, ownership, non-listing, and operating-activity requirements. The election generally requires shareholder approval and timely notification to the tax administration and is limited to five financial years. It does not constitute a general check-the-box election and is unavailable or restricted once statutory conditions cease to be satisfied. A SAS may also participate in French tax consolidation, subject to ownership and other statutory conditions, and may access the parent-subsidiary regime for qualifying dividends. France has no general United States-style check-the-box election for SAS entities and no elective hybrid-mismatch classification that converts a SAS automatically into a fiscally transparent entity. Anti-hybrid-mismatch rules under the EU Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive and French implementing legislation may deny deductions or impose inclusion where relevant.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No general turnover threshold permits a French SAS carrying out taxable SaaS supplies to remain outside VAT once it is liable for French VAT. The domestic franchise en base regime may apply to qualifying small businesses below the statutory thresholds. For services, the principal French threshold is EUR 37,500 of annual turnover, with a tolerance threshold of EUR 41,250, subject to applicable year-specific legislation and conditions. For goods and certain accommodation activities, separate higher thresholds apply. The franchise does not generally eliminate VAT obligations for cross-border transactions, reverse-charge transactions, imports, or transactions for which the taxpayer voluntarily opts for normal VAT taxation. The EU-wide EUR 10,000 threshold for certain intra-EU business-to-consumer distance sales and electronically supplied services is separate from the French domestic registration rules and may be administered through the Union OSS regime.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods from France to destinations outside the EU may be zero-rated or exempt with the right to deduct input VAT when the supplier can substantiate exportation and satisfy the applicable customs and VAT conditions. Intra-EU supplies of goods may qualify for exemption where the customer has a valid EU VAT number, the goods are dispatched to another Member State, and documentary and reporting conditions are satisfied. For SaaS and other electronically supplied services, there is generally no customs export declaration. A B2B service supplied to a business established outside France is normally outside the scope of French VAT under the EU place-of-supply rule, with the customer generally accounting for VAT under reverse charge in its jurisdiction. The supplier must verify and document customer business status, location, contractual supply, and payment or commercial evidence. B2C electronically supplied services are generally taxed where the consumer resides; the supplier may need to charge local VAT through the applicable OSS or non-Union OSS mechanism. A French medical or healthcare customer does not, by itself, make EMR software VAT-exempt. Input VAT refunds require valid invoices, a direct business purpose, and compliance with the applicable French VAT return or refund procedure.

  • For physical goods exported outside the EU: customs export declaration or equivalent electronic customs record, including the export reference and proof of exit.
  • Commercial invoice, purchase order or contract, transport documentation such as bill of lading, air waybill, CMR, or equivalent, and packing list where relevant.
  • Evidence linking the supplier, customer, goods, customs declaration, and transport movement.
  • For intra-EU goods supplies: customer VAT identification number, transport evidence, and required recapitulative or statistical reporting where applicable.
  • For pure EMR SaaS, cloud hosting, software licensing, and offshore services: customs documentation is generally not applicable. The substantiation file should instead contain the customer VAT or business identification, customer location evidence, contract, invoice with the applicable reverse-charge or place-of-supply wording, service-delivery records, payment evidence, and records supporting the tax treatment.
  • An inward remittance certificate is not a general French statutory prerequisite for VAT zero-rating. Bank records and payment evidence may nevertheless be requested as corroborating commercial evidence during an audit.

France is implementing mandatory electronic invoicing and electronic transaction reporting through approved electronic invoicing platforms and the French public invoicing infrastructure. The planned timetable requires all French-established VAT-taxable businesses to be able to receive electronic invoices from 1 September 2026. Issuance obligations are scheduled to apply from 1 September 2026 to large and medium-sized enterprises and from 1 September 2027 to small and micro-enterprises, subject to legislative and administrative updates. Electronic invoicing principally covers domestic business-to-business transactions between French-established VAT-taxable businesses; electronic reporting applies to relevant cross-border and business-to-consumer transactions. France does not operate a universal real-time invoice-clearance model for all invoices. Required data must be transmitted through an approved platform or the applicable public infrastructure, with structured invoice data, transaction reporting, payment information where required, and platform interoperability. Taxpayers must maintain compliant digital invoices, audit trails, authenticity and integrity controls, and legally required retention records. Cross-border SaaS invoices may require reverse-charge wording rather than French VAT, depending on customer status and place-of-supply rules.

France has no general statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation requirement for export VAT treatment. Export exemption or zero-rating may be denied or clawed back if exportation, customer status, destination, transport, or place-of-supply evidence is incomplete or inaccurate. The DGFiP may reassess unpaid VAT, deny input VAT recovery, assess late-payment interest generally at 0.20% per month under the applicable rules, and impose penalties including 10% for late or insufficient payment, 40% for deliberate failure, and up to 80% for fraudulent maneuvers or abusive concealment, subject to statutory conditions and procedural safeguards. False invoices, fraudulent customs declarations, organized VAT fraud, or intentional evasion may also trigger criminal prosecution, additional fines, and imprisonment under applicable French law. Repeated or material non-compliance may result in enhanced audit scrutiny, denial of simplified reporting treatment, and liability for directors or other participants where statutory conditions for personal liability are met.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France operates under full capital and current account convertibility as a member of the Eurozone and the EU. There are no exchange controls restricting the conversion of EUR to foreign currencies or the repatriation of profits, dividends, or capital. Profit remittance is subject to standard corporate tax documentation and anti-money laundering (AML) verification, but no prior administrative approval is required. Non-resident shareholders may freely receive dividends subject to withholding tax treaties and EU directives.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender or conversion of export proceeds is imposed. Exporters may retain foreign currency in designated accounts. However, all cross-border transactions exceeding €12,500 must be reported to the Banque de France for balance-of-payments statistical purposes. The reporting is typically done via the bank or directly through the BOP (Balance of Payments) online portal. There is no statutory timeline for conversion; proceeds may be held indefinitely in foreign currency accounts.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (Bank of France) in coordination with the European Central Bank (ECB) for monetary policy; the French Treasury (Direction Générale du Trésor) oversees balance-of-payments reporting and foreign exchange statistics.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

High feasibility for EU-licensed Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) operating under the PSD2 passporting regime. EMIs such as Qonto, Shine, or N26 Business can provide multi-currency accounts and cross-border payment rails with lower onboarding friction. Legal constraints include mandatory ACPR registration for French EMIs, but EU-passported EMIs can operate in France without separate licensing. For a low-risk SaaS business, EMIs are a viable alternative to traditional banks, though they may lack full credit facilities and complex trade finance instruments.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant wire transfer protocols for inbound capital injection (e.g., equity contribution from non-resident shareholders) must use SWIFT MT103 with a clear purpose code. For France, the appropriate purpose code for equity investment is 'P001' (Direct investment) or 'P002' (Portfolio investment) under the Banque de France BOP classification. The transfer must be accompanied by a copy of the board resolution approving the capital increase, the signed subscription agreement, and the bank's internal KYC documentation. The receiving bank will verify the source of funds and may require a certificate of non-resident status for tax withholding purposes. For amounts exceeding EUR 10,000, the bank must file a BOP declaration with the Banque de France.Clearing rails for outbound trade settlements include SWIFT MT103 for direct payments (e.g., software licensing fees, cloud infrastructure costs) and Commercial Letters of Credit (LC) for high-value or contractual trade transactions. For recurring SaaS disbursements, MT103 is standard with purpose codes such as 'P300' (Services) or 'P310' (Royalties). For LC, the bank will require a proforma invoice, transport documents (if goods), and compliance with UCP 600. Additionally, SEPA (Single Euro Payments Area) transfers are used for EUR-denominated payments within the EU, offering faster and cheaper settlement. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% standard corporate income tax rate for fiscal years beginning on or after 1 January 2022

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% under Article 119 bis CGI for dividends distributed to non-resident corporate shareholders; 75% if the shareholder is established in a non-cooperative jurisdiction (NCJS). Dividends to qualifying EU parent companies holding at least 5% or 10% and satisfying the Parent-Subsidiary Directive conditions are exempt from source withholding. Non-resident individual withholding treatment is governed by specific domestic withholding provisions and may be reduced under applicable double tax treaties.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory Master File and Local File are required where the French taxpayer or its group has annual gross turnover or total consolidated balance-sheet assets of EUR 400,000,000 or more (Article L13 AA LPF). Country-by-Country reporting is mandatory for groups with consolidated turnover of at least EUR 750,000,000. Below these thresholds, documentation is not automatically required but may be demanded by the auditor; contemporaneous OECD-compliant documentation is recommended for all cross-border SaaS arrangements.

Economic substance scope: France does not operate a standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime comparable to UAE ESR, but substance is enforced through the abuse-of-law doctrine (abus de droit), DTA anti-abuse clauses, Article L64 LPF, and the OECD Principal Purpose Test. A French SAS must demonstrate real operational substance: full and part-time employees, shared core decisions made in France, physical or cloud infrastructure under the entity’s control, material intercompany contracts, and an invoicing flow that corresponds to actual staff and costs. A shell French SAS used solely to licence foreign EMR algorithms, pay out dividends, or bracket commissions without active management activity will be challenged and recharacterised.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Fraud (Fraude fiscale)

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties: 40% for deliberate tax neglect and 80% for abusive or fraudulent activity, plus late interest at 0.20% per month. Criminal sanctions under Article 1741 CGI: fine up to EUR 500,000 and five years’ imprisonment; aggravated circumstances can raise penalties to EUR 1,000,000 and seven years, with confiscation of funds.

Director personal liability: Director who deliberately organises or procures the fraud may be declared jointly and severally liable for underpaid tax, penalties, and restitution. A court may impose a travel ban, prohibit directorship of French commercial companies, order asset freezing, and issue a criminal conviction that is visible to financial institutions and commercial counterparties.
Artificial Profit Shifting / Abusive Shell Company (Abus de droit)

Statutory sanction: DGFiP may invalidate the arrangement under Article L64 LPF, reinstate taxable profit in France, disallow foreign royalty and management-fee deductions, apply the 80% abuse-of-law penalty, and, for NCJS transfers, levy a 75% withholding tax on distributed benefits.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly endorsed a sham structure risk piercing of the corporate veil; they can be personally liable to the public treasury for reinstated tax and penalties. Travel bans, civil restitution orders, and disqualification from managing French legal entities may be ordered; nominal figurehead directors of medical EMR companies face heightened scrutiny because clinical-data activity requires genuine local responsibility.
Transfer Pricing Documentation Failure

Statutory sanction: Immediate fine for failure to hand over documents on audit; tax administration may recharacterise the controlled transaction by discretion and reassess French taxable profit, with late interest and, in refuse situations, a 40% bad-faith penalty. Simultaneous tax examinations and mutual assistance procedures may be opened.

Director personal liability: Directors who fail to maintain a defensible arm’s length file or who knowingly approve inflated intercompany buy-downs can have the reassessed corporate tax and penalties enforced against them where serious mismanagement (faute de gestion) is proven. Repeated failures may trigger criminal consequences for fiscal fraud if the documentation gap is used to dissimulate income.
