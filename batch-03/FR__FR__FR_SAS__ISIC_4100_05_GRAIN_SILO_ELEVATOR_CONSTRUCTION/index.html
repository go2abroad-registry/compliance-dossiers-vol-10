Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP), under the Ministry of the Economy, Finance and Industrial and Digital Sovereignty; tax services and online procedures are available through impots.gouv.fr. The Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères (SIEE) handles applicable French tax registrations for non-established businesses.

E-government filing portal

A newly incorporated French SAS obtains a SIREN and SIRET through registration in the Registre national des entreprises, managed by the Institut national de la propriété industrielle (INPI), via the formalités d’entreprises portal. The DGFiP assigns or confirms the French tax account identifiers. For VAT, the business requests registration from the competent Service des impôts des entreprises and receives a French VAT number in the format FR plus a two-character key and the SIREN. A foreign company making taxable supplies in France without a French establishment generally applies to the SIEE, providing incorporation and tax-residence evidence, identification of its legal representative, activity and transaction details, and, where required, a fiscal representative. EU-established applicants are generally not required to appoint a fiscal representative; non-EU applicants generally must appoint a representative unless an applicable legal exception or mutual assistance arrangement removes that requirement. VAT registration does not itself establish a permanent establishment.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A French SAS is generally subject to French corporation tax (impôt sur les sociétés, IS). A qualifying young company may elect, subject to statutory ownership, age, activity, size and other conditions, for temporary taxation under the income-tax regime for up to five fiscal years. This is not a US-style check-the-box election. No general entity-classification election is available to elect pass-through treatment, and no general French hybrid-mismatch election changes the SAS's default classification. Specific tax regimes and treaty relief may apply where statutory conditions are met.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No general turnover threshold applies to a business required to register for VAT on taxable transactions in France. A qualifying small business may use the franchise en base de TVA, subject to current statutory turnover limits and eligibility conditions. For ordinary services, the principal domestic threshold is EUR 37,500, with a higher tolerance threshold; for goods, the principal threshold is EUR 85,000, also with a higher tolerance threshold. Thresholds, transitional rules and eligibility should be confirmed for the relevant tax year. A foreign supplier's registration obligations depend on establishment, transaction type, customer status and applicable reverse-charge or special-scheme rules.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods dispatched or transported from France to a destination outside the European Union may be exempt from French VAT with a right to deduct input VAT, provided the statutory conditions are met and the exporter can prove the goods left the EU. Intra-EU supplies of goods may be exempt only when the customer is VAT-identified in another Member State, the goods are transported there, and reporting and documentary conditions are satisfied. Services are not zero-rated merely because the customer or payment is offshore: place-of-supply rules determine whether French VAT applies. A French construction or installation project may be taxable in France, including under special immovable-property rules, regardless of the customer's location. Refunds require valid supporting records and compliance with VAT return and reporting obligations.

  • Export customs declaration and electronic exit confirmation or equivalent evidence accepted by the tax authorities
  • Commercial invoice, sales contract or purchase order identifying the goods, parties, destination and transaction
  • Transport evidence appropriate to the route, such as bill of lading, CMR consignment note, air waybill or carrier confirmation
  • Evidence linking the exported goods to the invoice and customs declaration, including packing lists where relevant
  • For intra-EU supplies rather than exports outside the EU: customer VAT identification evidence, transport evidence, and required recapitulative and statistical reporting records
  • Bank remittance evidence may support the audit trail but is not, by itself, a general statutory condition for export VAT exemption

France is implementing mandatory electronic invoicing for domestic B2B transactions between businesses subject to French VAT, with phased requirements beginning in September 2026 and later phases for smaller businesses. In-scope invoices are to be transmitted through an approved platform (plateforme de dématérialisation partenaire) or the public invoicing portal where available; transaction and payment data reporting (e-reporting) applies to specified transactions outside the domestic e-invoicing scope. The regime is not a general real-time pre-clearance or digital-stamping system. Public-sector invoicing is handled through Chorus Pro. Businesses should verify the applicable rollout date, platform requirements and transaction coverage.

France imposes no general 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds. Incorrectly exempted or zero-rated supplies may result in assessment of unpaid VAT, late-payment interest and tax penalties. Penalties depend on the facts and applicable Tax Code provisions; deliberate fraud or fraudulent documentation can trigger materially higher penalties and, where statutory elements are met, criminal proceedings. Input VAT refunds may be denied or recovered if entitlement or supporting evidence is insufficient. Exporters should retain transaction, customs, transport, accounting and VAT records for the applicable statutory retention and limitation periods.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France uses the euro and generally has full current-account and capital-account convertibility within the European Union framework. A French SAS may receive and remit dividends, interest, principal, service fees, royalties, and capital proceeds cross-border without a general foreign-exchange approval or mandatory conversion into euros. Transfers remain subject to bank AML and counter-terrorist-financing controls, beneficial-owner identification, sanctions screening, tax compliance, transfer-pricing support, corporate-authority evidence, and applicable reporting. Payments involving sanctioned jurisdictions, restricted counterparties, unusual third-party accounts, cash-intensive structures, or unsupported intercompany balances may be delayed, rejected, frozen, or reported. Dividend and interest remittances require available distributable profits or valid debt documentation, corporate approvals, withholding-tax analysis, and applicable treaty or European Union directive relief. Cross-border cash movements of EUR 10,000 or more carried by individuals are subject to customs declaration requirements; this does not constitute a general restriction on bank transfers.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France has no general statutory requirement for exporters to surrender export proceeds to the Banque de France, convert a prescribed percentage of export receipts into euros, or repatriate export proceeds within a fixed foreign-exchange deadline. Export proceeds may ordinarily remain in foreign-currency accounts or be received through foreign or French accounts, subject to contractual banking requirements, accounting records, tax documentation, sanctions controls, and applicable statistical or payment-reporting obligations. Customs export formalities and VAT evidence must be maintained separately from foreign-exchange controls. Banks may impose internal receipt, documentary, or transaction-monitoring deadlines under their account terms and AML procedures; those contractual controls are not statutory surrender ratios.

Central bank supervision: European Central Bank for euro monetary policy and payment-system oversight; Banque de France for French banking supervision functions delegated by the European Central Bank, foreign-exchange statistics, balance-of-payments reporting, and national payment-system responsibilities; the French Treasury Directorate-General (Direction générale du Trésor) for financial sanctions and certain national financial-control measures.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for payment operations and low to medium as a substitute for a French domestic bank. A licensed European Economic Area electronic-money institution or payment institution may provide payment accounts, safeguarding, SEPA transfers, foreign-currency services, and, where authorized, cross-border payment services. An offshore digital EMI without European authorization, passporting, or a properly appointed European service structure cannot lawfully provide regulated payment services in France merely through an online interface. An EMI account should not be assumed to satisfy French share-capital escrow, notarial capital-deposit, local bank-account, lender, or major-customer requirements. EMI onboarding remains subject to AML, UBO, source-of-funds, sanctions, transaction-purpose, and geographic-risk controls.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route equity through a designated French bank capital escrow account when required for the incorporation or capital increase, with the transfer sent from the investor's account and a payment reference identifying the SAS, subscription or contribution purpose, and relevant corporate resolution. For shareholder loans or other intercompany funding, use a documented loan or financing agreement, transfer-pricing support, maturity and interest terms, lender UBO evidence, and a purpose reference consistent with the legal instrument. Use SEPA Credit Transfer for euro payments within the SEPA area and SWIFT cross-border payments for non-SEPA or non-euro transfers; ISO 20022 payment messages are increasingly standard, although banks may still describe legacy correspondent instructions using MT terminology. Verify beneficiary name, IBAN, BIC, intermediary-bank requirements, sanctions status, source of funds, and receipt against the general ledger. Retain bank advices, contracts, resolutions, subscription records, and accounting evidence. Do not route capital through personal accounts or unrelated third parties.Use SEPA Credit Transfer for eligible euro payments and bank-to-bank SWIFT transfers for non-SEPA or foreign-currency settlements. A SWIFT MT103 is a legacy customer-credit-transfer format; the applicable bank may process the same payment through ISO 20022 pacs.008 or another current message standard. For milestone EPC payments, require an executed contract, invoice, approved progress or delivery certificate, purchase order, beneficiary-account verification, sanctions screening, and dual authorization. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés). A 3.3% social contribution may apply to companies with turnover of at least EUR 7.63 million and corporate income tax exceeding EUR 763,000, producing an aggregate effective rate of approximately 25.83%, subject to statutory conditions.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

For dividends paid by a French company to a non-resident legal entity, the domestic withholding rate is generally 25%, subject to applicable exemptions and tax treaties. A 0% exemption may apply under the French implementation of the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive, generally requiring an eligible EU or EEA parent, a minimum 10% holding, a minimum two-year holding commitment, subject-to-tax and beneficial-ownership conditions, and anti-abuse compliance. Different domestic rules apply to individual recipients.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a financial year ending on 31 December, the electronic return is generally due on the second business day following 1 May of the following year. For a non-calendar year-end, the return is generally due within three months after the financial year-end. The final corporate income tax balance is generally payable by the 15th day of the fourth month following year-end, subject to the applicable payment calendar and electronic-payment rules.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Corporate income tax instalments are generally due on specified quarterly dates, with the final balance generally due by the 15th day of the fourth month after year-end. Dividend, interest, and royalty withholding is generally declared and paid by the 15th day of the month following the payment or distribution, subject to the applicable form and payment rules.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: EUR 150 million of annual pre-tax turnover or gross assets generally triggers the full French transfer-pricing documentation obligation, including a Local File and Master File under Articles L13 AA and L13 AB of the French Tax Procedures Code. A Country-by-Country Report generally applies to multinational groups with consolidated annual revenue of at least EUR 750 million. Separate transfer-pricing disclosure and summary requirements may apply below the full-documentation threshold.

Economic substance scope: France does not operate a single standalone economic-substance regulation equivalent to a generalized ESR statute. Substance is enforced through the French general anti-abuse rule, abuse-of-law doctrine, beneficial-ownership requirements, principal-purpose analysis under treaties, controlled foreign company rules, interest limitation rules, hybrid mismatch rules, anti-conduit principles, and EU anti-avoidance legislation. The SAS should maintain French decision-making, personnel or genuinely outsourced operational capacity, premises or operational infrastructure, project-management evidence, contracting authority, risk control, bank-account functionality, accounting records, and commercial rationale proportionate to its construction activities. A French company with no personnel, no operational control, circular funds, and pass-through invoices faces heightened recharacterization and permanent-establishment risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent tax returns

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties may reach 40% for deliberate conduct and 80% for fraudulent conduct, abuse of law, or certain obstruction, in addition to tax recovery and 0.20% monthly interest. Criminal tax fraud may carry up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 500,000 fine, increased in aggravated cases to up to seven years and EUR 3 million, with higher penalties linked to proceeds or specific circumstances. Corporate criminal fines may be multiplied by five.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution, personal fines, imprisonment, confiscation, and civil tax liability where they personally participated or committed serious and repeated tax misconduct. A director may also be declared jointly liable for tax debts in cases of fraudulent conduct or serious and repeated failure under French tax law. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may be ordered by a court in appropriate criminal proceedings; veil piercing is exceptional and fact-dependent.
False invoicing, fictitious subcontracting, and accounting fraud

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, denial of deductions and tax credits, 40% or 80% tax penalties, recovery of unpaid withholding and VAT, and potential criminal prosecution for forgery, use of forged documents, fraud, or tax fraud. Criminal sanctions depend on the offense and facts and may include imprisonment, fines, confiscation, and corporate fines.

Director personal liability: Directors who approve or knowingly tolerate false documentation may incur criminal and civil liability. A director may be barred from management, required to contribute to an insolvency shortfall in cases of management fault, and exposed to personal tax liability under the relevant statutory conditions.
VAT fraud and carousel transactions

Statutory sanction: Recovery of VAT, late-payment interest, 40% or 80% penalties, and criminal prosecution for aggravated fraud. VAT invoices must reflect the actual supplier, customer, place of supply, reverse-charge status, and taxable amount. Intra-EU reporting failures may create separate penalties.

Director personal liability: Responsible directors may face criminal sanctions, personal tax liability for fraudulent or repeated conduct, management bans, asset confiscation, and insolvency-related liability. The French tax authority may deny input VAT where the company knew or should have known that the transaction formed part of fraud.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: The tax authority may adjust taxable profit under the arm's-length principle, impose 40% or 80% penalties where appropriate, charge interest, apply withholding tax to recharacterized distributions or royalties, and impose documentation-related penalties. Failure to provide required transfer-pricing documentation can trigger a specific penalty generally based on the greater of a statutory minimum or 0.5% of the transactions lacking documentation, subject to applicable limits.

Director personal liability: Directors are exposed where they knowingly approve non-arm's-length arrangements, conceal records, or obstruct an audit. Personal liability is not automatic for an ordinary pricing error but may arise from fraud, serious management fault, or deliberate tax evasion.
Improper treaty or EU withholding-tax relief

Statutory sanction: The French payer may be assessed for unpaid withholding tax, interest, and penalties. Anti-abuse provisions may deny treaty or EU directive relief and recharacterize payments as taxable distributions, interest, royalties, or hidden distributions.

Director personal liability: Directors approving knowingly incorrect withholding treatment may face civil, administrative, and criminal consequences depending on intent. The payer's legal representative can be pursued for compliance failures, particularly where records were falsified or the arrangement was deliberately abusive.
Shell company, hidden beneficial ownership, and unlawful profit diversion

Statutory sanction: Potential consequences include tax reassessment, abuse-of-law treatment, confiscation, money-laundering prosecution, corporate dissolution in severe cases, and penalties for inaccurate beneficial-owner filings. French companies must maintain and report beneficial-owner information to the competent registry.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal criminal liability for concealment, misuse of corporate assets, money laundering, or fraudulent management. Management bans, asset confiscation, civil restitution, insolvency contribution claims, and imprisonment may apply. Travel bans require a specific judicial order and are not automatic.
Money laundering and AML failures

Statutory sanction: Money laundering may carry up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 375,000 fine, with aggravated cases carrying up to ten years and a EUR 750,000 fine. Legal entities may face fines multiplied by five, confiscation, and additional measures. AML-regulated professionals may face supervisory sanctions, fines, and restrictions for customer-due-diligence failures.

Director personal liability: Individuals who knowingly participate in laundering may be imprisoned, fined, disqualified, and subjected to confiscation. Directors may also face civil and regulatory liability for systemic AML failures. Suspicious transaction reporting obligations depend on whether the SAS or its professional advisers fall within the regulated perimeter.
Bribery, public procurement corruption, and facilitation payments

Statutory sanction: Active or passive bribery may result in imprisonment and substantial fines, including corporate fines that can reach EUR 5 million or twice the proceeds for certain offenses. Additional confiscation, exclusion from public procurement, and compliance-program measures may apply. Sapin II compliance duties may apply to qualifying companies.

Director personal liability: Individuals may face imprisonment, fines, professional disqualification, confiscation, and civil restitution. Directors can be liable for authorizing or concealing bribery even where the payment was made through an intermediary.
Undeclared work and construction labor violations

Statutory sanction: Undeclared work can trigger social-security reassessment, cancellation of exemptions, administrative fines, criminal penalties, and exclusion from public procurement. Individual penalties can include up to three years' imprisonment and a EUR 45,000 fine, with higher sanctions in aggravated circumstances; corporate fines may be multiplied by five. Construction-specific obligations include verification of subcontractor compliance and, where relevant, site documentation.

Director personal liability: Directors may be prosecuted personally, fined, imprisoned, disqualified, and held liable for social-security or insolvency consequences where they knowingly organize or tolerate undeclared work.
Accounting, statutory audit, and corporate-law failures

Statutory sanction: Possible consequences include refusal of tax deductions, administrative and criminal sanctions for false accounts, invalid or unlawful distributions, shareholder claims, auditor reporting, and management bans. Unlawful dividends may have to be repaid.

Director personal liability: The president and directors may incur civil liability for mismanagement, repayment of unlawful distributions, contribution to insolvency liabilities, and criminal liability for false accounts or misuse of corporate assets.
Construction-site safety and environmental non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Sanctions may include criminal fines, imprisonment for individuals in serious cases, administrative orders, work stoppage, remediation costs, environmental fines, loss of permits or procurement eligibility, and civil damages. Liability depends on the specific labor, environmental, planning, and safety offense.

Director personal liability: The president, site managers, and responsible officers may face personal criminal and civil liability for intentional or negligent breaches, including bodily injury or environmental damage. Corporate limited liability does not protect individuals from their own offenses.
