Statutory Formation & Board Resolutions

Competent corporate registry

The competent local clerk of the Commercial Court (greffe du tribunal de commerce), acting on the application transmitted through the national Business Formalities Portal operated by the Institut National de la Propriété Industrielle (INPI).

Governing statute

French Commercial Code, including Articles L. 210-1 to L. 210-9 (commercial companies and formation), L. 227-1 to L. 227-20 (SAS), and R. 123-1 et seq. (business register formalities). French Monetary and Financial Code and applicable MiFID II provisions govern regulated investment services and algorithmic trading.

Formation / constitutional filings

Electronic company-formation application (formalités de création) through the Guichet unique, supported by the signed articles of association (statuts) and required supporting documents; registration results in a French company identification number and a Kbis extract.

Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime No Capital Lockup Account Required

No statutory minimum share capital applies to a SAS. Cash contributions must generally be paid up to at least one-half of their nominal amount at formation; the balance is payable within five years of registration. Cash funds are deposited with an authorized depositary, such as a bank, notary, or Caisse des Dépôts, and released upon production of evidence of registration. Contributions in kind are subject to valuation rules and may require a commissaire aux apports unless a statutory exemption applies. The stated USD filing-fee figure is an indicative conversion only; French fees are set and charged in euros and vary with formalities.

Funding & unlock path: No trading-specific civil-defense clearance applies. Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP), including the competent Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE); online services are provided through the impots.gouv.fr professional tax portal. Customs matters are administered by the Direction générale des douanes et droits indirects (DGDDI), including the DELTA customs systems.

E-government filing portal

A French SAS is incorporated and registered through the Guichet unique des formalités des entreprises operated by the Institut national de la propriété industrielle (INPI). Registration produces a SIREN identifier for the legal entity and a SIRET identifier for each establishment. The company must activate its professional tax account with the DGFiP and identify its competent SIE. A French VAT identification number, consisting of the country code FR followed by a two-digit key and the nine-digit SIREN, is obtained or validated through the DGFiP. A foreign entity without a French establishment applies to the competent French non-established-business tax service for VAT registration, generally before commencing taxable French transactions. Non-EU businesses may be required to appoint a French tax representative unless an applicable legal exemption or mutual-assistance arrangement applies. Intra-EU operators may also require activation for intra-Community VAT transactions and VIES validation. Customs activity requires an Economic Operators Registration and Identification (EORI) number obtained through French customs.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A SAS is generally subject to French corporate income tax at entity level. An eligible SAS may elect for temporary income-tax transparency for up to five years if statutory conditions are satisfied, including limits on workforce and turnover or balance-sheet size, non-listing, and specified individual ownership and management requirements. The election must be made within the statutory period and is generally irrevocable after the permitted period. There is no general United States-style check-the-box election. French tax rules contain anti-hybrid-mismatch provisions implementing EU measures, but they do not provide a general elective hybrid classification regime. VAT elections include the franchise-in-base regime where eligible, the simplified regime where available, and the normal real regime; these are VAT regime elections rather than entity-classification elections.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For a French-established SAS, there is generally no broad VAT registration threshold: taxable activities are within the VAT system from commencement unless the entity validly qualifies for and elects the franchise en base. Indicative French franchise thresholds for 2025 are EUR 25,000 of prior-year turnover and EUR 27,500 of current-year turnover for services, and EUR 85,000 and EUR 93,500 respectively for goods and accommodation activities, subject to legislative changes and activity-specific rules. A non-established business generally has no general French turnover threshold for French taxable transactions and may need registration from the first relevant transaction, subject to reverse-charge, OSS, or other statutory exceptions. Proprietary trading in financial instruments for the entity's own account normally does not constitute a taxable supply of services; separately supplied brokerage, market-making, advisory, or execution services require transaction-specific VAT analysis and may be exempt or taxable depending on their legal and economic characteristics.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods dispatched from France to a destination outside the European Union may qualify for VAT exemption at export if the goods leave the EU and the exporter can substantiate the export through customs records and proof of exit. The exemption is conditional and is not available merely because the customer is non-French. Intra-Community supplies of goods may qualify for exemption where the customer has a valid VAT number in another EU Member State, the goods are transported to that Member State, and reporting and documentary conditions are met. Cross-border business-to-business services are generally governed by place-of-supply and reverse-charge rules rather than a French 0% export rate. Services supplied to customers outside the EU may be outside the scope of French VAT where the place-of-supply rules so provide. Financial transactions may be VAT-exempt under specific statutory exemptions, but proprietary high-frequency trading, exchange access, liquidity provision, and ancillary technology or execution services must be analyzed separately. Input VAT recovery requires a direct and legally sufficient connection with taxable or qualifying transactions; exempt financial activity can restrict recovery.

  • Electronic export customs declaration and its Movement Reference Number (MRN)
  • Customs confirmation of exit from the European Union or equivalent exit record
  • Commercial invoice identifying the exporter, customer, goods, value, and delivery terms
  • Transport document, such as bill of lading, airway bill, or consignment note
  • Packing list and relevant origin, licensing, or export-control documents where applicable
  • Accounting records linking the exported goods, invoice, customs declaration, and payment
  • Proof of customer payment or other commercial evidence where requested by the DGFiP
  • Inward remittance certificates are not a general French statutory requirement for export VAT exemption; bank evidence may nevertheless support the transaction file

France does not currently operate a universal real-time VAT invoice-clearance system for all domestic transactions. The 2024 Finance Law establishes phased mandatory electronic invoicing and electronic transaction-data reporting. All businesses must be capable of receiving electronic invoices from 1 September 2026; issuance obligations apply to large and intermediate businesses from 1 September 2026 and to small and medium-sized businesses and micro-enterprises from 1 September 2027, subject to any statutory postponement. Invoices must generally pass through an approved platform or the public invoicing portal under the applicable framework. E-reporting applies to specified transactions outside domestic business-to-business e-invoicing, including relevant consumer and cross-border transactions. Existing invoice-content, VAT ledger, retention, and audit-trail requirements remain applicable. No general fiscal digital stamp or transaction-by-transaction tax authority pre-clearance is currently required.

The DGFiP may deny or reverse the export exemption, assess unpaid VAT, and disallow related input VAT recovery if export, transport, customer, or customs evidence is incomplete or contradictory. Reassessments may include statutory late-payment interest, generally 0.20% per month, and tax penalties generally ranging from 10% for specified filing or payment failures to 40% for deliberate failure and 80% for fraudulent manoeuvres or abuse of rights, subject to the facts and applicable procedural rules. False customs declarations may trigger customs debt, administrative penalties, seizure or confiscation, and potential criminal liability under EU and French customs law. The company should retain a contemporaneous transaction file and reconcile customs, invoicing, accounting, payment, and VAT-return data to mitigate retrospective clawback risk.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The euro is fully convertible for ordinary current-account transactions, including trade payments, service fees, dividends, interest, royalties, and other legitimate profit remittances. France has no general capital-control regime applicable to a French SAS. Capital contributions, intercompany loans, acquisitions, dividend distributions, and repatriations may generally be settled cross-border without prior foreign-exchange approval, subject to corporate-law solvency and distribution rules, tax withholding and beneficial-ownership requirements, anti-money-laundering controls, sanctions, transfer-pricing documentation, and applicable statistical or regulatory reporting. Cross-border payments involving sanctioned persons, restricted jurisdictions, or unexplained third-party funding may be blocked or require enhanced review. Dividend and interest remittances must be supported by valid corporate resolutions, accounting records, tax analysis, and evidence that the recipient is entitled to the payment.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France has no statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to the Banque de France, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no general statutory deadline requiring conversion of foreign-currency export receipts into euros. Export receipts may be retained in foreign-currency accounts, subject to bank policy, accounting requirements, sanctions screening, tax reporting, and applicable EU and French statistical reporting. Cash or bearer negotiable instruments physically entering or leaving the European Union at or above EUR 10,000 must be declared to customs. Banks may impose contractual settlement, documentation, or conversion requirements, but those are not general statutory surrender rules.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France, operating within the Eurosystem under the monetary authority of the European Central Bank; foreign-exchange and financial-sanctions policy is also governed by the European Union and administered in France principally through the French Treasury and relevant supervisory authorities.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for supplementary payment operations and low-to-medium as the sole treasury bank for a regulated proprietary HFT business. A European Economic Area electronic-money institution or payment institution may provide euro payment accounts, SEPA transfers, payment services, and, where permitted, foreign-currency functionality through passported authorization or local authorization. An EMI does not provide a bank deposit, central-bank money, or ordinary lending, and customer funds must be safeguarded rather than treated as deposits. Account termination, transaction limits, restricted-country policies, limited foreign-exchange products, and inability to support securities settlement, margin, exchange connectivity, or institutional credit can materially impair HFT operations. Offshore digital EMIs outside the EEA cannot lawfully provide regulated payment services into France merely by being offshore; they generally require an appropriate EU authorization, passport, French establishment, or regulated intermediary structure. A primary account with a supervised French or EEA credit institution is recommended, with an EMI used only as a controlled secondary rail.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Route equity through the SAS's account at a French or EEA credit institution using SEPA for euro transfers or SWIFT cross-border payment messaging for non-euro transfers. The remitter should be the shareholder or documented financing entity, and the beneficiary name must exactly match the SAS's registered legal name. The payment narrative should identify the legal purpose, such as share-capital subscription, share-premium contribution, shareholder loan, or intercompany loan; France does not impose a single universal foreign-exchange purpose-code system for ordinary corporate transfers. Before release, provide the subscription agreement or loan agreement, corporate resolutions, funding-source evidence, UBO data, tax-residence information, and—during formation—the bank, notary, or Caisse des Dépôts documentation required for capital deposit. Reconcile the incoming funds to the general ledger and preserve the SWIFT UETR, payment confirmation, remitter details, currency, value date, and supporting documents. Apply sanctions, AML, and related-party screening before acceptance and verify whether any foreign lender, shareholder, or financial instrument triggers regulatory reporting.Use SEPA Credit Transfer or SEPA Instant Credit Transfer for eligible euro payments within the SEPA area, and SWIFT cross-border payment messaging for non-SEPA or non-euro payments. ISO 20022 payment messages are progressively replacing legacy formats; an MT103 may still be supported by a bank during transition but should not be treated as the sole future-state standard. For securities, derivatives, exchange, and prime-broker settlements, use the designated clearing broker, central counterparty, settlement bank, or custodian and maintain matched trade records. Commercial letters of credit are legally available for genuine goods transactions but are generally unsuitable for proprietary HFT execution and should not be used to disguise capital, margin, or financial-market settlements. Each payment should have a documented commercial or treasury purpose, verified beneficiary, sanctions screening, invoice or settlement statement where applicable, and segregation between operating expenses, client money, margin, collateral, and proprietary trading funds. Confirm bank cut-off times, value dates, nostro routes, charges, recall procedures, and reconciliation controls.Deliverable foreign-exchange forwards for hedging documented operating, funding, dividend, or settlement exposures, subject to bank counterparty limits, appropriateness and classification requirements, collateral terms, and applicable EMIR reporting or clearing rules.Non-deliverable forwards for currencies with restricted convertibility or where physical delivery is impractical, subject to the bank's approved-country policy, sanctions screening, ISDA documentation, valuation controls, collateral terms, and applicable EMIR requirements.Currency swaps and cross-currency interest-rate swaps for documented liquidity or funding exposures, subject to ISDA or equivalent master documentation, counterparty-credit limits, collateral and margin arrangements, valuation governance, and regulatory reporting.FX options, collars, and other derivatives may be used only for genuine hedging or treasury purposes within the company's regulatory permissions and risk policy; the SAS should document hedge purpose, exposure, valuation, counterparty approval, accounting treatment, and market-abuse controls.Hedging with a French or EEA bank, investment firm, or authorized broker is preferable. A proprietary HFT entity must not use derivatives with clients or offer hedging services to third parties without assessing investment-services authorization, MiFID II classification, EMIR obligations, prudential requirements, and applicable AMF or ACPR supervision.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Medium for supplementary payment operations and low-to-medium as the sole treasury bank for a regulated proprietary HFT business. A European Economic Area electronic-money institution or payment institution may provide euro payment accounts, SEPA transfers, payment services, and, where permitted, foreign-currency functionality through passported authorization or local authorization. An EMI does not provide a bank deposit, central-bank money, or ordinary lending, and customer funds must be safeguarded rather than treated as deposits. Account termination, transaction limits, restricted-country policies, limited foreign-exchange products, and inability to support securities settlement, margin, exchange connectivity, or institutional credit can materially impair HFT operations. Offshore digital EMIs outside the EEA cannot lawfully provide regulated payment services into France merely by being offshore; they generally require an appropriate EU authorization, passport, French establishment, or regulated intermediary structure. A primary account with a supervised French or EEA credit institution is recommended, with an EMI used only as a controlled secondary rail.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% is the general French withholding tax rate on dividends and similar distributions paid by a French company to a non-resident recipient, subject to treaty relief, European Union exemptions, and specific statutory exceptions. A 75% rate may apply to payments to recipients in non-cooperative states or territories in circumstances covered by French law.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a financial year ending on 31 December, the electronic corporate income tax return is generally due by the second business day following 1 May of the following year. For a financial year ending on another date, the return is generally due within three months after the financial year-end. The balancing payment is generally due by the applicable corporate income tax payment deadline, commonly 15 May for calendar-year taxpayers. Special rules apply to companies entering liquidation, ceasing activity, or undergoing certain reorganizations.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Instalments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September, and 15 December. The final balance is generally due by the payment deadline associated with the annual return, commonly 15 May for calendar-year taxpayers.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: EUR 150 million. Full Local File and Master File obligations generally apply where the French entity or French tax group exceeds the statutory turnover or gross-assets threshold, or where it is controlled by or controls an entity meeting the relevant threshold. Entities below the full-documentation threshold remain subject to the arm's-length principle and may have simplified documentation obligations. Country-by-Country Reporting generally applies to multinational groups with consolidated revenue of at least EUR 750 million.

Economic substance scope: France does not operate a single, standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore substance regimes. Substance is nevertheless tested through the French general anti-abuse rule, abuse-of-law doctrine, beneficial-ownership requirements, permanent-establishment principles, transfer-pricing rules, anti-conduit concepts, principal-purpose test provisions, controlled-foreign-company rules, anti-hybrid rules, and requirements that deductions reflect genuine business expenditure. A French HFT SAS should maintain French decision-making capacity, appropriately qualified personnel, risk controls, technology governance, trading authorization, books and records, effective management, and operational control proportionate to its activities. Artificial booking of trading profits, financing margins, or intellectual-property royalties without corresponding functions and risks is high risk.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Tax Return

Statutory sanction: Administrative reassessment, 10%, 40%, or 80% penalties depending on conduct, 0.20% monthly late-payment interest, enforced collection, and criminal tax-fraud penalties. Ordinary tax fraud may carry up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 500,000 fine; aggravated circumstances, including organized fraud or use of accounts or entities abroad, may increase exposure to seven years' imprisonment and a EUR 3 million fine.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto managers may incur criminal liability, personal tax liability in cases of fraudulent or repeated obstruction of collection, civil damages, asset seizure, and professional disqualification. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may be ordered in criminal proceedings or through judicial control. The corporate veil is not routinely pierced solely because of ownership, but personal liability may arise from personal participation, serious mismanagement, fraudulent insolvency, or abuse of corporate assets.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation and Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: Income adjustment to arm's-length profit, denial of deductions, withholding tax assessment, late-payment interest, and transfer-pricing penalties. Failure to provide required documentation may attract a penalty generally linked to the higher of a statutory minimum and a percentage of reassessed profits, subject to applicable caps and procedural rules.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil liability for deliberate misstatements, criminal tax-fraud exposure where concealment is intentional, and liability for organizing insolvency or obstructing an audit. Ordinary transfer-pricing disagreement does not by itself create criminal liability.
Unlicensed Investment Services and Algorithmic Trading

Statutory sanction: Administrative sanctions, prohibition or suspension of activity, withdrawal of authorization, market-access restrictions, financial penalties, disgorgement, and possible criminal sanctions under French financial and monetary law. Market-abuse penalties may reach substantial amounts calculated by reference to profit, loss avoided, or statutory maximums.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible managers may be personally sanctioned by the AMF or prosecuted for unauthorized provision of regulated services, market manipulation, obstruction, or false reporting. Management bans, professional disqualification, asset seizure, and imprisonment may apply depending on the offense.
Market Abuse and Manipulative Trading

Statutory sanction: AMF administrative sanctions may include injunctions, publication of the decision, professional restrictions, and financial penalties that can reach EUR 100 million or ten times the profit gained or loss avoided for certain market-abuse violations. Criminal penalties may include imprisonment and fines, subject to the offense and applicable double-prosecution limitations.

Director personal liability: Traders, executives, compliance officers, and directors may be personally prosecuted where they authorized, directed, knowingly tolerated, or failed to prevent the conduct in breach of applicable duties. Civil restitution, confiscation, professional bans, and imprisonment may apply.
Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing

Statutory sanction: Supervisory measures, injunctions, business restrictions, administrative fines, confiscation, and criminal money-laundering penalties. Money laundering may carry up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 500,000 fine, with aggravated forms carrying higher penalties, including up to ten years' imprisonment and increased fines.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers may be personally liable for knowing participation, complicity, willful blindness, or serious failure of mandated controls. Asset freezing, confiscation, management prohibition, civil restitution, and imprisonment may apply. Travel restrictions may be ordered in criminal proceedings.
Shell Company, Beneficial Ownership, and Substance Failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, dividend or royalty withholding at domestic rates, tax reassessment, abuse-of-law penalties, beneficial-owner disclosure sanctions, corporate dissolution or management restrictions in serious cases, and possible criminal fraud or money-laundering charges.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for false corporate filings, misuse of corporate assets, fraudulent management, tax fraud, and money laundering. Civil restitution, confiscation, professional disqualification, and imprisonment may follow where intentional conduct is established.
Withholding Tax and Profit Repatriation Failure

Statutory sanction: Recovery of unpaid withholding tax from the French payer, late-payment interest, penalties generally ranging from 10% to 80% depending on conduct, and potential personal liability for intentional non-compliance.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for fraudulent withholding-tax avoidance, deliberate failure to remit tax, or distributions made contrary to corporate-law restrictions. Ordinary administrative error generally produces corporate rather than personal liability, absent fraud or gross misconduct.
Corporate Asset Misuse and Illegal Distributions

Statutory sanction: Repayment, annulment of distributions, tax reassessment, penalties, damages, and criminal sanctions for misuse of corporate assets. In an SAS, prohibited or abusive financial assistance and related corporate offenses may also trigger sanctions under French company law.

Director personal liability: Presidents, directors, and de facto managers may face civil liability, disqualification, confiscation, and imprisonment. The corporate veil may be disregarded where the company is used as an instrument of fraud or where personal and corporate assets are deliberately confused.
Books, Records, Audit Obstruction, and False Reporting

Statutory sanction: Tax penalties, adverse assessment based on available information, audit-extension consequences, administrative sanctions, obstruction penalties, and criminal prosecution for false statements or destruction of evidence.

Director personal liability: Directors, officers, traders, and compliance personnel may be personally prosecuted for obstruction, falsification, or destruction of evidence. Imprisonment, fines, professional bans, and confiscation may apply.
Insolvency, Asset Stripping, and Fraudulent Preference

Statutory sanction: Clawback of transactions, insolvency proceedings, personal contribution to insufficient assets, management bans, director liability for shortfall, and criminal bankruptcy or fraudulent insolvency sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto managers may be ordered to contribute personally to the company's liabilities, prohibited from managing companies, subjected to asset seizure and confiscation, and imprisoned for fraudulent bankruptcy or related offenses.
