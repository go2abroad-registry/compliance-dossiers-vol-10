Registered Agent & Physical Address Licensed Agent Required

A French SAS must have a registered office in France under Code de commerce Article L123-11 and related provisions. Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum capital applies to an SAS. At least 50% of cash contributions must be paid upon incorporation, with the balance payable within five years. Cash contributions must be deposited into a blocked account with a bank, notary, or Caisse des Dépôts et Consignations; the depositary issues a certificate of deposit. The funds are released after registration and presentation of the Kbis extract. In-kind contributions require valuation by a commissaire aux apports unless the statutory exemption conditions are satisfied.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP), acting through the relevant Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE) and the Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères (SIEE) where applicable. Official tax portal: https://www.impots.gouv.fr. Corporate formation and registration filings are submitted through the Guichet unique des formalités des entreprises operated by the Institut national de la propriété industrielle (INPI): https://formalites.entreprises.gouv.fr.

E-government filing portal

A French SAS obtains its corporate identifiers through the INPI Guichet unique filing, followed by registration in the Registre national des entreprises and, where applicable, the Registre du commerce et des sociétés. The entity receives a SIREN number for the legal person and a SIRET number for each establishment. The French VAT identification number is issued by the competent SIE and normally has the format FR plus a two-digit control key and the nine-digit SIREN number. The entity must select its VAT regime and provide constitutional documents, registered-office evidence, legal-representative identification, activity information, bank details where requested, and evidence of business operations. A foreign entity without a French establishment applies to the SIEE for VAT registration using the applicable non-established-business process. An EU-established taxable person generally does not require a French fiscal representative; a non-EU-established taxable person generally must appoint a French fiscal representative unless an applicable mutual-assistance arrangement or statutory exception applies. VAT registration does not replace customs registration: an operator importing or exporting goods generally requires an Economic Operators Registration and Identification (EORI) number obtained through French customs.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A French SAS is ordinarily subject to French corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés, or IS) on its taxable profits. There is no United States-style check-the-box election and no general entity-classification election permitting unrestricted partnership treatment. Subject to statutory eligibility conditions, an SAS may elect temporary taxation under the personal income tax regime (impôt sur le revenu, or IR) for a maximum period of five years; the election generally requires that the company be newly formed, privately held, actively carrying on a commercial, industrial, craft or professional activity, and satisfy employee, turnover, balance-sheet and ownership conditions. The election must be made within the statutory filing period and is subject to shareholder and management requirements. France also implements anti-hybrid-mismatch rules under the Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive framework; these rules are corrective provisions and do not constitute a general classification election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

For the French domestic VAT franchise regime, the principal annual turnover threshold for activities involving supplies of goods is generally EUR 85,000, with a higher tolerance threshold generally applicable under the relevant tax-year rules. Services have separate lower thresholds. The threshold does not eliminate VAT obligations for intra-EU acquisitions, imports, certain reverse-charge transactions, or transactions for which the business voluntarily opts for a taxable regime. Thresholds are periodically amended; the applicable threshold must be verified for the relevant tax year and activity classification. A French SAS manufacturing and selling dialysis consumables will generally operate under a VAT-liable regime rather than rely on the franchise threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods physically dispatched or transported from France to a destination outside the European Union may qualify for VAT exemption with the right to deduct input VAT, provided the exporter can prove that the goods left the EU customs territory and that the export transaction is properly documented. The exporter should obtain the customs exit confirmation, normally through the electronic customs system, and retain the export declaration, Movement Reference Number (MRN), transport evidence, commercial invoice, packing records, delivery evidence and proof of the purchaser and destination. Intra-EU dispatches are generally not exports for French VAT purposes and are subject instead to the rules for exempt intra-Community supplies, including valid customer VAT identification and transport evidence. Services are not automatically zero-rated: B2B services supplied to a customer established outside France may be outside the scope of French VAT under the place-of-supply rules, while specific services connected with exports or international transport may qualify for exemption only if their statutory conditions are satisfied. Input VAT refunds require valid VAT invoices, a direct link to taxable transactions and compliance with the applicable French VAT return and refund procedures.

  • French or EU electronic customs export declaration
  • Movement Reference Number (MRN) and electronic confirmation of exit from the EU customs territory
  • Commercial invoice identifying the exporter, purchaser, goods, value, delivery terms and destination
  • Packing list and product-identification records, including applicable medical-device product references
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, CMR consignment note or equivalent transport document
  • Proof of delivery or other reliable evidence linking the goods to the non-EU destination
  • Export contract, purchase order and shipping instructions
  • Evidence supporting the customs classification, origin and any required export authorisations
  • Customer VAT identification evidence where the transaction is an intra-EU supply rather than a third-country export
  • Payment and accounting records sufficient to reconcile the export with the VAT return; a French inward-remittance certificate is not generally a universal statutory condition for export VAT exemption

France does not currently operate a universal real-time pre-clearance model for ordinary domestic B2B invoices. The French electronic-invoicing reform provides that all French-established businesses must be able to receive electronic invoices from 1 September 2026; issuance obligations apply to large enterprises and certain larger groups from 1 September 2026 and to small and medium-sized enterprises and micro-enterprises from 1 September 2027, subject to the final statutory implementation timetable. Invoices must generally be transmitted through an approved private platform (plateforme de dématérialisation partenaire, or PDP) and reported through the French public invoicing infrastructure for applicable transaction and payment data. Mandatory e-reporting applies to specified B2C and cross-border transactions. Public-sector invoices are processed through Chorus Pro. There is no general requirement for a digital tax stamp or continuous invoice-by-invoice fiscal clearance for ordinary French domestic transactions, although invoices must satisfy French VAT content, numbering, retention and audit-trail requirements.

France has no general foreign-exchange control rule requiring export proceeds to be repatriated within 180 days. Export proceeds must nevertheless be recorded accurately, and banking, customs, accounting and VAT evidence must be consistent. If the exporter cannot prove that goods exited the EU or incorrectly applies an export exemption, the DGFiP may assess the VAT that should have been charged, together with late-payment interest and applicable penalties. The standard penalty for deliberate non-compliance may be 40% of the reassessed tax, while an 80% penalty may apply in cases involving fraudulent manoeuvres or abuse of rights; other penalties may apply for concealment, inaccurate declarations, repeated failures or obstruction of an audit. Input VAT refunds may be denied or clawed back, and previously exempt export transactions may be retrospectively reassessed. Serious fraudulent conduct may also expose responsible persons to criminal tax-fraud sanctions, including imprisonment and substantial fines. Customs violations may result in additional duties, administrative fines, seizure or forfeiture, and other sanctions under the Union Customs Code and French customs law.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France uses the euro and benefits from full current-account and capital-account convertibility within the euro area and under applicable European Union law. A French SAS may generally receive foreign investment, hold foreign-currency accounts, make cross-border payments, purchase foreign currency, remit dividends, repay shareholder loans, and pay royalties or service fees to non-residents without a general exchange-control approval. Payments remain subject to corporate-law requirements, distributable-profit and solvency limitations, transfer-pricing rules, withholding-tax analysis, beneficial-ownership verification, AML/CFT controls, sanctions screening, and bank documentation. Dividend distributions require approved annual accounts and a lawful shareholder resolution. Cross-border related-party payments must be commercially supported and may require withholding-tax relief documentation, treaty forms, beneficial-owner evidence, and transfer-pricing support. Certain foreign direct investments in strategic sectors may require French prior authorization, including activities affecting public order, public security, national defence, or protected technologies; ordinary dialysis-consumables manufacturing is not automatically restricted, but an analysis is required if the business involves controlled technologies, sensitive data, or protected infrastructure. France and the European Union impose financial sanctions, asset freezes, payment restrictions, and export controls that can prevent otherwise lawful transactions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France has no general statutory requirement to surrender export proceeds to the Banque de France, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no general statutory deadline requiring exporters to convert foreign-currency receipts into euros. Export proceeds may generally remain in foreign-currency accounts or be converted at the company's discretion, subject to the company's banking agreement, accounting rules, tax reporting, sanctions controls, and contractual requirements. Export invoices and receipts should be supported by commercial documentation, customs records, transport evidence, and payment references. Banque de France statistical or balance-of-payments reporting may apply through financial institutions or reporting systems for specified cross-border transactions, particularly where reporting thresholds or regulatory surveys are triggered. French customs, VAT, Intrastat, and European Union statistical reporting obligations remain separate from exchange-control requirements.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France, acting within the Eurosystem under the authority of the European Central Bank; foreign-exchange and capital-movement policy is principally governed by European Union law, French monetary and financial legislation, and the French Treasury Directorate-General of the Treasury (Direction générale du Trésor). The Autorité de contrôle prudentiel et de résolution supervises French credit institutions and investment firms for prudential and AML/CFT purposes.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium for operational payments and low to medium as the sole treasury bank. An electronic-money institution or payment institution licensed in France or another European Economic Area state may provide payment accounts, SEPA transfers, foreign-exchange services, and certain cross-border payment functions under passporting or local authorization. It may not necessarily provide a French credit institution deposit account, capital-deposit escrow certificate, full lending facilities, documentary-credit capability, or unrestricted cash-management services. Client-money safeguarding is not equivalent to bank-deposit protection. Offshore digital EMIs outside the European Economic Area generally cannot provide regulated payment services to French customers without an applicable French or EEA authorization, passport, branch, or agent structure. Banks and EMIs may restrict high-value medical-device payments, third-country corridors, cash deposits, nested payments, or transactions lacking economic-purpose evidence. A regulated French or EEA bank should remain the primary account for share-capital deposit, payroll, taxes, customs, regulated trade finance, and contingency liquidity.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: EUR 50 million (annual sales or purchases with related parties within a single tax period trigger mandatory full transfer pricing documentation, comprising both the Master File and the Local File, under Articles L.13 AA and L.13 AB of the French Book of Tax Procedures), or EUR 400 million in balance sheet total; alternatively, a simplified documentation obligation applies under Article L.13 AB where the entity's annual net turnover exceeds EUR 50 million but the ownership of related-party transactions is below a lower threshold, in which case a summary statement suffices. For the dialysis hemofilter manufacturer, if consolidated group revenue exceeds EUR 750 million, full master file obligations attach.

Economic substance scope: France applies the economic substance doctrine through general anti-abuse rules, including the substance-over-form principle, the anti-abuse measures of the EU Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive (ATAD) transposed into French law, and the arm's-length standard of Article 9 of the OECD Model Tax Convention as interpreted by the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines. While France is not a low-tax offshore jurisdiction, the EU Economic Substance Regulations applicable to certain offshore financial centres are not directly applicable; however, the French tax administration will challenge any legal entity or PE lacking real French substance (managerial, operational premises, employees, commercial risk) under the abuse of law doctrine of Article L.64 of the French Book of Tax Procedures. A French SAS engaged in the manufacture of disposable dialysis hemofilters must maintain real operational substance: a registered office in France, board of directors or executive management located in France or genuinely exercising decision-making in France, an industrial establishment with manufacturing equipment and inventory of hollow fiber polyethersulfone dialyzers, qualified technical staff, bank accounts, and substantial invoice, logistics, and accounting activity. The entity must not be a letterbox or conduit; failure to demonstrate this substance may cause the tax administration to disregard the French entity and reattribute its income to a foreign shareholder or treat it as an artificial arrangement under the ATAD interest limitation, controlled foreign company (CFC), and anti-hybrid rules.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate Tax Evasion and Concealment of Taxable Income

Statutory sanction: Under Article 1741 of the French General Tax Code, tax evasion by fraud is punishable by imprisonment of up to 5 years and a fine of EUR 250,000 (increased to EUR 500,000 and up to 7 years imprisonment where the crime was committed within an organised gang). Additional fiscal penalties under Article 1729 can amount to 80% of the evaded tax for deliberate fraud with abusive maneuvers (40% is the standard bad-faith penalty and 80% is for manoeuvres frauduleuses) plus the applicable late-payment interest of 0.20% per month. The court may also order exclusion from public procurement, posting of judgment, confiscation of bank accounts and assets, and the prohibition to manage a company for up to 5 years.

Director personal liability: Corporate veil may be pierced under the abuse of law doctrine and liability for tax debts under Article L.267 of the French Book of Tax Procedures where the director or manager has engaged in successive serious irregularities that render the company unable to pay its tax debt. Directors face civil liability for restitution to the tax administration, personal bankruptcy consequences, national travel bans or seizure of personal assets, and, in the most serious cases, imprisonment under Article 1741. In criminal proceedings, the actual signatory of the return or the de facto manager is personally liable.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation and Offshore Profit Shifting

Statutory sanction: The French tax administration may recharacterize the transfer prices and make an upward adjustment to the French taxable base under the conditions of Article 57 CGI, applying a 25% corporate income tax rate plus late-payment interest of 0.20% per month, and the 40% or 80% penalty for bad faith or fraud. Additional sanctions include a possible 5% penalty under Article 1758 A for failure to file the transfer pricing documentation after a formal demand, or EUR 10,000 per year. Failure to produce the requested documentation may also establish a presumption of gross negligence with the corresponding 40% penalty.

Director personal liability: Directors and corporate officers who knowingly signed or participated in the false transfer pricing reports can be held jointly and severally liable for the additional assessments under Article L.267 if they committed serious misconduct by organizing the artificial transfer of profits abroad. Unpaid withholding taxes on deemed distributions to foreign shareholders may be recovered against the directors personally. Criminal prosecution for tax fraud may lead to 5 years imprisonment and EUR 250,000 fine per individual, and travel bans preventing the director from leaving France.
Artificial Shell Company / Lack of Economic Substance

Statutory sanction: Under the general anti-abuse rule (article L.64 LPF) the tax administration may disregard the legal form and subject the French entity's worldwide income to tax in France if it is deemed to have its effective place of management in France or if the entity is determined to be a sham or artificial arrangement. Reclassification can lead to corporate income tax reassessment at 25%, plus penalties up to 80%, interest, and the restitution of claimed tax credits (e.g., CIR). Failure to satisfy the EU ATAD CFC rules can result in attribution of the foreign subsidiary's income to the French entity at the 25% rate; the company may also be struck from the Trade and Companies Register for non-compliance and subject to compulsory liquidation proceedings.

Director personal liability: A de jure or de facto director who permits the company to operate as a shell could be held civilly liable for unpaid corporate taxes (Article L.267) and for the entire additional assessment. Personal exposure may include bankruptcy, disqualification from directing, travel bans to prevent leaving the jurisdiction while debts are being investigated, and imprisonment under Article 1741 where the shell arrangement is a vehicle for fraud.
Improper Dividend Repatriation and Withholding Tax Evasion

Statutory sanction: The French tax administration will recalculate the withholding tax due, charge the late-payment interest at 0.20% per month, and apply a bad-faith penalty of 40% or 80% for fraudulent maneuvers (Article 1729). Under Article 1732, failure to declare dividend distributions may attract an automatic penalty equal to 5% of the amounts omitted, and under Article 1759, a penalty of 100% of the amount that was paid from France in the absence of the required withholding, recoverable by legal action from the paying company. In cases of abusive arrangements, the additional tax may be denied for deduction at the parent level by the receiving country's CFC rules.

Director personal liability: The president or managing director of the SAS is personally liable for the withholding tax debt if the distribution was implemented without proper due diligence, especially where the director knew that the recipient was not eligible for treaty relief. Criminal charges for concealment of dividends can result in imprisonment and fines; the director may be personally sued for the 100% withholding penalty and barred from leaving France while the tax debt is pending.
Product Licensing, Registration, and Health-Industry Compliance Risk Interfacing with Tax Position

Statutory sanction: The French tax administration can deny tax benefits, including the research tax credit, on the grounds that the research and production activity is not a lawful or permitted activity or that the expenditure is not incurred in a genuine industrial and commercial setting. The company may be subject to administrative sanctions by the Agence nationale de sécurité du médicament et des produits de santé (ANSM), recall orders, prohibition on manufacture, and a fine of up to EUR 5 million under consumer and public health codes. Fiscal recalcitrance may be treated as an aggravating circumstance in health fraud prosecutions.

Director personal liability: Directors can face personal liability under health law for marketing non-compliant medical devices, including imprisonment of up to 2 years and fines of up to EUR 150,000 under the French Public Health Code. Under tax law, if the same persons used compliance deficiencies to present an insolvent company to escape tax, they can be held personally liable and subject to director disqualification, travel restriction, and restitution of all tax benefits improperly obtained.
