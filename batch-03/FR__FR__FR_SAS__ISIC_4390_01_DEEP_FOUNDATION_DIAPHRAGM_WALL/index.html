Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum paid-up capital for an SAS; capital is freely set in the Articles, often €1. Cash contributions must be effectively paid and deposited in a blocked account with a bank, notary, or the Caisse des Dépôts before registration; at least 50% of cash contributions is released upon incorporation, and the balance shall be paid into the company's account within five years. Non-cash (in-kind) contributions are vested at once and must be fully described in the Articles.

Funding & unlock path: Fire safety: obtain a fire safety certificate (certificat de sécurité) for site offices and any enclosed premises, with adequate extinguishing equipment and evacuation plans. Structural clearance: ensure the design, inspection, and testing of temporary works, sheet piles, diaphragm wall guides, cranes, and shoring comply with the Eurocodes and the employer's safety dossier (plan de prévention). Occupational clearance: all workers assigned to deep foundation operations must pass pre-employment medical examination through the service de santé au travail, and the firm must register with the French construction prevention or OPPBTP where applicable.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP), through the official impots.gouv.fr portal and the competent Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE). VAT registration and filings for non-established businesses are handled by the designated non-resident business tax service. Customs matters are administered separately by the Direction générale des douanes et droits indirects (DGDDI), including through douane.gouv.fr.

E-government filing portal

Before commencing taxable activity in France, submit the applicable business-registration formalities through the Guichet unique operated by INPI and request VAT registration from the competent SIE. A French-established SAS receives a SIREN and establishment SIRET through the registration process; its French VAT number generally takes the form FR plus a two-character key and the SIREN. A foreign entity without a French establishment applies directly to the competent non-resident VAT service, supplying its incorporation and tax-residence evidence, business activities, identification details, and anticipated French transactions. An EU-established applicant may generally register directly. A non-EU applicant generally must appoint a French fiscal representative, subject to applicable mutual-assistance exceptions. A VAT number is required before making transactions for which French VAT registration is mandatory. Registration does not itself determine whether a permanent establishment or corporate-tax filing obligation exists.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A French SAS is ordinarily subject to French corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés, or IS); there is no general US-style check-the-box election. A qualifying newly formed SAS may elect temporary taxation under the income-tax regime (impôt sur le revenu, or IR) for up to five financial years if statutory ownership, activity, age, and size conditions are met. The election is subject to deadlines and is not available solely by choice to every SAS. No general hybrid-mismatch classification election replaces the statutory French classification; anti-hybrid rules may deny deductions or impose inclusion under applicable French and EU law.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

No general turnover threshold protects a non-established business from French VAT registration where it makes taxable supplies in France for which it is liable to account for VAT. French small-business franchise thresholds apply only where their statutory conditions are met; they are not a general registration safe harbor for a foreign contractor. Construction and installation transactions may be subject to specific place-of-supply, invoicing, and domestic reverse-charge rules depending on the parties and transaction. Confirm the current threshold and treatment for the relevant tax period before relying on a small-business exemption.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Goods dispatched or transported from France to a destination outside the European Union may qualify for VAT exemption with a right to deduct (commonly described as zero-rating) when statutory export conditions are satisfied and the supplier retains reliable evidence of export. Intra-EU supplies are governed by separate exemption conditions, including valid customer VAT identification and evidence of transport. Services are not zero-rated merely because the customer or payment is offshore: the place-of-supply rules determine whether French VAT applies. B2B services supplied to a taxable customer established outside France are often outside the French VAT scope under the general rule, subject to exceptions; services directly connected with French immovable property, including relevant construction work, are generally located where the property is situated. A deep-foundation or diaphragm-wall contract performed on French land therefore requires transaction-specific analysis and ordinarily is not an export of services solely because the customer is non-resident.

  • Customs export declaration and MRN or other official evidence that the goods exited the EU customs territory
  • Commercial invoice identifying the supplier, customer, goods, VAT treatment, and transaction
  • Transport evidence appropriate to the route, such as bill of lading, airway bill, CMR consignment note, or carrier confirmation
  • Purchase order, contract, packing list, and proof of delivery or receipt where relevant
  • For services, contract, work descriptions, customer business-status evidence, place-of-supply analysis, and evidence supporting any claimed exemption or outside-scope treatment
  • Bank payment and remittance evidence where relevant to substantiating the transaction; France does not generally require an inward remittance certificate as a condition of export VAT exemption

France is implementing mandatory domestic B2B electronic invoicing and transaction/payment e-reporting through approved platforms connected to the DGFiP system. All businesses within scope must be able to receive e-invoices from 1 September 2026; issuance obligations are phased, with large and medium-sized businesses from 1 September 2026 and smaller businesses from 1 September 2027, subject to current statutory implementation rules. The regime is not a general requirement for real-time pre-clearance or digital stamping of every invoice. Required formats, platform transmission, and e-reporting depend on transaction type. Cross-border and certain consumer transactions generally fall under e-reporting rather than the domestic B2B e-invoice issuance obligation. Verify implementation dates and technical specifications before deployment.

France does not impose a general statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline for export proceeds. A zero-rating or exemption claim unsupported by the required conditions and evidence may be denied, with assessed VAT, late-payment interest, and tax penalties. Deliberate fraud or false documentation can trigger enhanced tax penalties and, where applicable, criminal prosecution. Input VAT refunds may be adjusted or recovered if the underlying transactions do not qualify or supporting records are inadequate. Retain invoices, customs and transport records, contracts, accounting records, and VAT returns for the applicable statutory period.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France maintains a liberalized capital account regime fully aligned with EU capital movement directives. Current account transactions are freely convertible at market rates. Capital account transactions including profit remittances and capital injections require no prior approval but are subject to mandatory reporting thresholds and enhanced AML/KYC verification. Profit remittance hurdles are limited to standard tax clearance, beneficial ownership documentation, and Banque de France statistical reporting obligations.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender or conversion of export proceeds required under French law. Export receipts may be retained in foreign currency. Transactions exceeding €100,000 must be reported to the Banque de France via the SIE electronic reporting system for statistical purposes. Conversion to EUR occurs at prevailing market rates without statutory fixed ratios or surrender requirements.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% corporate income tax. A 3.3% social contribution on corporate income tax may apply to companies with annual turnover of at least EUR 7.63 million and corporate income tax exceeding EUR 763,000, producing a maximum combined effective rate of approximately 25.83%. A reduced 15% rate may apply to qualifying small and medium-sized companies on the first EUR 42,500 of taxable profit, subject to ownership, turnover, paid-in-capital, and capital-payment conditions.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The ordinary French withholding tax rate on dividends paid to non-resident recipients is generally 25%, subject to domestic exemptions, EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive implementation, tax treaties, and anti-abuse rules. Dividends paid to entities located in or connected with a French non-cooperative state or territory may be subject to a 75% rate unless a statutory exception applies. A qualifying EU or European Economic Area corporate parent may obtain exemption where the ownership, holding-period, subject-to-tax, beneficial-ownership, anti-abuse, and documentation conditions are satisfied.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a financial year ending on 31 December, the corporate income tax return is generally due by the second business day following 1 May of the following year, subject to the electronic filing timetable and any officially published filing tolerance. For a year-end other than 31 December, the return is generally due within three months after the end of the relevant financial year. A French SAS must also file its annual accounts with the competent registry within the statutory corporate-law deadline.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Installments are generally payable on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September, and 15 December. The final balance is generally payable by the date applicable to the annual corporate income tax return. The exact due date may vary where the accounting year does not end on 31 December or where statutory payment exceptions apply.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory full transfer-pricing documentation generally applies where the French entity or French tax group has annual pre-tax turnover or gross assets of at least EUR 400 million, or where specified ownership or control links exist with an entity meeting the EUR 400 million threshold. The documentation obligation can also arise under other statutory conditions, including membership in a qualifying group. A transfer-pricing declaration and summary may apply at lower thresholds or under separate conditions. The Local File and Master File should be maintained contemporaneously and supplied within the statutory response period during an audit.

Economic substance scope: France does not operate a separate general-purpose Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to some offshore jurisdictions. French anti-abuse, beneficial-ownership, principal-purpose, abuse-of-law, controlled-foreign-company, anti-hybrid, interest-limitation, and permanent-establishment rules nevertheless require genuine commercial substance. The SAS should maintain French decision-making, personnel or operational capacity proportionate to its business, premises or effective operating arrangements, bank and accounting records, contract-performance evidence, construction-site documentation, asset ownership or control, and commercial risk assumption. Conduit companies, circular financing, artificial royalty chains, non-arm's-length management charges, and arrangements lacking business purpose may be denied treaty benefits or deductions and may trigger reassessment and penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Accounting

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, late-payment interest, penalties generally up to 80% for fraudulent maneuvers, and criminal tax-fraud penalties that may include imprisonment and substantial fines. Aggravated fraud involving organized concealment, offshore structures, or false documentation may attract enhanced criminal sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto managers may incur criminal liability, civil restitution, tax-liability proceedings for fraudulent or seriously negligent conduct, management bans, and asset-seizure measures. Imprisonment is fact-dependent and not automatic. A general travel ban is not a standard tax penalty, but judicial restrictions may arise in criminal proceedings or from immigration and enforcement measures.
Shell Company, Artificial Interposition, or Treaty Abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, withholding-tax reassessment, disallowance of deductions, abuse-of-law penalties generally up to 80% where the statutory conditions are met, late-payment interest, and possible criminal referral for fraudulent documentation.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal civil liability for intentional or gross misconduct, criminal liability for participation in fraud, management bans, and restitution of improperly avoided tax. Corporate personality does not protect a director from personal liability for their own unlawful conduct.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Primary transfer-pricing adjustment, withholding-tax exposure, denial of deductions, interest limitation, penalties for incomplete or missing documentation, and possible 40% or 80% penalties where deliberate or fraudulent conduct is established.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable where they knowingly approved false documentation, abusive payments, or diversion of corporate assets. Criminal liability requires proof of the relevant offense and personal participation or complicity.
Permanent Establishment Concealment

Statutory sanction: French corporate income tax and business-tax reassessment, payroll and social-security exposure, VAT reassessment, withholding obligations, interest, penalties, and possible permanent-establishment anti-abuse findings.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil liability, employment and social-security sanctions, and criminal exposure for deliberate concealment or false declarations. A travel ban or imprisonment is not automatic and depends on judicial findings.
VAT and Construction-Sector Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: VAT reassessment, late-payment interest, 5%, 10%, 40%, or 80% penalties depending on the conduct, invoice-related penalties, denial of input VAT, and potential criminal proceedings for organized VAT fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur personal liability for fraudulent VAT conduct or serious repeated non-compliance, including civil restitution, management bans, asset measures, and imprisonment where criminal liability is established.
Anti-Money Laundering and Beneficial-Ownership Failure

Statutory sanction: Regulatory sanctions, transaction blocking, suspicious-transaction reporting consequences, professional or licensing restrictions, confiscation, fines, and criminal money-laundering penalties where the underlying conduct is proven.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for knowing participation, facilitation, or negligent governance where a statutory duty is breached. Criminal sanctions can include imprisonment and fines; travel restrictions may arise only through judicial measures.
Corruption, Procurement, and Public-Contracting Misconduct

Statutory sanction: Criminal fines, imprisonment, confiscation, exclusion from public procurement, contract termination, damages, and corporate compliance-monitoring measures. Accounting entries for bribes are non-deductible.

Director personal liability: Directors, employees, intermediaries, and beneficial owners may be prosecuted personally. Civil restitution, management bans, asset confiscation, judicial restrictions, and imprisonment may apply according to the offense and individual participation.
Corporate Asset Diversion and Improper Profit Repatriation

Statutory sanction: Recovery of improper distributions, corporate and withholding-tax reassessment, denial of deductions, interest, penalties, and potential criminal liability for misuse of corporate assets or fraudulent bankruptcy conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors may be required to restore diverted assets, compensate creditors, bear liability for insufficient assets in insolvency proceedings, and face management bans or imprisonment where the relevant criminal offense is proven.
False Corporate Records and Filing Misrepresentation

Statutory sanction: Administrative reassessment, filing penalties, document-forgery sanctions, corporate-law penalties, criminal fines, and imprisonment depending on the document and intent.

Director personal liability: The individual signatory and responsible officers may be prosecuted independently of the SAS. Personal civil damages, restitution, management bans, judicial restrictions, and imprisonment may apply where intentional falsification is established.
Unlicensed or Non-Compliant Regulated Construction Activity

Statutory sanction: Administrative orders, work suspension, fines, contract cancellation, exclusion from public procurement, remediation costs, insurance denial, and criminal sanctions for serious safety or environmental violations.

Director personal liability: Directors and site managers may face personal criminal and civil liability for workplace injury, environmental damage, fraudulent certification, or deliberate regulatory avoidance, including fines and imprisonment in serious cases.
