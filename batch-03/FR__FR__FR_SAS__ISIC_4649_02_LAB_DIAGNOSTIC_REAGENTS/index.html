Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP), principally through the Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE) and the impots.gouv.fr professional portal; customs and import-export registrations are administered by the Direction générale des douanes et droits indirects (DGDDI) through the French customs online services, including DELTA IE and SOPRANO.

E-government filing portal

A French SAS is registered through the Guichet unique des formalités des entreprises operated by the Institut national de la propriété industrielle (INPI). The filing generates or confirms the legal entity's SIREN and establishment SIRET identifiers. The entity then receives its tax registration and SIE assignment and activates its professional account on impots.gouv.fr. The French VAT identification number is issued by the competent SIE after the business activity, establishment, taxable transactions, expected turnover, banking information, beneficial ownership and supporting corporate documents have been reviewed. The French VAT number normally consists of the prefix FR, a two-digit key, and the nine-digit SIREN. A non-established foreign entity must apply to the Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères (SIEE) where it carries out taxable transactions in France, unless a French fiscal representative or a reverse-charge rule removes the registration obligation. The applicant generally submits corporate registration evidence, constitutional documents, proof of business activity, tax residence information, identity and authority documents for representatives, banking details, transaction-flow information, and customs or logistics evidence where imports or exports are involved. An EORI number is required for customs operations and is obtained through French customs procedures; an EORI is separate from the VAT number. EU-established businesses may use applicable Union One Stop Shop procedures for qualifying intra-EU distance sales and services, but OSS does not replace all French domestic VAT registration obligations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A SAS is generally subject to French corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés, IS) at entity level. The ordinary corporate income tax rate is 25%, subject to statutory conditions and specialized regimes. A closely held SAS may elect temporarily for partnership-style taxation under Article 239 bis AB of the French Tax Code, generally for a maximum five-year period and subject to conditions concerning ownership, age, size, activity and shareholder participation. The election is not an unrestricted US-style check-the-box election. A SAS may also be subject to special tax regimes, including tax consolidation, the participation-exemption regime, qualifying group restructurings, and specific innovation or territorial incentive regimes where statutory conditions are met. France has no general hybrid check-the-box election allowing an entity to choose disregarded-entity or partnership treatment independently of the statutory French legal-form rules. Anti-hybrid-mismatch provisions implementing ATAD 2 can deny deductions, impose inclusion, or restrict relief for arrangements involving related entities or hybrid instruments and entities.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France operates a full convertibility regime under the euro system: no exchange-control authorization is required for current-account flows (trade, services, royalties) or for capital-account flows (equity injections, intra-group loans, dividend remittances, liquidation proceeds). As an EU and Eurozone member, France applies Article 63 TFEU on free movement of capital, and the euro is legal tender for all contracts. There are no restrictions on the currency of invoicing, no mandatory surrender of export proceeds, no foreign-exchange budget, and no central-bank prior notification for profit remittance. The only practical remittance hurdles are company-law conditions (distributable reserves, recovery of prior losses, statutory audit certification, and the prohibition on distributions where net assets would fall below share capital plus legal reserves) and tax gateways: dividends paid to a non-resident shareholder attract French withholding tax under Article 119 bis C.G.I. unless reduced or eliminated via double-tax treaty relief or under the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive (Article 119 ter C.G.I., 0% withholding for a qualifying EU parent holding at least 5% of the SAS capital for 24 months).

Export proceeds surrender rules

France imposes no export-proceeds surrender rule, no statutory repatriation timeline, and no mandatory euro-conversion ratio. The SAS may invoice in EUR or in any freely negotiated currency, may retain foreign-currency receipts in resident foreign-currency accounts (comptes de devises) opened with an ACPR-licensed credit institution, and may use these balances to settle foreign suppliers or intra-group liabilities without any approval. The only regulatory constraints are (i) the Banque de France balance-of-payments and OFS statistical declarations where cumulative cross-border flows with any non-resident exceed €750,000 in a calendar month, as detailed in ACPR Instruction 2021-G-01; (ii) the banking transparency and sanctions-screening obligations attached to each transfer; and (iii) customs and transfer-pricing documentation duties for the underlying medical-reagent trade. Export documents must substantiate the flow in the bank's file, but no forced conversion or repatriation is legally mandated.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (Bank of France), the national central bank of the Eurosystem, acting as statutory agent of the Ministère de l'Economie, des Finances et de la Souveraineté Industrielle et Numérique and of the Direction Générale du Trésor for exchange-control and balance-of-payments statistical reporting; banking and payment-institution prudential supervision is delegated to the Autorité de Contrôle Prudentiel et de Résolution (ACPR), an autonomous authority attached to the Banque de France. The applicable legal foundations are the Code monétaire et financier (CMF), especially Articles L.151-1 to L.165-1, the Code des relations économiques entre l'étranger et la France, the ACPR Instruction 2021-G-01, and the Banque de France balance-of-payments reporting regulations.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

High viability as secondary operational rails, but legally constrained as to full treasury clearing. French fintechs such as Qonto (payment institution authorized by the ACPR), Shine (Société Générale group subsidiary), and Treezor (banking-as-a-service payment institution) may lawfully deliver French IBAN accounts with SEPA and SWIFT-access capacities, and their EU passporting rights under PSD2 permit cross-border collection in the EEA. They are not authorized to issue documentary letters of credit, do not hold TARGET2 clearing membership, do not offer FX forward hedging, and do not extend credit or guarantee lines; client funds benefit from safeguarding rules rather than the Fonds de Garantie des Dépôts et de Résolution (FGDR) deposit guarantee. The fintech rails are therefore suitable only for payroll, domestic supplier payments, and the collection of local distributor invoices, while the primary import-letter-of-credit and clearing infrastructure must sit with a Tier-1 commercial bank.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% corporate income tax, generally applicable to French SAS entities from fiscal years beginning on or after 1 January 2022.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Dividends paid by a French SAS to a non-resident corporate shareholder are generally subject to a 25% withholding tax under domestic law, subject to applicable treaty relief and exemptions. A qualifying EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive exemption may reduce withholding to 0%, generally requiring qualifying ownership, minimum holding-period, subject-to-tax, anti-abuse, and beneficial-owner conditions. French domestic withholding may also be reduced or eliminated under an applicable tax treaty.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The annual return and tax package are generally due within three months after the financial year-end. For a financial year ending on 31 December, the filing deadline is generally the second working day after 1 May of the following year, subject to the annually published tax calendar. The final balance is generally due by the 15th day of the fourth month following year-end; for a 31 December year-end, the usual deadline is 15 May.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: EUR 150 million threshold, generally based on annual pre-tax turnover or gross assets, or applicable where the French entity is controlled by or controls an entity meeting the statutory threshold. Qualifying entities must maintain full transfer pricing documentation, including a Local File and Master File. A Country-by-Country Report generally applies to multinational groups meeting the EUR 750 million consolidated revenue threshold. The French transfer pricing declaration, Form 2257-SD, may be required for entities within its statutory scope, including certain entities with turnover or gross assets of at least EUR 50 million or belonging to groups meeting the relevant threshold.

Economic substance scope: France does not operate a standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to the UAE ESR. Nevertheless, French anti-abuse, transfer pricing, beneficial-ownership, controlled-foreign-company, anti-hybrid, interest-limitation, principal-purpose-test, and abuse-of-law rules require genuine commercial substance. The French SAS should maintain appropriate personnel, decision-making, premises or operational capacity, inventory and logistics controls, bank and accounting records, risk assumption, and commercially supportable functions. A low-substance entity earning significant distribution margins or paying substantial management, royalty, or financing charges to a foreign affiliate is exposed to transfer pricing adjustments, withholding tax assessment, denial of deductions, treaty denial, and possible abuse-of-law penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Tax Declarations

Statutory sanction: Administrative penalties may reach 40% for deliberate breaches and 80% for fraudulent maneuvers or abuse of law, in addition to reassessed tax and late-payment interest. Criminal tax fraud may result in imprisonment of up to five years and a fine of up to EUR 500,000, with aggravated cases potentially carrying up to seven years' imprisonment and a fine of up to EUR 3 million. Legal-entity fines may be multiplied under criminal law, subject to the applicable offense.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may face criminal prosecution, personal liability for certain tax debts in cases of fraudulent or serious and repeated misconduct, management bans, civil restitution, asset seizures, and imprisonment. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may be ordered by a court or imposed through judicial control where legally justified.
Transfer Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Taxable income may be reassessed under the French arm's-length principle, with interest and tax penalties. Failure to produce required transfer pricing documentation may trigger a penalty generally calculated by reference to the greater of a statutory minimum and 0.5% of the amount of transactions for which documentation is absent or incomplete, subject to statutory caps and updates. Deliberate concealment may trigger higher penalties and criminal tax-fraud exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed where they knowingly approve sham arrangements, fraudulent records, or repeated non-compliance. Corporate limited liability does not protect intentional criminal conduct or court-imposed tax-debt liability.
Undeclared Cross-Border Payments and Withholding Tax

Statutory sanction: The French payer may become liable for unwithheld tax, late-payment interest, surcharges, and penalties. Treaty benefits may be denied where the recipient lacks beneficial ownership or the arrangement fails the principal-purpose or anti-abuse test. Incorrect withholding returns may create additional reporting and penalty exposure.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil and criminal exposure for knowingly authorizing false withholding declarations or payment structures designed to conceal taxable distributions. Personal liability depends on the facts, degree of misconduct, and applicable court findings.
Shell Company, Abuse of Law, and Treaty Shopping

Statutory sanction: The French tax administration may disregard the arrangement, deny treaty benefits, reassess tax, impose abuse-of-law penalties potentially reaching 40% or 80%, and assess late-payment interest. Beneficial ownership and principal-purpose-test failures may independently defeat treaty relief.

Director personal liability: Directors who intentionally implement artificial structures may face management bans, civil restitution, asset seizure, and criminal charges where the conduct constitutes fraud or document falsification. Imprisonment is fact-dependent and not automatic.
Anti-Money Laundering and Beneficial Ownership

Statutory sanction: Applicable regulated professionals may face supervisory sanctions, monetary penalties, activity restrictions, and criminal consequences for money laundering. The SAS itself may face confiscation, fines, exclusion from public contracts, and reputational or banking consequences where it participates in laundering or related criminal conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors may face criminal liability, confiscation, professional or management bans, civil claims, and imprisonment for knowingly facilitating money laundering, concealment of proceeds, or false beneficial-owner information. Travel restrictions may be imposed by judicial order where legally permitted.
False Accounting, Commercial Fraud, and Misuse of Corporate Assets

Statutory sanction: Consequences may include tax reassessment, denial of deductions, penalties, repayment, insolvency claims, commercial sanctions, and criminal sanctions for misuse of corporate assets, forgery, fraud, or fraudulent accounting. Penalties depend on the specific offense and facts.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for restitution, damages, contribution to insolvency liabilities in cases of management fault, asset seizure, management bans, and imprisonment for criminal offenses. Limited liability does not shield directors from their own unlawful acts.
Regulated IVD Product and Customs Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Possible consequences include customs reassessment, seizure, product withdrawal, administrative fines, market prohibition, suspension of distribution, civil liability, and criminal prosecution under applicable medical-device, public-health, product-safety, and customs rules. Incorrect customs values may also trigger import VAT and duty assessments.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil damages, administrative sanctions, criminal prosecution, personal fines, management bans, and imprisonment where they knowingly place unsafe, falsely documented, or unlawfully imported products on the French market.
Late Filing, Failure to Pay, and Repeated Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: A 10% late-filing or late-payment surcharge may apply, with higher penalties following formal notice or in cases of deliberate non-compliance, together with statutory late-payment interest. Repeated non-compliance may trigger estimated assessment, loss of procedural protections, audit escalation, and criminal exposure if accompanied by fraud.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal tax-debt liability where serious and repeated misconduct makes collection impossible, as well as civil liability to the company or creditors. Imprisonment is not a normal consequence of an inadvertent late filing but may arise where late filing forms part of intentional fraud.
