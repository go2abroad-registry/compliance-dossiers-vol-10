Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP), including the competent Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE). Principal online portal: impots.gouv.fr and the professional tax account (espace professionnel). Customs and import-export VAT matters are administered through the Direction générale des douanes et droits indirects (DGDDI), principally via the French customs online systems and the European Union Customs Trader Portal.

E-government filing portal

A French SAS obtains its French SIREN and SIRET identifiers through registration with the competent business formalities authority via the Guichet unique des formalités des entreprises operated by the Institut national de la propriété industrielle (INPI). The DGFiP then allocates or confirms the corporate income tax account and, where applicable, the intra-Community VAT identification number. The company must establish a professional tax account on impots.gouv.fr, provide its incorporation extract, registered office details, legal representative information, bank details, business activity description, and expected turnover, and submit the appropriate VAT liability or exemption declarations. A non-resident entity conducting taxable transactions in France must appoint a French fiscal representative where required by applicable VAT rules, unless established in an EU Member State or otherwise covered by an applicable relief. A non-EU entity generally registers through the Foreign Businesses Tax Service (Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères) and may require a French fiscal representative for VAT compliance. Operators importing or exporting goods must also obtain an Economic Operators Registration and Identification number (EORI) from French customs. A VAT number is generally structured as FR plus a two-digit validation key and the entity's nine-digit SIREN.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A SAS is generally subject to French corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés, or IS) at entity level. There is no general US-style check-the-box election. An eligible SAS may elect temporary taxation under the personal income tax regime (impôt sur le revenu, or IR) for a maximum period of five fiscal years if statutory conditions are satisfied, including qualifying ownership, size, age, commercial activity, and shareholder participation requirements. The election must be made within the prescribed period and is generally irrevocable after the applicable deadline. A qualifying SAS may also participate in a French tax consolidation regime with qualifying French subsidiaries under statutory ownership and election conditions. VAT grouping may be available for closely linked French entities, but it is a separate indirect-tax election and does not change the SAS corporate income tax classification. French anti-hybrid mismatch rules implement the EU Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive framework and can deny deductions, impose inclusion, or restrict exemption where an arrangement produces a hybrid mismatch; they do not provide a general entity-classification election.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

There is no single universal VAT registration threshold for all activities. The French franchise en base regime generally applies to domestic turnover below the applicable statutory threshold. For services, including most freight-forwarding and brokerage services, the principal annual threshold is EUR 37,500, with a tolerance threshold of EUR 41,250 under the applicable current rules. For sales of goods and related activities, the principal threshold is generally EUR 85,000, with a tolerance threshold of EUR 93,500. Thresholds are subject to statutory indexation and must be verified for the relevant tax year. These thresholds do not eliminate VAT obligations arising from imports, intra-Community acquisitions, reverse-charge transactions, or specific place-of-supply rules. A freight forwarder may therefore require VAT registration even when domestic turnover is below the franchise threshold.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

France generally applies VAT exemption with a right of deduction, rather than a domestic 0% rate, to qualifying exports of goods outside the European Union. The exporter must demonstrate that the goods left the EU customs territory, that the transaction is made by or on behalf of an eligible exporter, and that the customs export formalities were correctly completed. The primary evidence is the electronic export declaration and the customs confirmation of exit, normally identified through the Movement Reference Number (MRN) and exit results. Services must be analyzed under the place-of-supply rules rather than automatically treated as exports. A business-to-business service supplied to a customer established outside France is generally located where the customer is established and may be outside the scope of French VAT, subject to customer-status evidence and anti-abuse rules. International transport and certain services directly connected with exports, imports, or international transport may qualify for specific French VAT exemptions where the statutory conditions and documentary evidence are satisfied. Freight-forwarding, NVOCC, brokerage, warehousing, documentation, and ancillary services must be analyzed separately; an invoice described as an export service is insufficient by itself. For VAT recovery, the claimant must retain valid purchase invoices, customs evidence, transport records, contracts, customer identification, proof of service, and records supporting the applicable place-of-supply or exemption rule.

  • Electronic customs export declaration accepted by French customs or the relevant EU customs authority, including the Movement Reference Number (MRN)
  • Customs confirmation that the goods exited the European Union customs territory, including the exit results or equivalent electronic exit evidence
  • Commercial invoice and packing list identifying the exporter, consignee, goods, value, delivery terms, and transaction reference
  • Bill of lading, sea waybill, or other carrier transport document for ocean freight
  • Freight-forwarding or NVOCC contract, booking confirmation, house bill of lading, master bill of lading, and delivery or loading evidence where applicable
  • Evidence linking the invoice to the customs declaration and exported goods
  • Customer and establishment evidence supporting the applicable business-to-business place-of-supply rule for offshore services
  • Proof of payment and accounting records where relevant to audit substantiation
  • Inward remittance certificate only where required by a bank, financing arrangement, or non-French foreign-exchange documentation regime; it is not a general French VAT zero-rating prerequisite

France has adopted a staged mandatory electronic invoicing and electronic transaction-data reporting framework. The framework uses an approved public invoicing portal and/or registered private dematerialization platforms (plateformes de dématérialisation partenaires, or PDPs). It is not currently a universal real-time invoice-clearance system for all transactions. The scheduled framework requires all French-established businesses to be able to receive electronic invoices from 1 September 2026, with mandatory issuance and transaction-data reporting phased by business size, generally from 1 September 2026 for large and medium-sized businesses and from 1 September 2027 for small and micro-enterprises, subject to legislative or regulatory changes. Domestic business-to-business invoices within the scope of French VAT must use the prescribed structured or hybrid electronic formats and transmit required data through the approved channel. E-reporting applies principally to specified business-to-consumer and cross-border transactions. Import and export customs documentation remains subject to separate DGDDI electronic customs systems. There is no general requirement for a French VAT digital stamp on every invoice, but invoices must contain the mandatory legal particulars and be retained in an auditable electronic form.

France has no general foreign-exchange control requiring export proceeds to be repatriated within 180 days. VAT exemption or recovery may be denied or clawed back if the taxpayer cannot prove export, EU exit, customer status, taxable use, or the statutory connection between the service and the export or international transport. The DGFiP may assess unpaid VAT, late-payment interest, and applicable tax penalties, including penalties for insufficient documentation, intentional non-compliance, or fraudulent conduct. Fraudulent VAT claims may also trigger administrative fines, criminal prosecution, liability of directors or officers, and recovery of improperly refunded VAT. Customs irregularities may result in post-clearance duty and import-VAT assessments, customs penalties, seizure or forfeiture measures, and liability under the Union Customs Code and French customs law. Repeated or material non-compliance can lead to denial of simplified procedures, enhanced customs controls, loss of trusted-trader status, and increased audit scrutiny. Records supporting exports, transport, invoicing, and VAT treatment should generally be retained for the applicable French statutory limitation period, commonly three years for VAT assessment purposes, subject to longer periods for fraud, criminal matters, customs records, and corporate accounting documentation.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France is a full member of the eurozone; the euro (EUR) is fully convertible for both current and capital account transactions. There are no general exchange controls on capital movements within the EU/EEA, and profit remittances (dividends, branch profits) are freely transferable subject to standard corporate documentation and anti-money laundering (AML) verification. Non-resident shareholders may receive dividends without prior authorization, but must provide proof of beneficial ownership and tax residency to avoid withholding tax issues. For transactions with non-EU/EEA countries, France applies EU-wide sanctions and targeted asset freezes, but no blanket convertibility restrictions. The only practical hurdles are documentary: banks require underlying commercial contracts, invoices, and customs declarations to process cross-border payments, and may apply enhanced due diligence for high-risk jurisdictions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France does not impose mandatory surrender of export proceeds. Exporters are free to retain foreign currency earnings in foreign currency accounts (comptes en devises) or convert them to EUR at market rates. There is no statutory timeline for repatriation of export receipts; however, for customs and VAT purposes, export transactions must be settled within the standard commercial terms (typically 90-180 days) to avoid reclassification as a domestic supply. The Banque de France requires statistical reporting for balance-of-payments purposes on a monthly basis for transactions exceeding €50,000, but this is a reporting obligation, not a surrender requirement. No mandatory conversion ratio applies.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (Bank of France) and the French Treasury (Direction Générale du Trésor) under the authority of the Ministry of Economy, Finance and Recovery; foreign exchange policy is coordinated with the European Central Bank (ECB) for euro-zone matters.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Wise Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium. French-regulated Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) such as Qonto, Shine, and Lydia Pro offer multi-currency accounts and SWIFT/SEPA transfers, but they are not full-service banks and may have lower transaction limits for high-volume NVOCC operations. They are viable for operational liquidity and FX conversion, but for large trade settlements (LCs, large wire transfers) a tier-1 bank is recommended. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Revolut Business, Wise Business) are permitted to operate in France under EU passporting, but they are subject to the same AML/KYC requirements and may not provide local French IBANs (though they can provide French IBANs via partner banks). Legal constraints: EMIs cannot provide credit facilities or issue guarantees; they are not covered by the French deposit guarantee scheme (FGDR) for funds held beyond the e-money threshold. For NVOCC operations requiring high-volume disbursements, a hybrid approach (tier-1 bank + EMI for FX) is viable.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

For initial capital injection from a non-resident shareholder: use SWIFT MT103 wire transfer from the shareholder's bank to the French SAS's operational account. The payment must include a clear purpose code (e.g., 'Capital contribution' or 'Shareholder loan') and reference the company's SIREN number. For amounts exceeding €10,000, the bank will require a copy of the share subscription agreement and proof of the shareholder's identity. For capital contributions in kind (e.g., equipment), a notarized valuation is required. The French Treasury (Direction Générale du Trésor) requires statistical reporting for direct investment flows (including capital injections) if the amount exceeds €15,000 per transaction; the bank will handle this reporting. For loans from shareholders, a loan agreement with interest rate and repayment schedule must be provided to avoid reclassification as hidden equity. The bank will verify the source of funds (e.g., bank statements) to comply with AML.For trade settlements (e.g., paying ocean carriers, port charges, or overseas agents), the primary clearing rail is SWIFT MT103 for wire transfers. For larger transactions or when a documentary credit is required, a Commercial Letter of Credit (LC) can be issued via the bank's trade finance department. For EU/EEA payments, SEPA Credit Transfer (SCT) is used, but for non-EU payments, SWIFT is standard. The bank will require supporting documents: commercial invoice, bill of lading, and customs declaration (for exports). For payments to high-risk jurisdictions, additional due diligence is required. The NVOCC may also use ACH Direct (SEPA Direct Debit) for recurring payments to domestic suppliers, but this is not typical for cross-border. For FX conversion, the bank will execute at the interbank rate plus a margin; alternatively, the company can use a fintech EMI for better rates.Forward contracts (FX forwards) with a maturity up to 12 months, available from tier-1 banks for EUR/USD, EUR/GBP, and other major currencies, subject to a credit line and underlying exposure documentation.Non-Deliverable Forwards (NDFs) for currencies with restricted convertibility (e.g., CNY, INR) are permissible but only through banks with a license to trade in those markets; the French bank will act as an intermediary.Currency swaps (FX swaps) for simultaneous spot and forward transactions, used to manage liquidity in multiple currencies, available from banks with a treasury desk.Options (FX options) are permissible but require a more sophisticated risk management framework and are typically used by larger NVOCCs; they are not listed in the schema but are allowed under French regulations.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Feasibility rating: Medium. French-regulated Electronic Money Institutions (EMIs) such as Qonto, Shine, and Lydia Pro offer multi-currency accounts and SWIFT/SEPA transfers, but they are not full-service banks and may have lower transaction limits for high-volume NVOCC operations. They are viable for operational liquidity and FX conversion, but for large trade settlements (LCs, large wire transfers) a tier-1 bank is recommended. Offshore digital EMIs (e.g., Revolut Business, Wise Business) are permitted to operate in France under EU passporting, but they are subject to the same AML/KYC requirements and may not provide local French IBANs (though they can provide French IBANs via partner banks). Legal constraints: EMIs cannot provide credit facilities or issue guarantees; they are not covered by the French deposit guarantee scheme (FGDR) for funds held beyond the e-money threshold. For NVOCC operations requiring high-volume disbursements, a hybrid approach (tier-1 bank + EMI for FX) is viable.

25%

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The standard French withholding tax on dividends paid by a French company to a non-resident corporate shareholder is generally 25%, subject to applicable exemptions and tax treaties. The EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive exemption may apply to qualifying EU corporate parents meeting ownership, holding-period, subject-to-tax, anti-abuse, and beneficial-ownership conditions. A domestic participation exemption may also apply in specific qualifying situations.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a financial year ending on 31 December, the return is generally due by the second working day following 1 May of the following year, subject to the applicable electronic-filing calendar. For a financial year ending on another date, the return is generally due within three months after the financial year-end. Corporate income tax installments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September, and 15 December, with the final balance generally due by the 15th day of the fourth month following year-end. For a 31 December year-end, the balance is generally due by 15 May.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: French full transfer-pricing documentation, including a Master File and Local File, is generally mandatory where the French entity or French tax group has annual pre-tax turnover or gross assets of at least EUR 400 million, or where it is controlled by, controls, or is part of an entity or tax group meeting the statutory EUR 400 million threshold. Certain transactions and groups below that threshold remain subject to the arm's-length principle, record-keeping, and information-production obligations. Country-by-Country Reporting generally applies to multinational groups with consolidated annual revenue of at least EUR 750 million.

Economic substance scope: France has no separate United Arab Emirates-style Economic Substance Regulations regime. Substance is nevertheless material under French transfer-pricing rules, the general anti-abuse rule, the principal-purpose anti-abuse rule, beneficial-ownership requirements, controlled foreign company rules, interest limitation rules, and judicial doctrines concerning abuse of law and abnormal act of management. A French SAS should maintain real French decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, premises or operational infrastructure, commercial risk control, bank-account control, books and records, and evidence that freight-forwarding functions and risks are actually performed in France. A contractual commissionaire or NVOCC structure must be consistent with actual conduct and risk allocation.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion and Fraudulent Tax Returns

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, late-payment interest, tax penalties generally ranging from 10% to 80%, and criminal tax-fraud penalties that may include imprisonment of up to five years and a fine of up to EUR 500,000. Aggravated cases can carry up to seven years' imprisonment and a fine of up to EUR 3 million, with higher limits in specified organized or aggravated circumstances. Legal entities may face substantially increased fines, confiscation, publication, and exclusion from public contracts.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto managers may incur criminal liability, civil liability, personal tax liability under the French tax-director liability procedure where fraudulent or repeated conduct makes collection impossible, and management bans. Confiscation and restitution may apply. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may be ordered through criminal procedure or judicial control.
VAT Fraud and Carousel Fraud

Statutory sanction: Recovery of unpaid VAT, late-payment interest, penalties commonly up to 40% for deliberate failure and up to 80% for fraudulent or abusive conduct, possible criminal prosecution, and suspension or restriction of VAT-related rights. Criminal penalties may include imprisonment and fines depending on the offense and aggravating circumstances.

Director personal liability: Managers may be prosecuted for intentional participation, fraudulent bookkeeping, or complicity. Personal civil exposure can arise where management misconduct causes tax loss, and criminal courts may impose confiscation, professional prohibitions, and restitution.
Customs and Import-Export Misdeclaration

Statutory sanction: Customs reassessment, seizure or forfeiture, customs fines, interest, loss of customs simplifications or authorizations, and potential criminal prosecution. Sanctions depend on the Customs Code offense, the goods, intent, and the value of the evaded duties or prohibited goods.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible employees may be personally prosecuted for intentional customs fraud or complicity. The SAS, its customs representative, and involved individuals may face joint or separate penalties, confiscation, and loss of authorization.
Transfer-Pricing Manipulation

Statutory sanction: Reassessment to arm's-length profit, denial of deductions, withholding-tax exposure, interest, penalties, and transfer-pricing documentation penalties. Failure to produce required documentation can result in a penalty based on the greater of a statutory minimum and a percentage of the reassessed profits or transactions, subject to applicable limits.

Director personal liability: Directors may face liability for deliberate misrepresentation, fraudulent accounting, abuse of corporate assets, or tax fraud. Mere non-arm's-length pricing without intent does not automatically create criminal personal liability, but repeated or deliberate manipulation materially increases exposure.
Undeclared Permanent Establishment or Agency Activity

Statutory sanction: French corporate income tax, VAT, payroll tax, business taxes, interest, penalties, and possible reassessment of the foreign enterprise's French taxable profit. Employment, transport, customs, and regulatory sanctions may also apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may be exposed for deliberate evasion, misleading filings, unlawful management, and failure to comply with French registration or licensing requirements. A French SAS does not shield individuals from their own criminal acts or serious management misconduct.
Dividend, Interest, and Royalty Withholding-Tax Failure

Statutory sanction: Assessment of the unpaid withholding tax against the French payer, late-payment interest, penalties, possible 40% or 80% increases depending on conduct, and denial of treaty relief. The payer may remain liable even where the recipient received the gross payment.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable in cases of deliberate withholding-tax evasion, fraudulent payment instructions, or insolvency caused by misconduct. Criminal exposure may arise for falsification, fraud, or complicity.
Shell Company and Beneficial-Ownership Abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty benefits, abuse-of-law reassessment, tax penalties, beneficial-owner reporting violations, possible dissolution or administrative consequences, confiscation, and criminal sanctions for fraud or money laundering where applicable.

Director personal liability: Directors, de facto managers, nominees, and beneficial owners may face criminal prosecution, confiscation, management bans, civil restitution, and liability for tax and creditor losses. Travel bans or passport restrictions require a specific judicial or criminal-procedure order and are not automatic.
Anti-Money Laundering and Terrorist-Financing Failures

Statutory sanction: Regulatory measures, administrative fines, professional restrictions, account or transaction freezes, confiscation, and criminal money-laundering penalties. Aggravated money laundering can carry severe imprisonment and fines, with enhanced consequences for organized activity.

Director personal liability: Directors and compliance officers may be personally liable for intentional participation, facilitation, concealment, or obstruction. Failure to maintain appropriate controls may also cause civil claims, banking termination, and loss of commercial authorizations.
Sanctions and Export-Control Breaches

Statutory sanction: Asset freezes, transaction prohibitions, administrative and criminal fines, imprisonment depending on the applicable sanctions regime, confiscation, and loss of customs or trade privileges.

Director personal liability: Directors and employees may be prosecuted personally for intentional violations, circumvention, or facilitation. Corporate status does not protect individuals from sanctions-related criminal liability.
Abuse of Corporate Assets and Insolvency Misconduct

Statutory sanction: Repayment, damages, clawback of transactions, bankruptcy or liquidation proceedings, director contribution to insufficient assets, management bans, and criminal sanctions for abuse of corporate assets or bankruptcy offenses.

Director personal liability: The SAS liability shield does not protect directors from personal liability for faute de gestion, abuse of corporate assets, fraudulent insolvency, or intentional wrongdoing. Civil restitution, asset seizure, management prohibitions, and imprisonment may apply.
Unlicensed or Non-Compliant Freight-Forwarding Activity

Statutory sanction: Administrative orders, fines, suspension or withdrawal of registration, prohibition on operating, contract and insurance consequences, and potential criminal sanctions for unauthorized activity.

Director personal liability: The president and de facto managers may be personally prosecuted for knowingly operating without required authorization or allowing regulated operations to continue after notice. Corporate liability does not eliminate individual responsibility for intentional breaches.
