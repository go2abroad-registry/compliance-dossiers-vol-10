Competent corporate registry

Governing statute

French Commercial Code (Code de commerce), Book II, Title II, Chapter VII, Articles L227-1 to L227-20 and R227-1 to R227-2; applicable SAS provisions incorporate specified rules applicable to sociétés anonymes, including Articles L225-3 and L225-5 on capital contributions and fund deposits.

Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

French Directorate General of Public Finances (Direction générale des finances publiques, DGFiP), through the relevant Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE) and, for non-established businesses, the Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères (SIEE). The principal online portal is impots.gouv.fr, supplemented by the formalities portal guichet-unique.entreprises.gouv.fr for business registration.

E-government filing portal

A French SAS is registered through the mandatory business formalities portal, which transmits corporate information to the competent authorities. The DGFiP then allocates the French tax identifiers, including the SIREN, SIRET and VAT identification number where applicable. The French VAT number generally consists of the country prefix FR, a two-character control key and the nine-digit SIREN. A non-established foreign entity carrying out taxable transactions in France must apply for French VAT registration with the SIEE before or promptly after commencing taxable activities, generally by filing the prescribed registration documentation and providing constitutional documents, proof of business activity, identity and authority documents, bank details and, where relevant, evidence of taxable transactions. Businesses established in another EU Member State may use the VAT registration process applicable to non-established EU businesses; businesses established outside the EU may require a French tax representative unless an applicable exemption or mutual-assistance arrangement applies. The VAT number must be verified through the EU VIES system where intra-EU transactions are involved.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A SAS is ordinarily subject to French corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés, IS) at entity level. A qualifying SAS may elect for income tax treatment (impôt sur le revenu, IR) under Article 239 bis AB of the French Tax Code for a maximum period of five financial years, subject to statutory conditions concerning age, size, ownership, management and business activity. The election is not a US-style check-the-box election and does not generally provide an unrestricted classification choice. France has no general domestic hybrid-mismatch entity election comparable to a check-the-box regime. Anti-hybrid rules may deny deductions or impose inclusion where arrangements produce deduction/non-inclusion or duplicate-deduction outcomes.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

The French VAT franchise regime is transaction- and activity-dependent. For ordinary service activities, the principal domestic turnover threshold is generally EUR 37,500, with a tolerance threshold generally of EUR 41,250; for trading and food-service activities, the relevant goods or mixed-activity thresholds are generally EUR 85,000 and EUR 93,500. Thresholds and qualifying categories are subject to statutory annual adjustments and special rules. A ghost-kitchen operator must assess registration based on the actual supplies, including prepared-food sales, delivery charges, platform commissions and any cross-border transactions. Registration may be required voluntarily or immediately under specific intra-EU, import, export, reverse-charge or transaction rules even where a domestic franchise threshold is not exceeded.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

French VAT is generally not charged on qualifying exports of goods dispatched from France to a destination outside the EU, provided the supplier can prove export and satisfy the applicable invoicing and customs conditions. The exemption is an exemption with right to deduct, rather than a domestic 0% rate. Qualifying intra-EU supplies of goods may be invoiced without French VAT where the customer is VAT-identified in another EU Member State, the goods are transported to that Member State and the supplier satisfies reporting and documentary requirements. Services supplied to a business customer established outside France may be outside the scope of French VAT under the B2B place-of-supply rule; this is technically different from a zero-rated export. Food prepared and delivered by a French ghost kitchen is normally subject to French VAT, commonly 10% for qualifying prepared food for immediate consumption, while alcoholic beverages generally carry 20% and certain packaged food products may qualify for 5.5%. The precise rate depends on product composition, packaging, consumption conditions and delivery model.

  • Commercial invoice, packing or delivery documentation and transport evidence identifying the goods, supplier, customer and destination outside the EU
  • Proof of exit from the EU customs territory, such as the electronic exit results, customs-certified export evidence or equivalent carrier and logistics documentation

France does not currently operate a universal real-time VAT invoice-clearance system for ordinary domestic business-to-business transactions. Mandatory electronic invoicing will be phased in through approved private platforms and the public invoicing portal under the French reform: all businesses must be able to receive electronic invoices from 1 September 2026; large and mid-sized businesses must issue electronic invoices from 1 September 2026; small and micro-enterprises must issue them from 1 September 2027. Electronic transaction reporting for certain business-to-consumer and cross-border transactions will apply on the same phased timetable. Public-sector invoices are submitted through Chorus Pro. Invoices must contain legally required particulars, be authentic, integral and readable, and be retained for the statutory period. VAT records and transaction data must support audit trails; no general digital stamp or pre-clearance code is presently required for ordinary SAS invoices.

France has no general statutory 180-day foreign-exchange repatriation deadline applicable to export VAT relief. Export proceeds remain subject to banking, anti-money-laundering, customs and accounting controls, and sector-specific or contractual requirements may impose separate timing obligations. If the taxpayer cannot substantiate export, transport, customer status or the correct VAT treatment, the DGFiP may reassess output VAT, deny or claw back input-VAT recovery, assess late-payment interest generally at 0.20% per month, and impose penalties. A 40% penalty may apply for deliberate failure, while an 80% penalty may apply in cases involving fraudulent manoeuvres or abuse of rights; additional penalties can apply for failure to produce records, inaccurate VAT returns or customs violations. Customs-related breaches may generate separate duties, fines, seizure or other measures under the Union Customs Code and French customs law. Refunds paid on the basis of false, incomplete or unsupported export evidence may be recovered retrospectively, with interest and penalties.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The euro is fully convertible for both current account transactions and capital account transactions, subject to administrative statistical reporting rather than approval. France applies EU capital liberalisation principles; non-EU/EEA capital movements above €10,000 require balance-of-payments declaration via the intermediary bank or direct declaration to DG Trésor. Profit remittance is generally unrestricted but dividend distributions to non-resident shareholders are subject to French withholding tax unless reduced by tax treaty, and must be supported by audited financial statements and corporate formalities of the SAS.

Export proceeds surrender rules

No mandatory surrender or compulsory conversion ratio applies to euro-denominated or foreign-currency export receipts in France. Exporters of services (including restaurant/delivery concept licensing) must repatriate proceeds and may be required to declare foreign assets/receivables for balance-of-payments statistics. There is no statutory timeline for conversion; invoicing can be in any currency, but foreign currency cash balances must be managed under normal banking supervision and reported for prudential/statistical purposes.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (Bank of France) as monetary and balance-of-payments authority; FX regulatory implementation under the Direction Générale du Trésor (DG Trésor) and banking supervision by the ACPR (Autorité de contrôle prudentiel et de résolution).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: High for low-risk treasury operations, subject to ACPR e-money institution (EMI) license or passport from another EU/EEA regulator. Legal constraints: EMIs cannot provide lending/credit facilities, and their ability to process certain SWIFT/MT103 cross-border payments may be limited compared to licensed credit institutions. Specifically for a ghost kitchen receiving delivery platform payouts from UberEats/DoorDash, a French EMI account (e.g., Qonto or similar) is viable for SEPA euro settlements but should not be sole banking infrastructure for non-EU vendor payments or escrow balances.

Qualifying small and medium-sized companies may apply a 15% rate to the first EUR 42,500 of taxable profit, subject to statutory conditions, including turnover not exceeding EUR 10 million, fully paid-up share capital, and at least 75% ownership by individuals or qualifying entities.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Dividends paid by a French company to a non-resident are generally subject to a 25% withholding tax for corporate recipients, subject to domestic exemptions and applicable tax treaties. Dividends paid to individuals are generally subject to a 12.8% withholding tax, subject to statutory exceptions. A qualifying EU parent may obtain exemption under the Parent-Subsidiary Directive implementation where ownership, holding-period, subject-to-tax, beneficial-ownership, and anti-abuse conditions are satisfied. Payments to non-cooperative jurisdictions may be subject to substantially higher rates.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a financial year ending on a date other than 31 December, the return and tax payment are generally due within three months after year-end. For a 31 December year-end, the return is generally due by the second business day following 1 May of the following year. Four corporate income tax instalments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September, and 15 December, with a final balance payment generally due by the return deadline. Newly formed companies and companies with low prior-year liability may qualify for instalment exemptions.

Deadline: · Frequency:

The balance is generally due by the deadline applicable to the annual corporate income tax return. Instalments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September, and 15 December.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: EUR 400 million threshold. Full transfer-pricing documentation, including a Local File and Master File, is generally mandatory for French entities or French tax groups meeting the statutory turnover or gross-assets threshold, or belonging to a group meeting the applicable threshold. Smaller entities remain subject to the arm's-length principle and must be able to substantiate related-party pricing. French transfer-pricing documentation and reporting requirements have been strengthened for financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2024. Country-by-country reporting generally applies to multinational groups meeting the EUR 750 million consolidated revenue threshold.

Economic substance scope: France does not operate a single, separately branded Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore jurisdictions. Substance is nevertheless required under French anti-abuse principles, beneficial-ownership rules, the general anti-abuse rule, controlled foreign company rules, interest limitation rules, withholding-tax provisions, and treaty principal-purpose-test standards. A French SAS should maintain genuine local management, decision-making, personnel or operational capacity proportionate to its functions, premises or documented access to operating infrastructure, books and records, commercial risk assumption, and evidence of services received. A conduit company, artificial royalty owner, circular-financing vehicle, or entity lacking decision-making capacity may lose treaty benefits and face reassessment.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate income tax evasion and fraudulent tax return

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, late interest, 40% penalty for deliberate conduct, or 80% penalty for fraudulent maneuvers or abuse of rights. Criminal tax fraud may carry up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 500,000 fine; aggravated cases may carry up to seven years' imprisonment and a EUR 3 million fine, subject to statutory aggravating conditions. Corporate criminal fines may be multiplied under the Penal Code, with confiscation and commercial prohibitions also possible.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto managers may face criminal prosecution, personal fines, imprisonment, confiscation, management bans, and civil liability. Tax authorities may seek joint and several liability under Article L267 of the French Tax Procedures Code where a manager's fraudulent conduct or repeated serious non-compliance makes tax collection impossible. Travel restrictions are not an automatic tax penalty but may arise from judicial control, arrest warrants, or criminal proceedings.
VAT fraud and fictitious input-tax recovery

Statutory sanction: VAT reassessment, interest, 40% or 80% penalties depending on conduct, potential loss of VAT rights, and criminal prosecution for fraud or document falsification. Additional penalties may apply for failure to issue compliant invoices or retain required records.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed to criminal fines, imprisonment, management bans, and tax-enforcement liability where they knowingly organized or tolerated VAT fraud. Insolvency proceedings may generate claims for insufficiency of assets if management misconduct contributed to the shortfall.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and profit shifting

Statutory sanction: Arm's-length adjustment, corporate tax, withholding tax, interest, penalties, and specific documentation-related penalties. Failure to produce required documentation can trigger a penalty generally equal to the greater of the statutory minimum and a percentage of reassessed profits, subject to legal caps and current-law calculation.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal liability where transactions are knowingly abusive, fictitious, or detrimental to the French company. Deliberate concealment can escalate to tax-fraud prosecution; ordinary documentation failure does not automatically create imprisonment.
Shell company, treaty abuse, or beneficial-ownership failure

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty or domestic exemptions, withholding-tax reassessment, interest, 40% or 80% penalties, general anti-abuse reassessment, and potential criminal consequences if documents or declarations are falsified.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for intentional misrepresentation, abuse of corporate assets, tax fraud, or participation in money laundering. Corporate separateness generally remains effective for legitimate liabilities, but fraudulent or abusive conduct can support personal and insolvency-related claims.
Failure to withhold or remit tax on cross-border payments

Statutory sanction: Recovery of unpaid withholding tax from the French payer, interest, filing penalties, and possible penalties for deliberate concealment or abuse. Treaty relief may be denied if residence, beneficial ownership, or procedural evidence is deficient.

Director personal liability: The company is ordinarily the primary debtor. Directors may incur personal or criminal exposure where non-withholding was deliberate, repeated, fraudulent, or connected with insolvency or asset stripping.
Accounting, invoicing, and record-retention violations

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment based on estimated turnover, rejection of deductions, penalties, accounting sanctions, and possible criminal liability for false accounting or document destruction. French accounting and tax records generally require long-term retention, commonly ten years for accounting documents, subject to the applicable record category.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil, criminal, and insolvency liability for organized concealment, fictitious accounting, or destruction of evidence. A court may impose management bans or order restitution.
Anti-money-laundering and terrorist-financing non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Regulatory measures may include warnings, injunctions, administrative fines, business restrictions, asset freezing, and criminal prosecution for money laundering. Money laundering can carry imprisonment and substantial fines, with aggravated penalties for organized or repeated conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted as principals or accessories, face imprisonment, fines, confiscation, management bans, and civil restitution. Travel restrictions may be imposed by judicial authorities in criminal proceedings but are not automatic.
Unlawful distribution, asset stripping, or insolvency misconduct

Statutory sanction: Recovery of unlawful distributions, repayment claims, clawback of transactions, insolvency proceedings, director liability for insufficiency of assets, and possible bankruptcy-related criminal sanctions including misuse of corporate assets.

Director personal liability: Directors may be ordered to contribute personally to the company's liabilities, disqualified from management, fined, or imprisoned in serious cases. The corporate veil is not automatically pierced; liability normally requires statutory grounds, personal misconduct, fraud, or insolvency-related fault.
Employment, payroll, and undeclared-work fraud

Statutory sanction: Back-payment of social contributions and payroll taxes, reassessments, surcharges, labor penalties, exclusion from public benefits, and criminal penalties for travail dissimulé, potentially including imprisonment and significant fines.

Director personal liability: The president or de facto manager may face personal criminal liability, management bans, imprisonment, and civil claims for employee entitlements and social-security losses.
Food, public-health, and operating-license violations

Statutory sanction: Administrative closure, withdrawal or suspension of authorization, product seizure or destruction, fines, civil claims, and criminal penalties for public-health or consumer-protection offences.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal criminal liability for knowing or negligent breaches, particularly where unsafe food causes injury. Insurance coverage may be denied for intentional or grossly negligent conduct.
Consumer, advertising, and virtual-brand deception

Statutory sanction: Injunctions, administrative fines, consumer refunds, damages, platform suspension, and criminal penalties for deceptive commercial practices, with aggravated sanctions in specified circumstances.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally prosecuted where they authorized or knowingly maintained deceptive practices. Civil liability may extend to consumer losses and regulatory remediation costs.
