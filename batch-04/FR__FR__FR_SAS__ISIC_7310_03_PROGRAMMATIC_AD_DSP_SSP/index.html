Paid-up Capital & Blocked Account Regime Mandatory Capital Deposit & Lockup

No statutory minimum capital applies to a SAS; capital may be set at EUR 1 or a higher amount in the articles. At least 50% of cash contributions must be paid upon subscription, with the balance payable within five years of registration. Cash contributions are deposited into a blocked account, and the depositary bank or notary issues the certificate of deposit required for registration. Contributions in kind generally require an auditor's valuation unless the statutory exemption conditions are satisfied.

Funding & unlock path: No general pre-opening industrial clearance is required for a normal office or software operation. Premises open to the public may qualify as an établissement recevant du public and require fire-safety and accessibility compliance, with review by the competent municipality or Prefecture. Employers must maintain workplace risk assessment documentation, prevention measures, emergency procedures, and mandatory occupational-health arrangements. Structural-load inspections are required where mandated by the lease, building rules, construction works, insurer, or applicable safety regulations.

Paid-in capital rule: No statutory minimum capital applies to a SAS; capital may be set at EUR 1 or a higher amount in the articles. At least 50% of cash contributions must be paid upon subscription, with the balance payable within five years of registration. Cash contributions are deposited into a blocked account, and the depositary bank or notary issues the certificate of deposit required for registration. Contributions in kind generally require an auditor's valuation unless the statutory exemption conditions are satisfied.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

The euro is a fully convertible currency in France; no general exchange control law restricts either current account or capital account convertibility. The French foreign-exchange regime is based on Articles L151-1 et seq. of the Monetary and Financial Code and on European Union Treaty freedoms of establishment and capital movement. Profit remittance barriers are effectively nil for dividends, branch profits, interest, royalties and agency fees, provided that the underlying transactions are genuine, arm’s length and fully documented. The only practical constraints are French withholding tax (generally 25% domestic rate, reduced or exempt under the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive and extended tax treaties), anti-abuse provisions (e.g., the French anti-hybrid and controlled-foreign-company rules), and the requirement that the SAS tax returns, audited financial statements, and beneficial ownership declarations are in good order.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France applies no statutory export-proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory conversion ratio. All export receipts may be retained in foreign currency or converted freely. The sole mandatory compliance duty is statistical reporting to Banque de France for balance-of-payments purposes where the transaction meets France’s reporting thresholds, and the general obligation to route cross-border payments through a French-resident credit institution or an authorized payment institution licensed in the EEA under the Payment Services Directive (PSD2).

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (Bank of France) acting as the national central bank and the repository of foreign exchange balance-of-payment statistics, together with the French Ministry of Economy, Finance and Industrial and Digital Sovereignty for capital movement policy and legislative instruments.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium — Fintech electronic money institutions (EMIs) and payment institutions (e.g., Qonto, Shine, Lydia, French FinTech neobanks) are legally viable for opening a French IBAN account for an SAS, but they are not credit institutions and therefore cannot provide credit facilities, escrow-grade cash segregation, or full corporate banking treasury services. They can support SWIFT and SEPA cross-border euro clearing, but for the scale expected in a programmatic AdTech exchange, an SFr-level Tier 1 bank relationship is recommended to satisfy aggregator counterparties and clearinghouse trust conditions. EMIs are subject to Banque de France registration and ACPR (Prudential Supervision and Resolution Authority) oversight; they are not permitted to take deposits or perform proprietary FX dealing.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Capital injections by shareholders into the French SAS must be routed via SWIFT MT103 for non-EEA remittances or SEPA Credit Transfer (pacs.008) for EEA euro transfers, with the payment type clearly coded as ‘BOP: DI’ (Direct Investment) for Banque de France balance-of-payments statistical returns when the transaction threshold applies. The bank requires the corporate resolution approving the capital increase, the French notaire or legalization certificate, proof of the shareholders’ funds, and the exact purpose code such as ‘Capital contribution – Ordinary share subscription’ with a breakdown between subscribed capital and share premium. Cross-border funds coming in are not subject to exchange controls; the only mandatory check is AML and sanctions screening and the obligation under the French Monetary and Financial Code to report any suspicious fund flow.Immediate and routine trade settlement disbursements to overseas media agencies, publishers, and DMP/SSP partners are made via the SWIFT MT103 wire protocol with an appropriate tariff code (e.g., ITF transactions or online payment) when the beneficiary is outside France. Where the beneficiary is inside the SEPA zone, the rails used are SEPA Instant Credit Transfer (SCT Inst) or SEPA Credit Transfer. Commercial letters of credit (Documentary LCs) are permissible but are generally not required for programmatic ad exchange payments given the intangible service nature; when used, the LC must be issued under UCP 600 rules, settled through a French or foreign confirming bank, and accompanied by invoices and traffic proofs. Settlement transactions are subject to Banque de France balancing of payments reporting when exceeding the statistical threshold (current: aggregated monthly outflows/inflows above the relevant threshold requirement).Over-the-counter FX forwards against the euro (e.g., EUR/USD, EUR/GBP) for hedging short-term accounts payable and receivable under ISDA GMRA or a standard French financial collateral arrangement.Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) for emerging-market currencies (e.g., INR, IDR, VND) to hedge AdTech counterparties where a fully deliverable forward is unavailable or legally restricted in the local jurisdiction.Currency swaps (cross-currency interest rate swaps, CCIRS) to rebalance long-dated operating cash flows and to cover refinancing risk in dual-currency treasury positions.FX options (call and put on euro crosses) for at-risk ad inventory settlements when the revenue stream is contractually contingent.Plain vanilla forward swaps embedded in commercial intercompany treasury netting agreements, provided they do not cross the line into speculative trading and are documented as hedging instruments under EMIR and MiFID II.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% French withholding tax generally applies to dividends and similar distributions paid by a French company to a non-resident. The rate may be reduced or eliminated under an applicable tax treaty or the EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive as implemented in France. The domestic EU parent-company exemption generally requires qualifying corporate status, minimum holding conditions, holding-period conditions, beneficial ownership and anti-abuse compliance.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The annual return is generally due within three months after the end of the fiscal year. For a fiscal year ending on 31 December, the ordinary electronic deadline is generally the second business day following 1 May, subject to the annual tax-administration calendar and any professional-account filing tolerance. The corporate income tax balance is generally payable by the 15th day of the fourth month following the fiscal year-end. Instalments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September, and 15 December, subject to statutory exceptions and adjustments.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: France requires full transfer-pricing documentation under Article L13 AA of the French Tax Procedures Code generally where the French entity or French tax group exceeds EUR 400 million in annual turnover or gross assets, or belongs to an entity or group meeting the statutory threshold, with additional statutory cases. The simplified documentation obligation under Article L13 AB can apply below that threshold. Form 2257-SD reporting generally applies to specified controlled transactions where statutory thresholds and conditions are met, including transactions exceeding EUR 100,000 by relevant category or other prescribed tests.

Economic substance scope: France has no separate UAE-style or Cayman-style standalone Economic Substance Regulations regime. Substance is nevertheless required under French transfer-pricing, beneficial-ownership, anti-abuse, general anti-avoidance, controlled foreign company, interest limitation, withholding-tax, and tax-residence rules. The SAS should maintain French decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, premises or operational infrastructure, risk control, contractual authority, accounting records, and commercial rationale proportionate to its programmatic advertising exchange and SSP activities. A conduit entity with no functional capacity may lose treaty or directive benefits and may face recharacterization, transfer-pricing adjustments, withholding-tax exposure, and penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate income tax evasion and fraudulent tax return

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, late-payment interest, penalties generally up to 40% for deliberate conduct and up to 80% for fraudulent manoeuvres, and criminal tax-fraud exposure. Tax fraud may result in imprisonment of up to five years and a fine of up to EUR 500,000, with aggravated cases carrying up to seven years and EUR 3 million, subject to statutory conditions and applicable alternative multiples based on proceeds.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto managers may face criminal prosecution, personal financial liability in specified cases of fraudulent or abusive management, management bans, asset seizure, and civil claims for unpaid tax where statutory conditions are met. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may be ordered in criminal or enforcement proceedings.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Primary adjustment to arm's-length profit, withholding-tax reassessment, interest limitation consequences, penalties for inadequate documentation, and general tax penalties. Failure to provide required documentation can trigger the statutory documentation penalty based on the covered transaction amounts or reassessed profits.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil and criminal exposure where transfer-pricing manipulation forms part of fraudulent tax conduct, false accounting, abuse of corporate assets, or deliberate concealment. Ordinary arm's-length disputes do not automatically create personal liability.
Shell company and treaty-shopping abuse

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty or directive benefits, domestic withholding-tax assessment, interest, penalties, and possible abuse-of-law or fraudulent-conduct sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal exposure where they knowingly implement a sham structure, submit false residence or beneficial-owner documentation, or participate in fraudulent tax evasion. Corporate separateness remains the rule unless statutory personal-fault or fraud grounds are established.
Beneficial-owner, withholding-tax, and dividend distribution breach

Statutory sanction: Recovery of unpaid withholding tax from the French payer, interest, penalties, possible denial of deductibility, and corporate-law consequences for unlawful distributions.

Director personal liability: Directors may be liable for unlawful distributions, breach of fiduciary duties, negligent tax administration, or deliberate misstatements. Criminal exposure increases where documents are falsified or payments are concealed.
Money laundering and proceeds-of-crime exposure

Statutory sanction: Money laundering may carry up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 375,000 fine, with aggravated forms carrying up to ten years' imprisonment and a EUR 750,000 fine, subject to statutory provisions. Corporate fines may be multiplied under French criminal-law rules, with confiscation and activity restrictions also possible.

Director personal liability: Directors and employees may be prosecuted personally, face imprisonment, fines, confiscation, professional or management bans, and asset-freezing measures. Travel restrictions may be ordered by judicial authorities but are not automatic.
AML, sanctions, and suspicious transaction control failure

Statutory sanction: Administrative, civil, contractual, and potentially criminal consequences may arise depending on the regulated status of the business and the underlying conduct. Sanctions may include fines, injunctions, account restrictions, confiscation, and termination of payment relationships.

Director personal liability: Managers may face personal criminal liability for knowing participation, facilitation, concealment, or obstruction. Mere regulatory control weakness generally requires proof of a statutory breach or personal fault before personal sanctions attach.
False accounting and corporate records

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, accounting and audit consequences, civil damages, criminal penalties for falsification or use of false documents, and possible insolvency or director-disqualification consequences.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto managers may face criminal prosecution, personal civil liability for asset shortfall in insolvency where management fault caused the shortfall, management bans, and confiscation.
VAT and digital advertising reporting failure

Statutory sanction: VAT assessment, interest, late-reporting penalties, penalties for inaccurate returns, denial of input VAT, and possible joint-liability or collection measures in fraudulent arrangements.

Director personal liability: Personal exposure may arise from deliberate VAT fraud, fraudulent invoicing, organized concealment, or serious management fault in insolvency. Routine technical VAT errors generally produce corporate rather than automatic personal liability.
Unlawful profit repatriation and asset diversion

Statutory sanction: Restitution, cancellation or clawback of payments, shareholder and creditor claims, tax reassessment, and criminal sanctions for abuse of corporate assets or unlawful distributions where statutory elements are satisfied.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for repayment, damages, management bans, and criminal penalties, particularly where they knowingly diverted corporate assets or impaired creditor interests.
