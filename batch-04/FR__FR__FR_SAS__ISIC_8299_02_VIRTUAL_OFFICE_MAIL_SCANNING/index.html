Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction Générale des Finances Publiques (DGFiP) — Direction Générale des Finances Publiques, Ministère de l'Économie, des Finances et de la Souveraineté industrielle et numérique; online professional portal: impots.gouv.fr (Espace professionnel); the single incorporation and registration gateway is the Guichet Unique of the Institut National de la Propriété Industrielle (INPI) at guichet-entreprises.fr. VAT identification is administered by the DGFiP local entity (Service des Impôts des Entreprises, SIE) or, for non-established businesses, by the DGFiP central unit for foreign enterprises (Service des impôts des entreprises étrangères); French intra-Community VAT numbers are validated through the EU VIES portal and the national taxe-tva.gouv.fr access.

E-government filing portal

A foreign-owned French SAS obtains its tax identity through the digital Guichet Unique operated by INPI, which acts as the single official entry point for INSEE, DGFiP, URSSAF, and the Greffe du Tribunal de Commerce. Step 1: execute the SAS articles of association in French law form and appoint a Président (permitted to be a non-resident individual or a foreign legal entity). Step 2: lodge the single incorporation declaration (electronic M0-form dossier) with the Guichet Unique; this automatically issues the statutory SIREN number (the French national tax identification number or TIN), the SIRET establishment number, the NAF/APE activity code (8299 — administrative and support services), and the legal entry in the Registre du Commerce et des Sociétés. Step 3: DGFiP allocates the business taxpayer file to the competent local SIE for corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés). Step 4: for VAT, DGFiP assigns the French intra-Community VAT identification number (format FR + 11 digits, built on the SIREN plus two check digits) upon completion of the tax annex within the registration file; a non-EU entity without a French fixed establishment must as a rule obtain a DGFiP-approved fiscal representative (représentant fiscal) unless the OSS/import scheme is used. Step 5: the company must activate its online professional space on impots.gouv.fr to enable electronic filing of the CA3 VAT return, the n° 2065 corporate income tax return, and the annual tax and social declarations.

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation (SAS is an opaque corporate taxpayer — 'société soumise à l'impôt sur les sociétés'; standard headline corporate income tax rate 25.00%, reduced rate 15.00% on the first EUR 42,500 of annual taxable profit for small enterprises with turnover ≤ EUR 10 million; a French registered office entails worldwide-profits taxation under art. 209 I CGI, subject to foreign tax credit relief; the SAS is not a pass-through vehicle by default and is not situated in a tax-exempt zone).

Available election forms

French tax law has no US-style 'check-the-box' election, but provides defined statutory elections: (i) temporary pass-through option under art. 239 quater C CGI for a newly created SME-qualifying SAS provided the shareholding is held by natural persons and turnover limits are met, allowing income-tax transparency for a maximum of five fiscal years; (ii) VAT single-entity grouping ('assujetti unique') election under art. 256 C CGI for closely linked French taxable persons; (iii) corporate fiscal unity ('intégration fiscale') election under art. 223 A CGI for a 95%-owned French subgroup, permitting a single consolidated corporate income tax return; (iv) hybrid financing arrangements are subject to mandatory matching adjustments and interest disallowances under art. 212 bis CGI with no elective opt-out, and DAC-6 disclosure obligations apply to reportable hybrid mismatches.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France grants full Euro convertibility on current and capital account operations, reflecting Articles 63-65 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union; there is no general exchange-control licensing, ceiling or prior-approval requirement for service-export revenue collections (including recurring card payments via Stripe), payments to non-resident subcontractors or licensors, dividend distribution, or profit repatriation by a French SAS to its non-resident shareholders. Profit remittance is subject solely to the standard French withholding tax framework for non-residents (Prélèvement Forfaitaire Unique of 30% on dividends for eligible corporate shareholders absent an applicable double-tax treaty) and to French company-law capital-conservation rules requiring positive distributable reserves before any distribution. The CMRA/mail-scanning scope (ISIC 8299) is a non-sensitive, non-regulated-from-an-FX-standpoint activity: inbound current-account flows and outbound service payments are free. Remaining duties are declarative, not authorisatory: cross-border transaction statistics must be reported to the Banque de France when an entity's annual cross-border transactions exceed EUR 15 million; below this threshold the entity's bank performs the reporting; and all flows remain subject to EU/French sanctions screening and the anti-money-laundering reporting duty to Tracfin for suspicious transactions.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There is no surrender ratio, no statutory conversion deadline and no mandatory retention period for export proceeds in France. A French SAS may keep USD, GBP or other foreign-currency receivables in a licensed foreign-currency account at a French credit institution, use them directly to settle foreign-currency liabilities, or convert them to EUR at market rates at any time. No certificate of export repatriation or proof of conversion is required by the foreign exchange authority; the same treatment applies to services exports such as digital mail scanning. The only obligation is statistical reporting of cross-border service flows to the Banque de France where the EUR 15 million annual threshold is exceeded; otherwise the bank aggregates and reports automatically.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (Bank of France) acting as the national central bank of the Fifth French Republic within the Eurosystem; foreign exchange regulation and cross-border capital compliance are administered by the Direction Générale du Trésor of the Ministry of Economy, Finance and Industrial and Digital Sovereignty under the Code Monétaire et Financier; prudential supervision of banks and payment institutions is performed by the ACPR (Autorité de Contrôle Prudentiel et de Résolution).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Revolut Fit: High

Digitally native EU EMIs are viable and recommended as a secondary liquidity channel: Qonto, Shine, and REVOLUT Business hold ACPR licences or valid EEA passports operated in France under PSD2, provide fully remote KYC, French IBANs and multi-currency wallets, and fit low-touch CMRA cash flows. Their limitations are structural: none can deliver the capital-blockage certificate for incorporation, a real credit relationship, commercial letter-of-credit issuance or an ISDA-based hedging platform. Offshore (non-EEA) digital EMIs are not viable for a French resident SAS: without ACPR authorisation or passporting they cannot legally execute payment services in France, and their use as collection or settlement rails would expose the entity and its directors to criminal sanctions under Articles L521-1, L522-1 and L571-3 of the Code Monétaire et Financier.

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% standard corporate income tax rate. A 3.3% social contribution may apply where the company's corporate income tax liability exceeds EUR 763,000 and turnover is at least EUR 7.63 million, producing a maximum combined effective rate of approximately 25.83%.

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

The standard French withholding tax on dividends paid by a French company to a non-resident corporate shareholder is generally 25%, subject to treaty relief and statutory exemptions. The EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive exemption may apply to a qualifying EU or EEA corporate parent, commonly requiring a minimum 10% holding maintained for at least two years, subject-to-tax and anti-abuse conditions, and appropriate documentation. Dividends paid to individuals are subject to different rules, including a generally 12.8% income-tax withholding component, without prejudice to social levies and final tax treatment.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For a financial year ending on 31 December, the corporate income tax return is generally due by the second working day following 1 May of the following year. For a financial year ending on another date, filing is generally due within three months after the financial year-end. The final corporate income tax balance is generally payable by the fifteenth day of the fourth month following year-end, subject to the specific payment calendar. Electronic filing and payment are mandatory for a SAS.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Instalments are generally due on 15 March, 15 June, 15 September, and 15 December. The final balance is generally due by the fifteenth day of the fourth month following financial year-end, with special timing for companies closing on 31 December.

Deadline: · Frequency:

Generally filed with or within the statutory period for the corporate income tax return. The exact due date follows the current French tax-calendar rules and may depend on the taxpayer's financial year-end.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Full French transfer-pricing documentation is generally mandatory for companies meeting the EUR 150 million turnover or gross-assets threshold, companies belonging to a group meeting the relevant threshold, or companies otherwise falling within statutory documentation rules. A simplified transfer-pricing documentation regime may apply below the full-documentation threshold. An annual transfer-pricing declaration is generally required for entities meeting the EUR 50 million turnover or gross-assets threshold or otherwise covered by the statutory group and transaction rules. The EUR 150 million and EUR 50 million thresholds must be tested against current French legislation and the company's group profile.

Economic substance scope: France has no single Cayman-style economic substance regulation applicable to every company, but substance is central to French anti-abuse rules, treaty entitlement, beneficial-ownership analysis, controlled foreign company rules, transfer pricing, interest deductibility, and the general abuse-of-law doctrine. A French SAS operating a virtual-office and digital-scanning business should maintain real French decision-making, appropriate personnel or outsourced operational capacity, secure premises or documented service infrastructure, equipment, customer contracts, operating procedures, accounting records, bank controls, and evidence of actual services. A registered address without genuine operations, personnel, decision-making, customer-service capability, or mail-handling controls is a material audit and recharacterization risk. Commercial domiciliation activities may also require compliance with French regulated business-domiciliation rules, contractual requirements, customer identification, premises, and recordkeeping obligations.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Corporate income tax evasion and fraudulent accounting

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment, late-payment interest, 40% or 80% tax penalties depending on the conduct, and criminal tax-fraud prosecution. Ordinary tax fraud may carry imprisonment of up to five years and a fine of up to EUR 500,000, with higher sanctions for aggravated circumstances, including up to seven years' imprisonment and a fine of up to EUR 3 million, subject to statutory conditions.

Director personal liability: Directors and de facto managers may face criminal prosecution, personal fines, imprisonment, disqualification from management, and civil tax liability where their personal conduct caused or organized the fraud. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may be ordered by a court in appropriate criminal proceedings. The corporate veil is not routinely pierced merely because a director is a shareholder.
Shell company, fictitious establishment, or abuse of corporate form

Statutory sanction: Tax reassessment based on actual activity and beneficial ownership, denial of treaty benefits, abuse-of-law penalties potentially reaching 80% of reassessed tax, closure or regulatory sanctions where applicable, and potential criminal liability for fraud or money laundering.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur civil liability for mismanagement, criminal liability for fraudulent organization or concealment, management bans, asset-seizure measures, and imprisonment where the conduct satisfies a criminal offense.
Unlicensed or non-compliant commercial domiciliation

Statutory sanction: Administrative and commercial sanctions may include injunctions, fines, suspension or withdrawal of authorization, contractual invalidity risks, and criminal exposure where false declarations, fraud, or unlawful business activity is involved. Exact sanctions depend on the breached provision and facts.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally prosecuted for knowingly operating an unlawful or fictitious business, may face management disqualification and civil damages, and may be liable for unpaid penalties where personal fault or fraudulent conduct is established.
Anti-money-laundering and customer-identification failure

Statutory sanction: Regulatory measures may include warnings, reprimands, suspension, withdrawal of authorization, administrative fines, and referral for criminal prosecution. Money laundering may carry up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 500,000 fine, with aggravated penalties potentially reaching ten years' imprisonment and EUR 750,000 or more depending on the offense and applicable law.

Director personal liability: Managers and compliance officers may face personal criminal liability for intentional participation, complicity, concealment, or deliberate failure to act. Courts may impose management bans, asset freezes, confiscation, and restrictions on movement where legally justified.
VAT fraud and payment-platform concealment

Statutory sanction: VAT assessments, late-payment interest, 10%, 40%, or 80% penalties depending on the violation, denial of input VAT, and criminal prosecution for organized or fraudulent conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors may become personally liable for tax debts in cases of serious and repeated management misconduct that makes collection impossible, and may face criminal fines, imprisonment, management bans, and asset-seizure measures for intentional fraud.
Transfer-pricing manipulation and profit shifting

Statutory sanction: French taxable income may be increased under the arm's-length principle, with corresponding withholding-tax, interest, documentation, and penalty consequences. Failure to provide documentation may result in significant statutory penalties and adverse reconstruction of taxable profit.

Director personal liability: Directors may face civil liability for mismanagement and criminal exposure if the pricing forms part of deliberate tax fraud, false accounting, money laundering, or asset diversion. Ordinary TP disagreement does not automatically create personal criminal liability.
Improper dividend, hidden distribution, or capital extraction

Statutory sanction: Repayment of unlawful distributions, corporate income tax adjustments, withholding tax, interest, penalties, possible denial of deductions, and potential criminal sanctions for misuse of corporate assets or fraudulent accounting.

Director personal liability: Directors may be required to restore unlawfully distributed amounts, compensate creditors or the company, and face criminal liability for abuse of corporate assets, breach of fiduciary duties, or deliberate concealment.
Withholding-tax non-compliance

Statutory sanction: Recovery of unpaid withholding tax from the French payer, late-payment interest, penalties, denial of treaty relief, and possible anti-abuse reassessment. The French SAS may remain liable even where the recipient agreed contractually to bear the tax.

Director personal liability: Personal liability may arise for deliberate concealment, fraudulent filings, or serious management misconduct. Directors may face criminal sanctions where withholding-tax evasion forms part of tax fraud or money laundering.
False registered address, mail interception, or privacy breach

Statutory sanction: Civil damages, contractual claims, regulatory sanctions under data-protection law, possible CNIL measures, and criminal liability for unlawful interception, disclosure, or misuse of correspondence. GDPR administrative fines may reach the applicable statutory maximum, including up to EUR 20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover for the most serious infringements.

Director personal liability: Directors and responsible officers may face personal criminal liability for intentional interception, disclosure, fraud, or obstruction, together with civil damages and professional disqualification where applicable.
Destruction or falsification of books and electronic records

Statutory sanction: Adverse tax assessment, rejection of accounts, penalties for non-cooperation, tax-fraud sanctions, obstruction consequences, and criminal prosecution for falsification or destruction of evidence.

Director personal liability: Directors, accounting officers, and persons ordering the destruction may face fines, imprisonment, management bans, civil restitution, and confiscation of relevant assets. Court-imposed travel restrictions may apply in criminal proceedings but are not automatic.
