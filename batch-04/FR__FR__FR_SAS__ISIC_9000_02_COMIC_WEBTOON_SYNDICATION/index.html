Tax Identity & Indirect Tax

Tax authority e-portal, entity tax classification elections, VAT thresholds, and 0% export documentation chain.

Tax ID Application Infrastructure

Competent tax authority

Direction Générale des Finances Publiques (DGFiP) – Service des Impôts des Entreprises (SIE); national portals: www.impots.gouv.fr (professional e-services Espace Professionnel), www.formalites.entreprises.gouv.fr (Guichet Unique des Formalités des Entreprises, single business-registration gateway), and www.impots.gouv.fr/oss (EU One Stop Shop for VOlT and distance sales). Non-resident operators are handled by the DGFiP Service des Impôts des Entreprises étrangères (SIE étranger) in Paris.

E-government filing portal

STEP 1: French-incorporated SAS obtains SIREN/SIRET automatically from INSEE via the Guichet Unique after corporate registration; STEP 2: DGFiP issues the intra-EU VAT identification number (FRTVAnnnnnnnnn based on the SIREN) and activates the corporate income tax (IS) and VAT accounts under the same SIREN fiscal file; STEP 3: foreign entities without a French establishment that cannot use reverse charge or OSS must file DGFiP Form 15771-SD with the SIE étranger, appointing a DGFiP-approved fiscal representative (représentant fiscal agréé) when established outside the EU and potentially posting a bank guarantee; STEP 4: a French business bank account and a gestion mandate or power of attorney are practical prerequisites; STEP 5: processing time is typically 5–10 days for French incorporations and 2 weeks to 2 months for non-resident direct VAT registration.

Tax ID type & application path: Entity_Level_Taxation / Territorial_Scope (Pass_Through_Eligible only via temporary statutory election). An SAS is by default a separate legal person subject to corporate income tax (Impôt sur les Sociétés, IS) under CGI Art. 206-1, with no fiscal transparency in the standard regime; IS is levied at 25% with a reduced 15% rate on the first EUR 42,500 of annual profit for qualifying SMEs (Turnover below EUR 10,000,000; 75%+ individual ownership); French territoriality (CGI Art. 209-I) confines IS to profits derived from activities carried on in France, save for any ministerial-approved worldwide or consolidated election. Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation / Territorial_Scope (Pass_Through_Eligible only via temporary statutory election). An SAS is by default a separate legal person subject to corporate income tax (Impôt sur les Sociétés, IS) under CGI Art. 206-1, with no fiscal transparency in the standard regime; IS is levied at 25% with a reduced 15% rate on the first EUR 42,500 of annual profit for qualifying SMEs (Turnover below EUR 10,000,000; 75%+ individual ownership); French territoriality (CGI Art. 209-I) confines IS to profits derived from activities carried on in France, save for any ministerial-approved worldwide or consolidated election. For VAT, the SAS is an entity-level taxable person and its French head-office registration covers all French establishments.

Available election forms

CGI Art. 239 bis AB: a qualifying small SAS (Turnover below EUR 10,000,000, majority held by individuals with substance, unlisted) may elect, for a maximum of five fiscal years, to be fiscally transparent and taxed at the shareholder level under Impôt sur le Revenu (IR); CGI Art. 223 A: a French parent holding at least 95% of a subsidiary may elect the tax-integrated group regime (intégration fiscale) for consolidated IS; CGI Art. 209: ministerial approval may be obtained for worldwide profits (bénéfice mondial) or consolidated (bénéfice consolidé) taxation. France has no US-style check-the-box election and no elective hybrid-mismatch treatment: ATAD anti-hybrid rules (CGI Arts. 7, 12 and 209-0 A et seq.) apply automatically. For indirect tax, an EU-established SAS may elect the OSS (One Stop Shop) regime to declare EU-wide B2C e-services from France and may drop out of the French micro-enterprise VAT franchise.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

France has no general VAT registration threshold for an incorporated SAS; the micro-enterprise exemption (franchise en base de TVA) applies only below EUR 36,800 of annual turnover for services and EUR 91,900 for goods (prestations de services and ventes thresholds, annually adjusted; upper ranges EUR 44,300 and EUR 101,000). A commercial webtoon publishing operation monetized via Apple IAP and Google Play Billing will normally exceed these limits, so the SAS must register and charge French VAT from the first euro on domestic B2C electronic supplies. For intra-EU cross-border B2C digital services (Chapter unlocking, subscriptions), the pan-EU annual threshold of EUR 10,000 (CGI Art. 293 A ter) triggers OSS registration and VAT accounting in each member state of consumption; non-established foreign digital suppliers must collect French VAT with no de minimis threshold on French consumers.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of physical goods outside the EU qualify for French VAT zero-rating (exonération) under CGI Art. 262-I, and intra-Community supplies of goods to EU VAT-registered customers qualify under CGI Art. 261 C-I, provided the exporter retains proof that the goods physically left the French or EU territory; services directly connected to export transport and handling are likewise zero-rated under CGI Art. 262-II. For the actual webtoon digital-publishing model, 'export zero-rating' is largely inapplicable: B2B licenses and cross-border syndication contracts are taxed in the customer's country under the general place-of-supply rule (CGI Arts. 258, 259, 259 A), with the French supplier issuing a reverse-charge invoice without French VAT and quoting the customer's valid EU VAT ID; B2C digital services to foreign consumers are statutorily taxable only in the consumer's country (CGI Art. 259 D), which produces de facto 0% French taxation. Claims of 0% on any physical deluxe/print export must be matched by shipping evidence and the remittance of export consideration.

  • Customs Export Declaration (DAE / DAU - EX-E) lodged via DELTA-X with the exporter-of-record status matching the SAS EORI number (FR...)
  • Bill of Lading / CMR / airway bill evidencing physical departure of the goods from EU territory
  • Inward Remittance Certificate or bank SWIFT confirmation evidencing settlement of export proceeds by the foreign customer
  • Commercial invoice addressed to the foreign importer with Incoterms and export terms
  • Packing list (applicable to physical deluxe reprints, collectibles or printed webtoon volumes)
  • Proof of intra-Community supply: customer's valid EU VAT ID and the recapitulative statement entry for EU zero-rated dispatches
  • International syndication or licensing agreement evidencing the offshore digital-service B2B transaction
  • For the app-store channel only: Apple/Google settlement statements and OSS country-by-country listings in substitution for customs documentation

France is deploying a transaction-based e-invoicing and e-reporting reform: from 1 September 2026 large and mid-size enterprises must issue structured e-invoices (FACTUR-X, UBL) for B2B supplies through a certified Partner Dematerialization Platform (PDP) or the public portal Chorus Pro (PPF); small and micro enterprises join on 1 September 2027 (loi de finances 2023 and décret n° 2024-219). B2C payment and transaction data (e-reporting) for both domestic and cross-border flows are transmitted to DGFiP by the PDP/PPF in periodic batch files; there is no real-time per-invoice fiscal clearance and no digital stamping akin to the Italian SDI or Turkish regimes, but certified PDPs expose APIs consistent with the national Chorus Pro/NITVAN e-invoicing specifications. B2G e-invoicing on Chorus Pro has been mandatory since 2017. For app-store digital distribution, Apple and Google may be deemed suppliers of the B2C supply under the EU e-commerce VAT package (Art. 9a of Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/1315), meaning the French SAS must keep OSS listings, reverse-charge evidence and platform settlement statements in lieu of classic invoice flow.

DGFiP's recovery right generally runs until the end of the third calendar year following the year the VAT became chargeable (CGI Livre des procédures fiscales, Art. L169); the window extends to ten years where the zero-rating claim is tainted by occult activity or fraudulent maneuvers (Art. L176 LPF). If audit evidence shows goods never left France, forged export proofs, artificial delay in repatriating exports proceeds without economic justification (abus de droit under Art. L64 LPF), or digital supplies mislabeled as exports, DGFiP retroactively reclassifies the receipts as French-standard-rated supplies at 20%, claws back the VAT, applies late-payment interest (intérêt de retard at 0.20% per month), and triggers penalties under CGI Art. 1729: 40% increase for bad faith (mauvaise foi) or 80% for fraudulent maneuvers (manœuvres frauduleuses), alongside 10% for ordinary filing negligence. Criminal exposure under CGI Art. 1741 reaches EUR 500,000 fine and 5 years' imprisonment, elevated to EUR 750,000 and 7 years for organized fraud; directors and de facto managers may be held solidarity liable, and the customer's input-credit claim is denied when the export/zero-rating evidence fails the legal documentation tests (CGI Annex II, Art. 271).

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France applies full current and capital account convertibility of the euro. No prior exchange-control license is required for current payments, capital movements, dividends, or profit repatriation under EU Treaty freedoms and the French Monetary and Financial Code. The only mandatory formality is statistical balance-of-payments (BOP) declaration to Banque de France when capital or direct investment flows exceed certain reporting thresholds; profit remittance requires duly approved annual accounts, a formal board/shareholder distribution resolution, and standard AML/KYC checks, but no FX conversion permit.

Export proceeds surrender rules

France imposes no statutory export proceeds surrender requirement and no mandatory foreign-currency conversion ratio or timeline. Export receipts may be retained in foreign currency, used directly for current payments, or converted to euros at the exporter's discretion; however, all correspondent transactions remain subject to EU/UN sanctions screening and French AML/CFT due diligence.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France; foreign exchange regulatory competence shared with the Direction Générale du Trésor and the Autorité de Contrôle Prudentiel et de Résolution (ACPR).

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Medium. An EU/EEA-licensed electronic money institution (EMI) can passport into France under PSD2 and may accept euro balances; however, an EMI is not a full credit institution, cannot offer commercial credit, and must safeguard client funds with a credit institution. Non-EU fintechs require ACPR registration or a French branch, and digital-content subscription flows can trigger enhanced AML requirements; therefore an EMI is viable as a collection and disbursement rail but should be supplemented by a tier-1 bank for capital escrow, clearing, and derivative execution.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Required routing is a SWIFT MT103 from the non-resident shareholder’s bank to the French SAS’s euro current/escrow account (FR76 IBAN), or a SEPA Credit Transfer for EU/EEA funds. For statistical purposes, remittance information must indicate the economic nature, e.g. “SHARE CAPITAL INCREASE / SUBSCRIPTION” (and, where applicable, a Banque de France BOP transaction code), and the bank will classify the payment internally. The SAS must produce the capital increase resolution, K-bis filing, UBO declaration, source of funds documentation, and confirm the absence of sanctions exposure. Stage 06

Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés).

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

Dividends paid by a French company to a non-resident corporate shareholder are generally subject to 25% French withholding tax, subject to domestic exemptions, EU Parent-Subsidiary Directive relief, treaty relief and anti-abuse conditions. Dividends paid to non-resident individuals are generally subject to 12.8% withholding tax, subject to applicable rules and treaty treatment.

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

The annual return is generally due by the second working day after 1 May of the following year. For a financial year ending on a date other than 31 December, the return is generally due within three months after the financial year-end, subject to electronic filing calendar rules. Corporate income tax installments are generally due on 15 February, 15 May, 15 August and 15 November, with a final balance generally due by the 15th day of the fourth month following year-end.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Mandatory comprehensive Local File and Master File obligations generally apply where the French entity's annual turnover or gross assets are at least EUR 150 million, where it controls or is controlled by an entity meeting the statutory threshold, or where it belongs to a qualifying group containing an entity meeting the threshold. A Country-by-Country Report generally applies to multinational groups with consolidated annual revenue of at least EUR 750 million. An annual simplified transfer-pricing declaration generally applies at the EUR 50 million turnover or gross-assets threshold, subject to statutory conditions.

Economic substance scope: France has no separate general Economic Substance Regulations regime equivalent to certain offshore ESR regimes. Substance remains required under French anti-abuse law, beneficial-ownership principles, treaty principal-purpose testing, EU anti-hybrid and anti-tax-avoidance rules, controlled-foreign-company rules, interest-limitation rules and the abuse-of-law doctrine. The SAS should maintain French decision-making, personnel or outsourced operational capacity, premises or operational infrastructure, accounting records, bank control, commercial risk assumption and evidence of services actually received. A conduit company with no genuine functions, assets or risks may lose treaty benefits and face recharacterization, denied deductions, withholding assessments and penalties.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax evasion and fraudulent concealment

Statutory sanction: Administrative reassessment, 40% penalty for deliberate failure or 80% penalty for fraudulent maneuvers, plus 0.20% monthly late-payment interest. Criminal tax fraud may result in up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 500,000 fine, increased in aggravated cases to up to seven years' imprisonment and EUR 3 million, subject to statutory conditions.

Director personal liability: Directors may face criminal prosecution and, under French Tax Procedure Code Article L267, personal joint liability for tax debts where fraudulent maneuvers or serious and repeated non-compliance made collection impossible. Civil restitution, asset seizure and professional disqualification may follow. Travel restrictions are not automatic but may be imposed or arise through judicial measures or tax-enforcement procedures.
VAT fraud and digital-platform misclassification

Statutory sanction: VAT reassessment, late-payment interest, 10% to 80% penalties depending on conduct, potential criminal tax-fraud exposure, and loss of VAT registration or platform compliance privileges where applicable.

Director personal liability: Directors may incur liability for intentional or grossly negligent VAT misconduct, including tax-debt recovery actions, criminal penalties and disqualification. Personal liability is fact-dependent and is not automatic merely because the company failed.
Transfer-pricing manipulation

Statutory sanction: Adjustment of taxable profit, denial of deductions, withholding-tax reassessment, 40% or 80% penalties where applicable, documentation penalties, and possible Article 57 French tax-adjustment proceedings. The absence of a Local File or Master File can trigger a specific documentation fine calculated under statutory rules, potentially reaching the greater of a fixed statutory minimum or 0.5% of the amount covered by undocumented transactions, subject to applicable caps and amendments.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally exposed where they approved fraudulent arrangements, concealed related-party transactions or caused serious and repeated tax non-compliance. Ordinary pricing disputes do not automatically create personal criminal liability.
Shell company and treaty-shopping arrangement

Statutory sanction: Denial of treaty or EU relief, withholding-tax assessment at domestic rates, interest, anti-abuse penalties, abuse-of-law recharacterization and possible criminal tax-fraud exposure where concealment or falsification is established.

Director personal liability: Directors may face criminal liability for knowingly implementing sham structures, together with asset seizure and personal tax-debt recovery in aggravated cases. Treaty relief is denied based on facts; corporate limited liability is not automatically disregarded.
Undeclared beneficial ownership and corporate transparency

Statutory sanction: Civil and criminal sanctions may include fines, injunctions, restrictions on registration or access to corporate services, and related anti-money-laundering measures. False declarations may constitute a separate criminal offense.

Director personal liability: Legal representatives and beneficial owners may face personal criminal exposure for intentional false declarations or obstruction. Directors may also face disqualification and asset-seizure measures where linked to money laundering.
Anti-money-laundering and illicit-finance activity

Statutory sanction: Money laundering may result in up to five years' imprisonment and a EUR 375,000 fine, with aggravated sanctions potentially reaching ten years' imprisonment and EUR 750,000 or more under applicable circumstances. Confiscation, account freezing and regulatory reporting measures may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors and officers may be prosecuted personally for intentional participation, facilitation or negligent governance where legally applicable. Courts may impose imprisonment, fines, confiscation, disqualification and judicial supervision; travel restrictions may be ordered in criminal proceedings.
Copyright, licensing and unauthorized content exploitation

Statutory sanction: Injunctions, withdrawal or blocking orders, damages, restitution of profits, seizure of infringing materials and criminal copyright penalties. Copyright infringement may generally attract up to three years' imprisonment and a EUR 300,000 fine, with increased sanctions for organized or aggravated conduct.

Director personal liability: Directors may face personal criminal exposure for knowing infringement or organized infringement and may be liable for civil restitution where they personally participated or abused the corporate form. Ordinary corporate infringement does not automatically pierce the SAS liability shield.
Improper dividend distribution and unlawful profit repatriation

Statutory sanction: Repayment of unlawful dividends, corporate-law sanctions, tax reassessment, withholding-tax liability, interest and penalties. Fraudulent insolvency conduct may trigger additional criminal and civil sanctions.

Director personal liability: Directors may be required to restore assets, compensate creditors, bear liability for management faults contributing to an insufficient asset position and face disqualification or criminal prosecution. Shareholders receiving dividends in bad faith may also face repayment claims.
