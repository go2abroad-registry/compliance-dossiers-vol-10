Competent tax authority

Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP), through the impots.gouv.fr professional portal and the relevant Service des impôts des entreprises (SIE). Business formation and initial registration data are filed through the Guichet unique operated by the Institut national de la propriété industrielle (INPI); INSEE issues the SIREN and SIRET identifiers, while DGFiP assigns or confirms the French VAT identification number.

E-government filing portal

Default Tax Classification & Elections

Default tax classification

Entity_Level_Taxation

Available election forms

A SARL is generally subject to French corporate income tax (impôt sur les sociétés, IS). A qualifying SARL may elect for income-tax transparency (impôt sur le revenu, IR), including a family SARL election where all qualifying shareholders are members of the same qualifying family group. A SARL incorporated for fewer than five years may elect for IR for a maximum five-year period if statutory conditions are satisfied, including size, ownership, activity, and capital requirements. France has no general US-style check-the-box election and no general elective hybrid-mismatch classification mechanism. Anti-hybrid-mismatch rules implement the EU ATAD 2 framework and can deny deductions, impose income inclusions, or restrict relief where relevant cross-border hybrid arrangements exist.

VAT / GST & 0% Export Zero-Rating

Standard VAT / GST rate

20%

Mandatory registration threshold (Nexus)

France has no universal VAT registration threshold for ordinary taxable businesses: VAT registration and liability generally arise from the first taxable transaction for which the business is responsible for French VAT. The franchise en base de TVA may exempt qualifying small businesses below applicable turnover limits, generally EUR 85,000 for goods, food, and accommodation activities and EUR 37,500 for services, with increased thresholds generally applicable at EUR 93,500 and EUR 41,250 respectively, subject to the rules and transitional provisions in force for the relevant tax year. Businesses exceeding the applicable thresholds must transition to an actual VAT regime. Intra-EU acquisitions, imports, distance sales, domestic reverse-charge transactions, and other special regimes may trigger registration independently of turnover.

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

Exports of goods from France to destinations outside the European Union are generally exempt from French VAT under the export exemption rules, functionally equivalent to zero-rating, where the goods leave the EU customs territory and the supplier retains legally sufficient proof of export and supplier involvement in the dispatch. Typical evidence includes the electronic export declaration, MRN, customs exit confirmation, transport documents, commercial invoice, packing list, and proof linking the goods to the transaction. Intra-EU supplies of goods may qualify for exemption where the customer is identified for VAT in another Member State, the supplier reports the transaction correctly, and transport from France to that Member State is substantiated. Services are not automatically zero-rated merely because the customer is foreign. B2B services are generally outside French VAT under the customer-location rule and require reverse charge by the customer; B2C services and special-category services follow separate place-of-supply rules. Export exemption or non-taxation must be supported by customer status, place of supply, transport, customs, and accounting evidence. Input VAT refunds may be claimed through periodic French VAT returns or, for eligible non-established businesses, applicable EU or non-EU refund procedures.

  • French or EU export customs declaration and Movement Reference Number (MRN)
  • Electronic customs exit confirmation or equivalent proof that the goods exited the European Union
  • Commercial invoice identifying the French supplier, customer, goods, values, Incoterms, and VAT treatment
  • Packing list and product description sufficient to reconcile the shipment with the invoice
  • Bill of lading, air waybill, CMR consignment note, courier record, or other transport evidence
  • Evidence of delivery or receipt outside the European Union where available
  • EORI number and customs representation records
  • Purchase order, sales contract, shipping instructions, and accounting reconciliation
  • Evidence of payment where relevant to establish the transaction and reconcile proceeds; an inward remittance certificate is not generally a mandatory French export-VAT document
  • For intra-EU supplies: customer VAT identification number, recapitulative statement evidence, and proof of transport to the other Member State

France currently does not operate a universal real-time VAT invoice clearance system for ordinary private-sector transactions, and there is no general requirement for a government digital stamp on every invoice. Electronic invoicing is mandatory for transactions with French public-sector bodies through Chorus Pro. The private-sector reform requires all businesses established in France to be able to receive electronic invoices from 1 September 2026. Issuing obligations are scheduled from 1 September 2026 for large and medium-sized businesses and from 1 September 2027 for small and micro-enterprises, subject to statutory implementation and transitional rules. In-scope domestic B2B invoices must generally be transmitted through an approved Partner Dematerialization Platform (PDP), with structured invoice data transmitted to the administration. B2C and cross-border transaction data is generally subject to electronic reporting rather than domestic B2B invoice transmission. Required data fields include supplier and customer identification, VAT numbers where applicable, transaction amounts, VAT rates, payment terms, and transaction category. Businesses must retain electronically issued and received invoices in an auditable form for the statutory retention period.

France has no general statutory foreign-exchange repatriation deadline applicable to export proceeds and no general 180-day repatriation requirement. Export VAT exemption may be reassessed where customs exit, transport, customer, invoice, accounting, or transaction-linkage evidence is missing, inconsistent, fabricated, or not retained. The reassessment can include the VAT that should have been collected, statutory late-payment interest generally calculated at 0.20% per month under Article 1727 of the French General Tax Code, and applicable procedural or payment penalties. Deliberate or materially abusive conduct may attract a 40% penalty for deliberate failure, an 80% penalty for fraudulent manoeuvres or abuse of rights, and potentially criminal tax-fraud sanctions, including imprisonment and substantial fines. Incorrect input-VAT refunds may be clawed back with interest and penalties. Customs violations may additionally trigger customs duties, import VAT, administrative penalties, seizure, or other sanctions. The taxpayer bears the burden of maintaining an auditable evidentiary chain linking the exported goods, customs exit, invoice, accounting records, and applicable VAT treatment.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

France is a eurozone member and the euro is fully convertible. Article 63 TFEU guarantees free movement of capital and payments within the EU and with third countries, subject to EU-wide safeguards. Both current-account transfers (trade, services, royalties, salaries) and capital-account operations (equity subscription, shareholder loans, dividend remittances, liquidation proceeds) are permitted without exchange-control authorization. Practical hurdles for profit remittance from a French SARL are: compliance with French company-law solvency and dividend-distribution rules, preparation of approved financial statements, anti-money-laundering source-of-funds controls by the paying bank, possible withholding-tax relief procedures when dividends are paid to non-resident shareholders, and periodic statistical balance-of-payments declarations to the Banque de France for cross-border direct-investment flows above reportable thresholds.

Export proceeds surrender rules

There are no EU or French statutory requirements to surrender export proceeds, no mandatory conversion ratio, and no deadline for converting foreign currency into euros. A French exporter may retain foreign-currency receivables in a foreign-currencybank account or freely sell them on the interbank market. The practical compliance burden consists of customs valuation and Intrastat/EXTRASTAT reporting for goods movements, balance-of-payments declaration duties for settlement with non-residents when triggered, anti-money-laundering transaction documentation, and the corporate requirement to record and tax realized foreign-exchange gains or losses. No export proceeds need to be repatriated to France within any specified window.

Central bank supervision: Banque de France (Central Bank and balance-of-payments statistical authority); Direction Générale du Trésor, Ministère de l'Économie et des Finances, acts as the competent authority for cross-border capital-movement regulations and international financial sanctions.

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Feasibility rating: Medium as a supplementary disbursement channel, Low as a primary treasury rail. Legal constraints: a non-French electronic-money institution (EMI) must be authorized or validly passported under the Second Electronic Money Directive and PSD2 to issue e-money or provide payment services in France. Offshore and non-EU EMIs generally cannot lawfully provide French IBANs or hold French escrow funds without a licensed branch, a partner-banking sponsor, or another compliant local structure. A SARL may use an EU/EEA-authorized EMI for sales-collection convenience and low-value outbound payments, but inbound equity capital, commercial-letter-of-credit issuance, high-value trade settlements, and any bank-confirmed hedging lines must be run through a Tier-1 French credit institution to mitigate clearing, segregation, and regulatory risk.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Inbound share capital must be settled by a high-quality wire transfer from the non-resident parent or subscriber to the French SARL's segregated capital-subscription bank account using the beneficiary IBAN and BIC. The remitting bank must transmit an MT103 with a clear reference in the remittance-information field identifying the transaction as an 'apport en capital' or paid-in capital contribution. Use the ISO 20022 External Purpose Code 'CAPC' for a capital increase, the bank-specific code for equity injection if required by the French balance-of-payments survey, or 'LOAN' for a shareholder loan. No exchange-control approval is required for an inbound EUR remittance, but the first transfer may be quarantined pending full AML/source-of-funds clearance. The SARL must file the applicable direct-investment declaration with the Banque de France (or through its bank) where the transaction threshold or reporting regime applies, and must retain the capital-subscription deed, updated shareholding register, board resolutions, identity documents, bank reference letters, and proof of the upstream corporate chain to satisfy both French bank compliance and anti-money-laundering verification.Outbound settlement should use the most cost-efficient and compliant rail based on beneficiary jurisdiction: SEPA Instant Credit Transfer or SEPA Credit Transfer with IBAN for European suppliers; SWIFT MT103 for non-SEPA cross-border wire transfers; and irrevocable confirmed commercial letters of credit under UCP600 for high-value imports such as chemical heating cores, insulated aluminium bodies, food-grade packaging, and assembly-equipment purchases. Documentary collections under URC522 may be used for trusted ongoing suppliers. Each payment file must contain the correct ISO purpose code for trade in goods, invoice and packing-list cross-references, Incoterms, customs tariff classification, country-of-origin evidence, and any required import/export documentation. Corporate Tax & Transfer Pricing

Corporate income tax (CIT), box-by-box return breakdown, dividend WHT, and director personal liability.

Statutory corporate income tax (CIT)

25% (standard rate; reduced 15% on first €42,500 of taxable profit for SMEs)

Dividend withholding tax (WHT)

25% (domestic law) for dividends paid to non-resident corporate shareholders; 30% for individuals (flat tax).

Mandatory Return Breakdown

Deadline: · Frequency:

For fiscal year ending December 31, the return must be filed by the second business day after May 1st of the following year. For other fiscal year-ends, the deadline is within 3 months of the fiscal year-end.

Box-by-Box Return Breakdown (non-resident focus)

BoxField Non-resident guidanceTax-evasion pitfall
Box 1 (Résultat fiscal) Taxable income (or loss) after adjustments for tax deductions and add-backs. Non-resident companies with a permanent establishment in France must report French-source income in this box; otherwise, file a limited return for withholding tax purposes. Late filing penalty: 10% of the tax due if filed within 30 days of the deadline; 40% if filed after 30 days; 80% if the return is fraudulent. Interest on late payment: 0.20% per month. Additional penalties for failure to file annexes: €150 per missing document.
Box 2 (Plus-values nettes à long terme) Net long-term capital gains subject to reduced rate (currently 15% for certain assets). Report gains from disposal of French real estate or shares in French real estate companies; may be subject to specific withholding. Late filing penalty: 10% of the tax due if filed within 30 days of the deadline; 40% if filed after 30 days; 80% if the return is fraudulent. Interest on late payment: 0.20% per month. Additional penalties for failure to file annexes: €150 per missing document.
Box 3 (Crédits d'impôt) Tax credits (e.g., research tax credit, family tax credit) to be offset against corporate income tax. Non-residents may claim credits only if they have a permanent establishment in France and the credit is attributable to that PE. Late filing penalty: 10% of the tax due if filed within 30 days of the deadline; 40% if filed after 30 days; 80% if the return is fraudulent. Interest on late payment: 0.20% per month. Additional penalties for failure to file annexes: €150 per missing document.
Box 4 (Réductions d'impôt) Tax reductions (e.g., for investments in overseas territories) that reduce the tax liability. Generally not available to non-residents unless the activity is carried out through a French PE. Late filing penalty: 10% of the tax due if filed within 30 days of the deadline; 40% if filed after 30 days; 80% if the return is fraudulent. Interest on late payment: 0.20% per month. Additional penalties for failure to file annexes: €150 per missing document.
Box 5 (Résultat fiscal) Final taxable income after all adjustments, used to compute the corporate income tax. For non-residents, this box reflects the taxable income attributable to the French PE; if no PE, the return is limited to withholding tax obligations. Late filing penalty: 10% of the tax due if filed within 30 days of the deadline; 40% if filed after 30 days; 80% if the return is fraudulent. Interest on late payment: 0.20% per month. Additional penalties for failure to file annexes: €150 per missing document.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: Full transfer pricing documentation (Master File and Local File) is required if the French entity or group has annual turnover or total assets exceeding €400 million, or if the group's consolidated turnover exceeds €400 million. For smaller entities, a simplified documentation (form 2257) is required if the entity has related-party transactions exceeding €50,000.

Economic substance scope: France applies the 'substance over form' doctrine and anti-abuse rules (e.g., Article 155-A of the French Tax Code) to prevent artificial arrangements. While there is no specific Economic Substance Regulation (ESR) as in offshore jurisdictions, French tax authorities scrutinize the actual business activity, decision-making, and operational substance of entities claiming tax benefits. A French SARL must demonstrate real economic presence (employees, premises, business functions) to avoid challenges under the general anti-abuse rule (GAAR) and to benefit from treaty relief.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion (Fraude fiscale)

Statutory sanction: Criminal penalties: up to €500,000 fine and 5 years imprisonment for individuals; corporate fine up to €1,000,000. Civil penalties: 40% to 80% of the evaded tax, plus interest at 0.20% per month.

Director personal liability: Directors may be held personally liable for unpaid corporate taxes if they have committed serious misconduct (e.g., fraud, misappropriation). They may face civil restitution, travel bans, and imprisonment (up to 5 years).
Shell Company (Société écran)

Statutory sanction: The tax administration may disregard the entity and reattribute income to the actual beneficial owner. Penalties: 80% of the tax due for abuse of law (abus de droit), plus interest. Criminal sanctions for money laundering if applicable.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for the tax debts of the shell company if they acted in bad faith. They may face criminal prosecution for tax fraud and money laundering, leading to imprisonment and asset seizure.
AML (Anti-Money Laundering) Non-Compliance

Statutory sanction: Administrative fines up to €5 million or 5% of turnover for legal entities; criminal penalties for directors up to 5 years imprisonment and €375,000 fine.

Director personal liability: Directors can be held personally liable for AML breaches, including civil damages and criminal sanctions. They may face travel bans and disqualification from managing companies.
Transfer Pricing Misreporting

Statutory sanction: Adjustment of taxable income, 40% penalty for lack of documentation, and interest. In cases of serious mispricing, a 80% penalty may apply.

Director personal liability: Directors may be personally liable for the tax adjustment if they knowingly approved the mispricing. They may face civil liability for damages to the company and criminal charges for tax evasion.
